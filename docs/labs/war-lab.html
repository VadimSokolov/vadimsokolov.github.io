Polson–Sokolov Lab · Wins above replacement

Wins Above Replacement

A player is worth what his team would lose if he were replaced by the best freely available substitute. Build a team's wins from a replacement-level floor, derive the ten-runs-per-win rule from the Pythagorean law, take apart Pete Crow-Armstrong's 2026 value table, and then estimate player values as regression coefficients in simulated soccer and hockey leagues, where shrinkage toward a prior does the work and an Ovechkin type sniper shows why goals alone are not enough.

1 · The residual idea

Team wins = a replacement-level team + what each player adds

A roster of freely available players (replacement level) wins about 29 to 30 percent of its games, roughly 48 of 162. Each player's WAR is what he adds over the replacement player who would have taken his playing time. So a team's wins are about 48 + the sum of its players' WAR. Move the sliders to build a team. Only the centre fielder's 10.6 is a real figure; the other groups are illustrative.

2 · Runs per win from the Pythagorean law

Where “about ten runs per win” comes from

The Pythagorean law gives a team's winning percentage p from runs scored per game R and runs allowed per game A: p = Rx/(Rx + Ax). For an average team, R = A = r, the runs per game per team in the league (the run environment). The slope of the curve there is x/(4r): one more run per game raises p by that much. Turned around, one extra win over a season costs ρ = 4r/x runs. That number ρ is runs per win.

    4 · Soccer · the residual as a regression coefficient

    Plus-minus, adjusted plus-minus and ridge in a simulated league

    Soccer has no run-by-run accounting, so each player's value is estimated directly. Split every match into segments between substitutions. The response yt is the goal difference per 90 minutes in segment t. Each player j gets an indicator xtj = +1 if he is on the pitch for the home side, −1 for the away side, 0 if he is off. The regression coefficient αj is what is left of goal difference after crediting teammates and opponents. This page runs the note's simulation in your browser: 20 teams of 18 players, 10 seasons, three segments per match (45, 25 and 20 minutes), true values αj ~ N(0, 0.10²) goals per 90, and one centre back pair (true values 0.30 and 0.10) who play together whenever fit.

    5 · Hockey · goals above replacement

    Hockey: where plus-minus began, and why goals are not enough

    Hockey general managers invented plus-minus: a skater gets +1 when his team scores while he is on the ice and −1 when it concedes. The raw number mixes the player with his linemates and opponents, so the residual version is again a regression. Macdonald fits adjusted plus-minus by least squares on shift data; Gramacy, Jensen and Taddy use a regularised logistic regression on which team scored each goal; Thomas, Ventura, Jensen and Ma model scoring for and against as competing hazards, which separates offense from defense. Public WAR systems such as Evolving Hockey's GAR add up goals above replacement across even strength, power play, shorthanded play, shooting and penalties, and convert at roughly 5 to 6 goals per win because scoring is low.

    Lines change every 45 seconds or so and coaches juggle them, so collinearity is milder than in soccer. The binding constraint is the number of events: a season has only a few thousand even strength goals. The test case is an Ovechkin type sniper. As the note reports, Alex Ovechkin has signed with Washington again for a 22nd season in 2026/27, and nearly all of his value comes from shooting and the power play, with a defensive residual typically below average.

    The note's script simulates 4 seasons of a 32 team league, 18 skaters per team (576 players) in three lines of six that mix 30% of the time, and 80 shifts of 45 seconds per game: 419,840 shifts. Every skater has an offensive and a defensive effect, both N(0, 0.08²) on the log rate scale. Player 0 is the sniper, with offense 0.25 and defense −0.10. The same shifts are fitted twice: on goals (3 per 60 minutes per side, 34,779 in total) and on shot events at ten times that rate (347,012), the information expected goals models use.

    The sniper under each fit

      One idea in four sports

      Model team output, credit each player with a residual, convert to wins

      Every version of WAR does the same three things. It models team output, gives each player what is left after crediting context, teammates and opponents, and converts runs or goals into wins and wins into dollars. Baseball can do the first two steps by accounting; soccer, hockey and American football need a regression, and there the replacement level becomes a prior.

      Team output as a residual sum
      Yteam = Yrepl + Σi (Yi − Yirepl)
      wins ≈ 48 + Σi WARi (48/162 = 29.6%)
      WAR, with RAA = runs above average (batting, baserunning, fielding), Pos = positional, Lg = league, Repl = replacement shift
      WARi = (RAAi + Posi + Lgi + Repli) / ρ
      Runs per win ρ from p = Rx/(Rx + Ax)
      ∂p/∂R = x/(4R) at R = A, so ρ = 4r/x
      Pythagenpat x = (2r)0.287: r = 4.4 → x = 1.87, ρ = 9.4
      Adjusted plus-minus, segment t, with xtj ∈ {+1, 0, −1}
      yt = β0 + Σj xtj αj + εt, Var(εt) = σ²/wt, wt = minutes/90
      Ridge (regularised adjusted plus-minus, RAPM), centred at m
      α̂ = argmin Σt wt(yt − β0 − xtᵀα)² + λ Σj (αj − m)²
      = posterior mean and mode under αj ~ N(m, σ²/λ)
      Goals to points (American Soccer Analysis slope)
      PARj = 0.662 × (GAj − GArepl)
      Hockey: Poisson events Nta for attacking side a in shift t
      log E Nta = b + h·1{a = home}
      + Σattackers j θjoff − Σdefenders k θkdef
      Goals above replacement, T hours of ice time at base rate μ₀ per hour
      GARj = μ₀T(eθjoff − eθreploff) + μ₀T(e−θrepldef − e−θjdef)
      WARj = GARj / 5.5

      Takeaway for students. WAR is a residual plus a convention. The residual says how much better than average a player was in his chances; the convention (replacement level, runs per win, dollars per win) turns it into a single number. When the residual has to be estimated by regression, the data identify only some combinations of players, and the prior decides the rest.

      Replacement level is a convention

      replacement team ≈ 48 wins in 162

      The first three lines of the value table are residuals against average: zero for an average player with the same chances. The replacement line moves every player down to the freely available floor, by the same amount per unit of playing time. Each WAR system picks that floor by calibration. The note suggests estimating it instead, as the mean of the population of freely available players, with the positional adjustment as a group-level effect.

      Ten runs per win is calculus

      ρ = 4r/x ≈ 9.4 (r = 4.4), 9.7 (r = 4.6)

      At R = A the Pythagorean curve has slope x/(4R). More scoring in the league means each run is worth less, so ρ rises with r. Crow-Armstrong's table implies 104.3/10.6 = 9.84 runs per win, consistent with the Pythagorean calculation.

      Point WAR hides uncertainty

      FanGraphs 10.6 vs Baseball Reference 9.8

      Baseball Reference, with different fielding inputs and replacement calibration, puts the same season almost a win lower. A Bayesian treatment would report a posterior interval for WAR, and so for dollar value, with most of its width from fielding.

      Collinear players: only the sum is identified

      xt,A = xt,B in every segment ⇒ data see αA + αB

      A centre back pair on the pitch together every minute gives two identical columns. Least squares cannot split them, so the split comes from the method: the minimum-norm solution and the ridge prior both split evenly. The note recommends reporting the identified combinations apart from the prior-allocated parts.

      The level is set by the prior too

      Σj xtj = 11 − 11 = 0 in every segment

      Every segment has 11 players at +1 and 11 at −1, so adding the same constant to every αj leaves the fit unchanged. The data say who is better than whom, never how good the league is. Move the prior centre m: every ridge estimate shifts by exactly m. Points above replacement do not move, because the replacement level shifts with them.

      Soccer and the NFL

      Kharrat, McHale and Peña's plus-minus ratings use the segment regression above. Pelechrinis and Winston fit a Skellam regression on about 20,000 matches from 11 European leagues and find the transfer market undervalues defenders relative to goalkeepers. Szymanski's wage regressions say payroll explains most of league position and the residual is the manager.

      nflWAR (Yurko, Ventura and Horowitz) values each play by expected points added and win probability added, then a multilevel model attributes it to players: each player's credit is a random effect, a shrunken residual.

      Hockey: more events beat a better prior

      goals 34,779 vs shot events 347,012

      Lines change every 45 seconds, so skaters appear in many combinations and collinearity is mild. The limit is information. On four seasons of goals the best ridge fit puts the sniper at 0.7 WAR, rank 386 of 576. On shot events, ten times the data, it recovers 3.8 WAR against a true 4.3. Public hockey WAR models are built on expected goals for this reason.

      Which prior?

      normal: λθ² Cauchy: log(1 + θ²/s²)

      Ridge treats all players alike and pulls outliers hardest, and a sniper is an outlier in the offensive distribution. In the hockey simulation the heavy-tailed Cauchy prior leaves him nearly unshrunk (4.7 WAR) at the same overall error as ridge (3.8). The note suggests a horseshoe prior, which would do this while pulling the many near-replacement players to the floor, matching how talent is distributed.

Illustrative: the roster groups in section 1 other than the centre fielder.

      Try this in class

      1. In section 1, ask the class how many wins the team loses if the centre fielder is replaced, then click "Replace the centre fielder". Ask why the answer is 10.6 and not "all the wins he was part of".
      2. In section 2, switch between the fixed exponent 2 and Pythagenpat at r = 4.4. Have students compute ρ = 4r/x by hand for each (8.8 and 9.4). Then drag r from 3.5 to 6. Why is a run worth less in a high-scoring league? (Hockey's 5 to 6 goals per win comes from the same logic at low scoring.) In section 3, click "Average hitter, runner and fielder". The positional, league and replacement lines alone give 25.5 runs, about 2.6 WAR. Ask what WAR an average everyday centre fielder should have, and why it is not zero.
      3. In section 4, compare plain plus-minus, least squares and ridge in the scorecard. Plain plus-minus credits a player with his teammates' quality. Least squares fixes that but is noisy. Read off the ridge λ with the lowest error and compare it with σ²/τ² = 2.6/0.01 = 260.
      4. Click "Split the pair 30% of the time": in 30% of segments one centre back plays without the other. Least squares now separates them, noisily, and ridge gives the better player the larger share. Set "Other segments with one of the pair alone" back to 0% and the split returns to exactly 50/50, decided by the method alone.
      5. In section 5, with ridge at λ = 100, compare the two scatters: on goals the sniper sits in the crowd, on shot events he stands out. Ask what rank 386 of 576 would mean for his contract. Then switch to the Cauchy prior and watch the shot-event estimate move back toward 0.25 while the RMSE stays at 0.030.
