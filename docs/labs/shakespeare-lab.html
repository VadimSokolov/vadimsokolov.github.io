Shakespeare Lab: unseen words and a kernel smoother

Two questions about the same body of text. How many words did Shakespeare know but never write, and did he write a poem found in 1985? Efron and Thisted answered both from one table: how many word types he used once, twice, three times. Then a character-level transformer, trained on the same text, writes fluent pseudo-Shakespeare. Its core operation, attention, is an old statistical tool, the kernel smoother, applied to learned features.

The counts plus the 846 types used more than 100 times give exactly the 31,534 types of the canon.

Words as species

Treat each word type as a species and the canon's 884,647 tokens as a sample. \(n_x\) is the number of types seen exactly \(x\) times. The question of unseen species, posed by Fisher for butterflies, becomes: how many types would a second canon add?

A fingerprint for authorship

A new poem of 429 tokens is a tiny further sample. How many of its words should be new to the canon, used once, used twice? The predictions come from the low end of the same table.

Attention is a smoother

A transformer replaces each token's vector with a weighted average of the others, with weights from an exponential kernel on learned features. That is the Nadaraya–Watson estimator, with a learned metric and stacked many times.

Part IHow many words did Shakespeare know?

The frequency spectrum

14,376 types appear once in the canon, 4,343 twice, 2,292 three times. On a log–log scale the spectrum is close to a straight line, a power law. A model for it is a model for how new words arrive. The Ewens sampling formula, the one-parameter Dirichlet process partition, predicts \(n_x \approx \theta / x\). The two-parameter Pitman–Yor version adds a discount \(\sigma\) and predicts \(n_{x+1} / n_x = (x - \sigma)/(x + 1)\). Fit by eye with the slider.

Words he knew and never used

Suppose type \(i\) arrives at Poisson rate \(\lambda_i\) per canon. It is missing from the canon with probability \(e^{-\lambda_i}\) and present in a new sample of \(tN\) tokens with probability \(1 - e^{-t\lambda_i}\). Expanding gives the Good–Toulmin estimator of the number of new types,\[ \Delta(t) = \sum_i e^{-\lambda_i}\bigl(1 - e^{-t\lambda_i}\bigr) \;\approx\; \sum_{x \ge 1} (-1)^{x+1}\, t^x\, n_x, \]with no assumption about the rates. Its standard error is about \(\bigl(\sum_x t^{2x} n_x\bigr)^{1/2}\). For \(t > 1\) the terms grow and the alternating series is useless. Two repairs: a parametric mixture of rates (Efron and Thisted's gamma), or a smoothed series that damps the high terms (Efron and Thisted's binomial tilt, sharpened by Orlitsky, Suresh and Wu, 2016).

Terms with \(x > 100\) are not in the table; at \(t \le 1\) they move \(\Delta(t)\) by at most a few tens. Efron and Thisted's own bound, optimizing over every mixing distribution consistent with the counts, gave the headline: at least 35,000 words he knew and never wrote.

The 1987 test: "Shall I die? Shall I fly"

In 1985 Gary Taylor found a nine-stanza poem in the Bodleian and attributed it to Shakespeare. Thisted and Efron (1987) asked whether its rare-word use looks like his. The poem has 429 tokens, so \(t = 429 / 884{,}647 \approx 4.85 \times 10^{-4}\). To first order in \(t\), the expected number of the poem's types used exactly \(x\) times in the canon is\[ m_x \approx t\,(x + 1)\, n_{x+1}, \]the Good–Turing relation, and \(x = 0\) gives the types new to the canon, \(t\,n_1\). Each type turns up in the poem with small probability, so each count is close to Poisson around \(m_x\). That gives intervals the 1987 comparison did not report.

Part IIAttention is kernel smoothing

Nadaraya–Watson in one line, attention in one line

Given pairs \((x_j, y_j)\) and a kernel \(K\), the Nadaraya–Watson estimate at \(x\) is a weighted average of the responses, \(\hat m(x) = \sum_j w_j(x)\, y_j\) with \(w_j(x) = K(x, x_j) / \sum_l K(x, x_l)\). Attention computes, for token \(i\),\[ \text{out}_i = \sum_j \frac{\exp(q_i^\top k_j / \sqrt d)}{\sum_l \exp(q_i^\top k_l / \sqrt d)}\, v_j, \qquad q_i = W_Q x_i,\ k_j = W_K x_j,\ v_j = W_V x_j. \]Query for evaluation point, keys for design points, values for responses, \(\sqrt d\) for the bandwidth. Move the query and the bandwidth and watch the weights.

From one smoother to a model of Shakespeare

A transformer learns the metric (\(W_Q\), \(W_K\)), learns what is averaged (\(W_V\)), runs several smoothers at once (heads), and stacks them with a small feed-forward network and a residual connection between passes:\[ F = \mathrm{softmax} \circ W_U \circ \mathrm{LN} \circ B_L \circ \cdots \circ B_1 \circ (E + P), \qquad B_l = (I + \mathrm{FF}_l)(I + \mathrm{Attn}_l). \]Four numbers fix it: width \(d\), blocks \(L\), heads \(h\), context \(T\). Each block carries about \(4d^2\) parameters in attention and \(8d^2\) in the feed-forward. Width is expensive and depth is cheap.

Form without reference, and what that does to the 1987 test

A six-block character model learns scene headings, speaker names, line breaks, turn taking and a metre that scans, all from predicting one character at a time. It does not learn who anyone is. Polson's note shows the failure mode with lines written by hand in such a model's manner (an illustration, not a sample from a trained run):

ACT III. SCENE II. Another part of the field.

GLOUCESTER
Now is the hour that hath no master in it,
And all my fathers in the open air
Did bid me take the country for a wife.
What say the lords? They say the morning comes.

MESSENGER
My lord, the sea hath answered for the king,
And I have seen the second of his name
Lie down upon the honour of the hour.

Every line is well formed and refers to nothing. If such text is cheap, statistics of surface regularity are weaker evidence of authorship than in 1987. The rare-word test holds up better than most: to match the far tail of the spectrum a generator needs Shakespeare's vocabulary, not only his cadence. Whether a model trained on the canon passes it is an open question in the note: generate poems, run the test, and measure its power against a modern imitator.

What carries over

  1. Counts of counts are the data

    For species, words or anything else sampled with repetition, the spectrum \(n_x\) is what you keep. Unseen mass, new types and authorship predictions are all read off its low end.

  2. Extrapolation needs a model or a damper

    Good–Toulmin is model-free and exact in expectation up to \(t = 1\), and explodes after. Beyond it you choose: a mixing distribution for the rates, or a smoothed series with a stated range of validity (about \(t \approx \log N\)).

  3. A test needs an interval and an alternative

    Expected counts with no spread cannot reject anything. Poisson intervals give the spread; a Bayes factor against a named author, with his own spectrum, gives the alternative.

  4. Attention is a smoother you can read

    Each row of attention weights is a set of Nadaraya–Watson weights, or a posterior over which token is relevant. The output is a posterior mean of the values. What the transformer adds is learning the metric and stacking.

Why the exponential kernel is Gaussian

\(\exp(-\tfrac12\lVert q - k\rVert^2) = \exp(-\tfrac12\lVert q\rVert^2)\,\exp(q^\top k)\,\exp(-\tfrac12\lVert k\rVert^2)\). The first factor cancels in the normalization. If the keys have equal length, which layer normalization encourages, so does the last, and softmax attention is a Gaussian kernel smoother. Tick both boxes in the "cat sat" panel with equal-length keys to see the weights coincide.

Ewens, Pitman–Yor and the gamma fit

Ewens' \(n_x \approx \theta/x\) is the Dirichlet process; it puts too many words at \(x = 2\) and beyond. The gamma mixture of Poisson rates fitted to the 100 counts has shape \(\alpha = -0.40\), and a Pitman–Yor discount of about 0.40 fits by eye: the same power-law tail, seen two ways. With a negative shape the fitted vocabulary is unbounded, which is why the 1976 headline is a lower bound.

What the 1987 test does not do

It is a fingerprint, not an attribution. A forger, an imitator or a contemporary with similar reading would pass it. Thisted and Efron found that poems by Jonson, Marlowe and Donne fitted worse and held-out Shakespeare poems about as well; a test that fails to reject does not confirm, and most editors still doubt the attribution on literary grounds.

Try this in class

  1. In the spectrum panel, set σ = 0 (Ewens). How badly does it miss \(n_2\)? Find the σ that matches \(n_2/n_1\) and check what it predicts for \(n_{10}\).
  2. Move t from 0.5 to 2. Where does the Good–Toulmin band stop being informative? Compare the gamma fit and the smoothed estimator at t = 5: why do they disagree, and which assumption is each making?
  3. For the Taylor poem, compute by hand \(m_0 = t\,n_1\) and check it against the table. Which observed count is furthest out in its predictive distribution? Would you reject at the 10% level?
  4. Lengthen the poem to 3,000 tokens. How do the expected counts and the width of the intervals scale? How long would a poem need to be before a 30% excess of rare words became detectable?
  5. Drag the bandwidth of the kernel smoother to its smallest and largest values. Which limit is "attend only to yourself" and which is "average everything"? What plays the role of h in attention?
  6. In "the cat sat", raise τ to 5 and lower it to 0.1. Describe the weight matrix in each limit. Then turn on the causal mask: why does "the" now return exactly its own value?
  7. Set the width to 4,096 with 32 blocks. What fraction of the parameters sits in the feed-forward layers? Double the context: what changes in the parameter count, and what in the compute?
