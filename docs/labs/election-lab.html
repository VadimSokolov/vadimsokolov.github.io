Election Forecast Lab

Fit Ray Fair's vote equation to 27 US presidential elections, 1916 to 2020, and see why the economy only predicts the vote once its sign flips with the party in power. What goes into the vote equation

The response is VP, the Democratic share of the two-party vote. I says which party holds the White House. Multiplying an economic variable by I makes growth count for the Democrats when they hold office and against them when the Republicans do, so one coefficient means “growth helps whoever is in power”.

Models that keep I (M1, M2, M3, M5) add or subtract a fixed amount for the party in power. M5 drops DUR, so its I coefficient (−3.6) does DUR's job and hands an open-seat Democratic challenger about 3.6 points. Specification choice moves the 2028 answer as much as a point of inflation does.

Model against Polymarket

The forecasting cycle

Specify, select, validate, forecast with an interval, then check

The naive regression of VP on G, P and Z explains 4% of the variation. The same three variables, multiplied by I, explain 68%. Adding DPER and DUR gets to 84%. The 2024 forecast of 46.1 missed the realised 49.25 by 3.1 points, 0.8 predictive standard deviations, and called the winner.

  1. Specify with the sign flip

    Voters reward or punish the party in power. Multiply G, P and Z by I so one coefficient covers both parties. Without it, Johnson's 1964 boom and Reagan's 1984 boom cancel.

  2. Select with out-of-sample error

    R² always rises with more regressors. Adjusted R², AIC, BIC and leave-one-out RMSE all charge for them. All four pick M4: DPER, DUR, G·I, P·I, Z·I. I and WAR add nothing once DPER and DUR are in (joint F = 0.87, p = 0.43).

  3. Forecast with an interval

    The prediction standard error adds the uncertainty in the line to the scatter around it. For 2024: √(2.41² + 2.96²) = 3.82, so the 95% interval is 46.14 ± 2.08 × 3.82 = [38.2, 54.1].

  4. Turn the interval into a probability

    P(VP > 50) = 1 − Ft,21((50 − 46.14) / 3.82) = 0.16. The realised 49.25 sat inside the interval, 0.8 standard deviations above the forecast.

Takeaway for students. A regression forecast is a number, an interval and a probability. With 27 observations the interval is wide (about ±8 points), so a model can be right about the economy and still miss an election by four points. Judge it by calibration over many elections, not by one call.

Twenty-seven elections

Six coefficients from 27 data points leaves 21 degrees of freedom. Each election is one observation, so the 1932 Depression or the 1992 Perot race can move a coefficient noticeably. Leave-one-out and real-time errors (3.4 and 3.7) are larger than the in-sample 2.6, which is the price of fitting and testing on the same small sample.

Popular vote, not the Electoral College

VP is the national two-party popular vote. The presidency is decided state by state. In 2016 the Democrats won the popular vote (51.2%) and lost the election. A model probability of VP > 50 is not the probability of winning the White House, and in recent elections the Electoral College has leaned Republican relative to the popular vote.

Model uncertainty

The intervals assume the chosen model is right. Picking M4 after looking at six candidates on the same data makes it look a little better than it is. Across M1 to M5 the 2024 win probability ranges from 0.07 to 0.34, and for the 2028 baseline from 0.21 to 0.54.

2028 inputs are mostly unknown

As of October 2026, 6 of the 15 quarters for P and Z are in (Z = 2 so far, P about 3.6) and G, growth in the first three quarters of 2028, has not happened. The scenario tool asks “what if”. The simulation spreads the forecast over guesses about the economy, and those guesses are assumptions, not data.

Extrapolation in 2024

The case-study inflation value P = 8.3 lies beyond every past election (maximum 7.8 in 1980). Its leverage h₀ = 0.665 exceeds every in-sample leverage (maximum 0.467), which widens the interval. Fair's actual input, P = 4.54, sits inside the data, and the forecast moves to 49.23 against the realised 49.25.

About the data

The economic variables follow Fair's definitions and are revised over time, so published values differ slightly between vintages. The data file's President column labels 1944 with Truman and 1972 with Ford; Roosevelt and Nixon won those elections (corrected in the tooltips here). The project description also lists a House-vote variable VC that the file does not contain. Neither affects the fit.

Try this in class

  1. Before showing Step 1, ask: does strong growth help the Democrats? Show Growth against Democratic share (correlation 0.20), then flip to incumbent share (0.73). Ask students to find 1964 and 1984 and explain why they cancel.
  2. In the ladder, click M2 (Fair's full equation) and M4. R² falls from 0.853 to 0.839 but every other column improves. Ask which column you would trust for forecasting and why.
  3. Build your own model: start from M4 and tick I (this is M3), then WAR (this is M2). LOO RMSE rises from 3.41 to 3.66 to 4.21 while R² barely moves. Then click M5, which swaps DUR for I, and see AIC rise by 5.7.
  4. In Step 4, switch to Fair's actual inputs. Ask: was the case-study forecast wrong, or were its inputs wrong? Compute the error in predictive standard deviations for both (0.81 and about 0.01).
  5. In Step 5, find the inflation level at which the baseline (G = 2, Z = 3) becomes a toss-up. Then compare the model's chance of a Democratic popular-vote win (about 0.20) with the market's 64% for a Democratic president, and list three reasons the two could differ.
