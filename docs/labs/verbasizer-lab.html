Verbasizer Lab: random lines, chosen with feeling

In 1995, recording Outside with Brian Eno, David Bowie used a Mac program written with Ty Roberts, the Verbasizer. It cut sentences from newspapers, diaries and an invented story into fragments and reshuffled them into new lines. Bowie crossed out most of them and kept a few that carried feeling. The program had no model of language at all. It split writing into a cheap random generator and an expensive human selector, and the feeling lived in the selector. A language model fuses the two and pulls toward the most probable line. This lab rebuilds the split: a random shuffler, a critic that only scores, and you as the selector, with a Bayesian update that learns what you keep.

Based on Polson and Sokolov, Verbasizer 3.0: Random Generation, Emotional Selection (implementation note and slides, November 2026), and its prototype verbasizer.py. The critic in this page is a stub that runs in your browser: a hand-made emotion lexicon, a bigram language model of the source texts for surprisal, and a score for how well fragments join. The source texts were written for this lab.

The cut-up as a sampler

Sources \(s = 1, \dots, S\), each cut into fragments \(\mathcal F_s\). A line of \(k\) fragments:\[ s_j \sim \mathrm{Cat}(w), \quad f_j \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal F_{s_j}), \quad L = (f_1, \dots, f_k). \]The weights \(w\) were Bowie's sliders: how much newspaper, how much diary.

Taste as a rejection rule

The artist keeps a line with probability \(\pi(\text{keep} \mid L)\), a rule that lives in the artist's head. The final lyric is a draw from the generator reweighted by \(\pi\): a rejection sampler whose acceptance function is taste.

Verbasizer 3.0

Keep the randomizer random. Add a critic that scores each line for emotion, coherence and surprise, and never rewrites. Rank inside a coherence band, strange but singable. Learn the source weights and the target emotion from what the artist keeps.

Step 1. Cut

Three sources, as on the album: news, a diary, and an invented story. The cutter walks through each text and snips it into windows of one to \(K\) words, the scissors of Burroughs and Gysin. Edit any source and cut again; every fragment keeps its source tag.

Fragments, by source

Short fragments give strange lines and long ones give prose with seams. The note suggests a second cutter that ends fragments on clause boundaries found by a parser.

Step 2. Shuffle, and Step 3. Criticize

Each round draws source weights, then \(n\) candidate lines of \(k\) fragments with no fragment repeated. The critic scores every line: an emotion vector \(e(L)\) on four axes (valence, arousal, dread, longing), a coherence \(c(L) \in [0, 1]\), and a surprisal \(u(L)\) in bits per word. The ranking is\[ \mathrm{score}(L) = \lambda \cos\bigl(e(L), e^\ast\bigr) + \gamma\, z\bigl(u(L)\bigr), \qquad c_{\min} \le c(L) \le c_{\max}, \]with \(z\) the surprisal standardized within the batch. Above \(c_{\max}\) a line is ordinary prose; below \(c_{\min}\) it is noise.

    Step 4. Select: you are the Verbasizer's other half

    The top \(m = 8\) lines of the round. Mark the ones you would keep and submit. Each source gets a Beta prior on the chance that a line using it is kept; a kept line adds one to \(a_s\) for each source in it, a discarded line one to \(b_s\). Next round the weights are a Thompson draw, \(\theta_s \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a_s, b_s)\), \(w_s \propto \theta_s\): sources you like get used more, and every source keeps some chance. The target emotion moves toward what you keep, \(e^\ast \leftarrow (e^\ast + \eta\, e(L)) / (1 + \eta)\), \(\eta = 0.2\).

      Implementation notes from the paper

      Surprisal needs token log probabilities: \(u(L) = \frac{1}{|L|}\sum_t \log 1/p(t \mid \text{prefix})\) from a local open model.

      Cache scores by fragment tuple. Fragments recur; lines do not.

      Log everything: every candidate, its scores, what was shown, what was kept. The keep log is the data for learning \(\pi\).

      Richer selector: \(\Pr(\text{keep} \mid L) = \sigma(\beta^\top \phi(L))\) on features \(\phi = (e, c, u, \text{source counts})\), with a horseshoe prior on \(\beta\), ranking by posterior predictive keep probability.

      Generation and selection

      1. Separate the proposal from the judgement

        A cheap generator that is allowed to be wrong, and a selector that decides. Rejection sampling, Metropolis–Hastings and Bowie at a PowerBook share the shape.

      2. The most probable line is the least interesting

        Ranking by probability alone drives a language model to the mode: grammatical, familiar, flat. The coherence band and the surprise term are there to keep the selection off the mode.

      3. Learn the selector, not the text

        Each keep or discard is one Bernoulli observation. Beta updates per source and Thompson draws turn them into a policy that exploits what is liked and keeps exploring.

      4. Test against the obvious alternatives

        Three arms: raw cut-ups, the Verbasizer with an artist selecting, and pure LLM lyrics written to the same brief. The hypothesis is that the second beats the first on feeling and the third on surprise.

      What the stub critic does

      Emotion: the average of hand-made four-axis vectors for the words of the line found in a small lexicon (words outside it are ignored; a line with none gets 0.25 on every axis, as in the prototype). Surprisal: a bigram model of the three sources plus a page of plain English, interpolated with add-one unigrams. Coherence: at each seam between fragments, how much more likely the next word is after the last word than on its own, mapped into [0, 1] and averaged. Seams that happen to read as English score high.

      Optimize toward e*, or wander?

      The note leaves this open. Moving \(e^\ast\) toward kept lines narrows what is shown; the artist meets fewer lines he would not have asked for. A variant draws the target itself from a distribution around \(e^\ast\) each round, Thompson sampling on emotions as well as on sources.

      Is the keep rule learnable?

      Hold out ten rounds of keep decisions and ask whether the learned selector predicts them. If it does, part of the artist's taste is a function of features you can measure. If it does not, the note asks the better question: whether the point of the selector is that it cannot be learned. And Bowie added emotion in performance as well, which no critic of text can see.

      Try this in class

      1. Set K to 1, then to 6, and shuffle a few rounds each. Where do the candidates move in the coherence–surprisal plot? Which setting gives lines you would sing?
      2. Close the coherence band to 0.8 to 1.0, then to 0 to 0.2. Read the top lines each time. Then compare with the red points, the lines a probability-maximizer would pick.
      3. Set γ to 0 and then to 1. What happens to the shown lines when surprise counts as much as fit to the target emotion?
      4. Play the selector for five rounds, keeping only lines about weather and the sea. Which source did the Beta posteriors learn to favour, and did the target emotion follow you?
      5. Run the simulated artist with "it is mostly diary", then with "longing > 0.6". In which case do the source weights learn the rule and in which does the target emotion carry it? Why can't source weights alone learn a rule about content?
      6. Rewrite the diary source in a cheerful voice and rerun "longing > 0.6". What happens to the diary's keep rate, and what does that say about learning taste from source tags?
