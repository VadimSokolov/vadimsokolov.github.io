The curse of many tests

Test enough hypotheses and something will look significant

At α = 0.05, each null test has a 1-in-20 chance of a false alarm. That is fine for one test. With 100 tests you expect about 5 false alarms and are 99.4% sure to get at least one. Corrections trade some power for protection against that.

  1. Raw α controls each test, not the study

    Every null test has probability α of a false positive. Across k independent null tests, the chance of at least one is 1 − (1 − α)k. For Google's 41 shades that is 88%.

  2. Bonferroni: use α/k

    Testing each hypothesis at α/k keeps the family-wise error rate at or below α. It is simple and conservative: real effects need much smaller p-values to survive.

  3. Holm–Bonferroni: same guarantee, more power

    Sort the p-values. Compare the smallest with α/k, the next with α/(k − 1), and so on, stopping at the first failure. It never flags fewer than Bonferroni.

  4. Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false discovery rate

    Sort the p-values and find the largest i with p(i) ≤ i·α/k. Flag every hypothesis up to that one. On average at most a share α of the flagged results are false. This is the standard in genomics and medical imaging.

  5. The best defence is pre-registration

    Declare the primary metric and the hypotheses before seeing the data. The QSR team pre-registered, used Bonferroni-type corrections, and validated the top findings on held-out stores.

Takeaway for students. Statistics is what lets you tell the executive "these four things are real; the other 96 are not". A "significant" result pulled from many tests is weak evidence on its own. Ask how many tests were run, which correction was used, and whether the finding held up on new data.

Other pitfalls of A/B testing

Peeking at results and stopping when they look good is multiple testing in time: each look is another chance for a false positive. Pre-commit the sample size or use sequential methods.

5 interim looks at α = 0.05: about 14% false positives

Selection, interference, units

Self-selection, one user's treatment affecting another (SUTVA), and randomising the wrong unit all bias the estimate itself. No multiple-testing correction fixes them.

What the simulation assumes

Tests are independent. Null p-values are uniform on (0, 1). Real effects shift the z statistic by the amount that gives the chosen power at raw α, two-sided. The QSR split of 4 real effects is illustrative: the case reports 4 shipped insights, not how many were real.

z ~ N(λ, 1), p = 2·(1 − Φ(|z|))

Try this in class

  1. Load 41 shades of blue. Press "New draw" a few times. Most draws have at least one red tile at raw α: a "winning" shade that is pure noise. Bonferroni and BH are almost always all grey.
  2. Load the Economist illustration. Count the red tiles at raw α: about 45, next to about 80 green ones. So 36% of the "confirmed" results are wrong. Compare the BH column.
  3. Load QSR. Ask the room how many of the 4 real effects survive Bonferroni at 80% power. Then raise the power to 0.99 and watch more of them survive.
  4. Set m = 0 and slide k from 1 to 100. Follow the amber dot along the FWER curve, and check the simulated FWER in the table against 1 − 0.95k.
  5. Switch the p-value plot to "Histogram". Null p-values are flat. Real effects pile up near 0. The height of the spike above the flat floor is the signal BH looks for.
