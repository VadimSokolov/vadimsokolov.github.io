Catastrophe modelling

Roll back the tree, then let the test update your beliefs

Money is in thousands of dollars, as in the worked example: the wall is 40, a slide costs 1,000, and the test is 3. The boxes on the right recompute as you move the sliders.

  1. Read the tree left to right

    Squares are decisions you make. Circles are chance events, and the probabilities on their branches sum to 1. Each path ends in a total cost. The top two plans skip the test. The bottom plan pays the geologist and then decides after seeing the result.

  2. Solve it right to left

    Start at the leaves. At each circle, replace the branches with their expected cost. At each square, keep the cheaper branch and cut the others. This is backward induction, the same idea as dynamic programming. Without the test, building costs $40.5K in expectation and not building costs $10K, so on expected cost alone you do not build.

  3. The test result changes the probabilities

    The geologist catches 90% of real slides and clears 85% of safe seasons. A positive result raises P(slide) from 1% to 5.7%. A negative result drops it to 0.12%. These posteriors replace the prior on the branches after the test, so the best action can differ by result: build after a positive, skip the wall after a negative.

  4. Information has a price ceiling

    Knowing the test result before you choose lowers the expected cost from $10K to $7.75K. That $2.25K gap is the most the test is worth. The geologist charges $3K, so on expected cost alone the test is not worth buying. Even a perfect forecast is worth only $9.1K.

  5. Expected cost hides the catastrophe

    "Don't build" is cheapest on average but leaves a 1-in-100 chance of losing the house. Testing first costs only $750 more in expectation and cuts that chance by about 7 times. Which plan is right depends on how much you fear the tail.

A note on rounding

Hand calculations that round intermediate values give $10,760 and 0.00146 for the test plan. Exact arithmetic gives $10,750 and 0.00145 (path 2 is exactly 0.00100), which is what this lab shows. The conclusions do not change.

Takeaway for students. A decision tree separates what you control from what you do not, and Bayes' rule tells you how a piece of evidence should move the chance branches. Expected value picks one plan. Adding a constraint on the tail, or a utility that punishes ruin, can pick another. Always report both the expected cost and the chance of disaster.

Try this in class

  1. Before showing the tree, ask students to vote: build, don't build, or test first. Then reveal the expected costs and the table.
  2. Drag the test fee down. Find the fee at which the tree switches to "Hire geologist". It should be just under the value of the test, $2,250.
  3. Raise P(slide) slowly from 0.01. Find where building the wall becomes cheaper than not building (about 0.042 with the default costs), and watch the test's value rise and then fall as the decision becomes obvious.
  4. Set specificity to 0.50. The test now raises many false alarms. Ask what happens to P(slide | positive) and to the value of the test.
  5. Choose a 1-in-500 risk limit. "Don't build" is ruled out and the tree switches to testing first. Tighten to 1 in 2,000 and only the wall remains.
