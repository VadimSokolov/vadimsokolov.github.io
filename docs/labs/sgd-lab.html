SGD Lab: noisy steps downhill

Every network in these labs is trained by stochastic gradient descent: estimate the gradient of the loss from a small random batch, take a step against it, repeat. The estimate is noisy, and the noise is not a nuisance to be averaged away. It decides how close SGD gets to the optimum, how the step size should shrink, and, with a little added noise, turns the optimizer into a sampler from the Bayes posterior. Everything here runs on a two-parameter regression, so the whole loss surface fits on the screen.

← All labs

The data: \(n = 200\) points \(y_i = 1 + 2x_i + \varepsilon_i\), \(\varepsilon_i \sim N(0, 1)\), and the squared-error loss of the line \(\theta_1 + \theta_2 x\). All computation is in your browser. References: Robbins and Monro (1951); Polyak and Juditsky (1992); Kingma and Ba (2015) for Adam; Welling and Teh (2011) for stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics; Mandt, Hoffman and Blei (2017) for SGD as approximate Bayesian inference.

An unbiased, noisy gradient

The loss is an average over data, \(L(\theta) = \frac1n \sum_i \ell_i(\theta)\). A random batch \(B\) gives \(g = \frac1{|B|}\sum_{i \in B} \nabla \ell_i(\theta)\), with \(\mathbb E\, g = \nabla L(\theta)\) and a variance that shrinks like \(1/|B|\). One pass over the data buys \(n/|B|\) steps instead of one.

Robbins and Monro, 1951

To find the root of a function you can only observe with noise, step with sizes \(\eta_t\) that satisfy \(\sum_t \eta_t = \infty\) (you can reach anywhere) and \(\sum_t \eta_t^2 < \infty\) (the noise adds up to something finite). \(\eta_t = \eta_0/(1 + t/\tau)\) qualifies. A constant step does not.

Noise as a feature

With a constant step SGD never settles: it wanders in a ball around the optimum whose size is set by \(\eta\) and the batch size. Add Gaussian noise of the right size and the wandering becomes a draw from the posterior. That is stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics.

Part IIThe noise ball

Constant steps hover, shrinking steps converge

Run 300 independent SGD chains from the same start and plot where they are after \(t\) steps. With a constant step the cloud stops shrinking: near the optimum the loss is a quadratic with Hessian \(H\), and the stationary spread \(\Sigma\) of SGD solves \(H\Sigma + \Sigma H = \eta\, C / |B|\), with \(C\) the covariance of one example's gradient. In this regression \(C \approx \sigma^2 H\), so \(\Sigma \approx \frac{\eta \sigma^2}{2|B|} I\): a circle, whatever the shape of the valley. With \(\eta_t = \eta_0 / (1 + t/\tau)\) the cloud keeps shrinking, and averaging the iterates shrinks it faster still.

Part IIISGD as a sampler

Stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics

Write the posterior under a flat prior as \(p(\theta \mid y) \propto e^{-U(\theta)}\) with \(U(\theta) = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - \theta_1 - \theta_2 x_i)^2\). Langevin dynamics steps \(\theta \leftarrow \theta - \epsilon \nabla U(\theta) + \sqrt{2\epsilon}\, \xi\), \(\xi \sim N(0, I)\), and for small \(\epsilon\) its draws come from the posterior. Welling and Teh replace \(\nabla U\) by the batch estimate \(\frac{n}{|B|}\sum_{i \in B} \nabla U_i\) and skip the accept–reject step. The extra noise from the batch adds to the injected noise, so the cloud is too wide unless \(\epsilon\) is small. Turn the injected noise off and you are back to plain SGD, whose cloud is the wrong size and shape for a posterior.

What carries over

  1. The step size has a ceiling

    On a quadratic with largest curvature \(\lambda_{\max}\), gradient descent converges only when \(\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}\), and the flattest direction converges at rate \(1 - \eta\lambda_{\min}\). The ratio \(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}\), the condition number, is why a long narrow valley is slow: centring and scaling the inputs is the cheapest optimizer there is.

  2. Constant steps buy speed and a floor

    A constant \(\eta\) gets near the optimum fast, then hovers at a mean squared distance of about \(\eta \sigma^2/|B|\) in this problem. Halve \(\eta\) or double the batch and the floor halves. Step-decay schedules in deep learning are this trade, made by hand.

  3. Average the iterates

    Polyak–Ruppert averaging of a constant-step or slowly decaying SGD reaches the best possible rate, the sampling error of \(\hat\theta\) itself, without tuning the decay. The average is less noisy than any single iterate because consecutive iterates are noisy in nearly independent directions.

  4. An optimizer is a sampler with the wrong temperature

    SGD with constant step is a Markov chain with a stationary distribution. Add noise of variance \(2\epsilon\) and the stationary distribution is the posterior, provided the batch noise is small next to it. Momentum and Adam change the path, not the target.

Momentum and Adam

Heavy-ball momentum keeps a running sum of past gradients, so steps across a narrow valley cancel and steps along it add up; on a quadratic it improves the dependence on the condition number from \(\kappa\) to \(\sqrt\kappa\). Adam divides each coordinate's step by a running root mean square of its gradients, which undoes bad scaling of the inputs coordinate by coordinate, but not correlation between them.

Why not always full batch?

Per pass over the data, full-batch gradient descent takes one step and SGD with batch size 1 takes \(n\). Early in training, when the gradient is large compared with its noise, the cheap noisy steps win by a wide margin. Near the optimum the noise dominates and the batch should grow or the step shrink: the two are nearly interchangeable, since the floor depends on \(\eta/|B|\).

The exact answer, for checking

The loss here is a quadratic, so \(\hat\theta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\) and \(H = X^\top X / n\) are known exactly, and the posterior under a flat prior is \(N(\hat\theta, \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1})\). A deep network has none of these in closed form, which is why it pays to see the behaviour first where the answer is known.
