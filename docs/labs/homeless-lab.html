Step 6 · Mechanism and confounding

Is vacancy the channel?

Rent-control cities have far fewer empty rental units. If rent control raises homelessness, it would most plausibly do it by tightening the housing market. Compare the models with and without vacancy. The identity c = c′ + a·b splits the total association into a part through vacancy and a part that is left over.

Mediation-style comparison

The full cycle in six steps

A strong, stable association that the data alone cannot call causal

Rent-control cities average 8.4 homeless per 1000 against 3.7 elsewhere. Every reasonable regression keeps a gap of roughly 3 to 5 per 1000. The question for the class is what that number means.

  1. Look before you fit

    The raw gap is 8.38 − 3.74 = 4.64 per 1000. Miami (15.9) tops the list without rent control; St. Louis and Worcester are close behind.

  2. Adjust for the usual suspects

    With nine predictors (Tucker's model), R² = 0.567 and rent control is 4.17 (se 1.31, p = 0.003). Poverty, unemployment, public housing and population are all insignificant.

  3. Read the dummy honestly

    The January-above-60°F coefficient of 11.37 is Miami's residual from the other variables. Drop Miami and the dummy, and every other coefficient is unchanged. Rent control plus the dummy alone has adjusted R² 0.510, above the full model's 0.469.

  4. Vary the specification

    The estimate stays between 4 and 5 in the named models. Across all 384 combinations it runs from about 2.6 to 5.2. The low end comes from continuous January temperature (2.87, p = 0.058 in Tucker's set), because Los Angeles, San Francisco and Santa Monica are warm and rent-controlled.

  5. Test without distributional assumptions

    No shuffle of the label among 50 cities reproduces 4.94 in the two-variable model; with 5,000 shuffles the p-value is below 1/5,000.

  6. Ask about the channel

    Vacancy alone predicts homelessness (−0.32 per point), but with rent control in the model it adds nothing (−0.07, p = 0.51). Rent control lowers vacancy by 6.3 points. With nine treated cities we cannot separate the two.

Takeaway for students. Robustness across specifications, a permutation test and clean diagnostics make an association credible. They do not make it causal. Cities chose rent control, and the cities that chose it (old, coastal, expensive, tight markets) may have had more homelessness for the same underlying reasons.

Correlation is not causation

The regression compares rent-control cities with other cities that look similar on the measured variables. Rent control was not assigned at random. Cities with booming demand and scarce housing are the ones that adopt it, and those same conditions push people out of housing.

Reverse causation is possible too: high rents and visible homelessness can create political pressure for rent control.

Mediators are not controls

If rent control lowers vacancy and low vacancy raises homelessness, then vacancy sits on the causal path. Adding it to the regression removes part of the effect you want to measure. The drop from 4.94 to 4.50 is the part that runs through vacancy, under strong assumptions.

That vacancy adds nothing given rent control does not prove vacancy is irrelevant. The two are strongly correlated (r = −0.59), and nine cities carry all the information on rent control.

What the data cannot tell us

  • One cross-section of 50 cities, observed once. No before and after.
  • Nine rent-control cities, almost all on the coasts and expensive.
  • Homeless counts depend on how each city counts.
  • Missing variables: median rent or rent-to-income, housing permits, deindustrialisation, deinstitutionalisation, shelter capacity.
  • St. Louis and Worcester (old manufacturing centres) are underpredicted by about 7 per 1000.

Try this in class

  1. Before fitting anything, ask the class to guess the rent-control coefficient in Tucker's nine-variable model from the raw gap of 4.64. Load the Tucker model and reveal 4.17. Which variables moved it, and in which direction?
  2. In the diagnostics, drag Miami's homeless rate from 15.9 to 2. With the dummy in, nothing else changes. Toggle the dummy off and repeat: now the rent-control estimate moves. Ask why a coefficient with t = 3.95 can carry no information about climate.
  3. Have each student build the "best" model they can defend and pin it. Compare the pinned forest with the specification curve. How many of the 384 models give p above 0.05, and what do they have in common? (All use continuous January temperature.)
  4. Run 5,000 shuffles for the two-variable model, then for the model with continuous January temperature. Compare the permutation p-value with the t-test p-value in each case.
  5. Switch the mechanism panel to all Tucker controls. Does c = c′ + a·b still hold exactly? Discuss: if you were a city council member, would you put vacancy in the model or leave it out, and what question does each choice answer?
