Fit it yourself

These careers are simulated. Each one gets characteristics drawn from distributions we chose (listed below), then a yes/no outcome drawn from the 8-coefficient model. The intercept is tuned so about 13.9% of the simulated population reaches the top. The page then forgets the true coefficients and estimates them by iteratively reweighted least squares (IRLS), the Newton–Raphson method for logistic regression.

Logistic regression in three lines

Coefficients add in log-odds, multiply in odds, and bend in probability

The model adds up one number per factor on the log-odds scale. That makes comparisons easy: a top MBA (1.16) is worth about as much as 13 years of experience (1.16 / 0.09), and each extra job function (0.26) about 3 years. The S-curve then turns the sum into a probability.

  1. Add up the evidence

    Start from the anchor's log-odds and add β times the change in each factor. For Person B, logit(0.15) = −1.73. Going from 2 to 4 functions adds 2 × 0.26 = 0.52, so η = −1.21.

  2. Read it as odds

    eη is the odds. Each coefficient multiplies the odds by eβ. A top MBA multiplies them by e1.16 ≈ 3.19. One more industry sector multiplies them by e−0.22 ≈ 0.80, a 20% cut.

  3. Turn it into a probability

    p = 1 / (1 + e−η). For η = −1.21 this gives 22.9%, eight points above B's 15%. Breadth across functions helps; hopping across industries hurts.

  4. Choose the anchor honestly

    Rounded coefficients only fix differences. Some person has to be pinned to a known probability. Pinning B at 15% reproduces all six of B's published one-change results. It puts C at 65% (published 63%) and A at 11% (published 6%), because the published model has more variables than these eight.

Takeaway for students. A logistic coefficient is a statement about odds. Its effect in percentage points depends on where you start, so under the B anchor the same top MBA adds about 17 points to Person A (11% to 29%), 21 points to Person B (15% to 36%), and only about 3 points to someone already at 95%.

Association, not causation

The coefficients compare people who differ in one recorded factor while the others are held fixed. They do not say what would happen if you changed that factor. People who get a top MBA or switch functions were selected, by admissions offices, employers and themselves, and may differ in ambition, networks or ability that the model never sees.

The "best next move" list reads the model as if it were causal. Treat it as a description of patterns in the data.

Who is in the data

  • Only alumni of top-10 consulting firms who worked there between 1990 and 2010. The results may not carry over to other starting points or later years.
  • LinkedIn profiles are self-reported. People who did well may be more likely to keep a complete, public profile, a form of survivorship.
  • "Top" job titles depend on how companies of 200 or more employees name roles.

Gender and the A/C gap

The male coefficient (+0.31) describes a gap in recorded outcomes between men and women in this sample after the other seven factors are accounted for. It summarises the past. It is not a statement about any individual and not advice.

The eight coefficients shown are rounded, and the published model includes more variables, such as a non-MBA master's degree and finer detail on region and education. That is why no single anchor reproduces the published 6%, 15% and 63% for A, B and C at once.

Try this in class

  1. Load Person A. Before showing the move list, ask students which single change helps A the most and by how many points. Then scroll to "Your best next move" and check. (A top MBA, about +17 points under the B anchor.)
  2. Find a profile where adding a top MBA adds fewer than 5 points. Use the S-curve panel to explain why: the profile must already sit far out on a flat part of the curve, near 0% or near 100%.
  3. Compute the years-of-experience equivalent of one more industry sector by hand (−0.22 / 0.09 ≈ −2.4 years), then confirm it in the odds-ratio table. Ask: is "two companies is worth three years" a sensible way to talk, and when does it break?
  4. In the simulation, slide the number of careers down until the 95% interval for the industry coefficient includes zero. Students should find that somewhere around 1,000 to 2,000 careers the sign is no longer reliably detected. Then predict the SE at n = 5,000 from the 1/√n rule and check.
  5. Switch the anchor to the base rate. Every prediction moves, but every waterfall step stays the same size. Ask what extra information a study must publish so readers can compute probabilities and not only odds ratios.
