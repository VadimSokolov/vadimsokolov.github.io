Tree Models Lab: from twenty questions to forests and boosting

A tree predicts by asking yes or no questions about the input, one after another, until it reaches a leaf. Part I asks the questions by hand to tell the six chess pieces apart, and shows how to score a question. Part II lets the computer ask them about a number, growing a regression tree split by split. Part III grows the tree too big on purpose and prunes it back with cross-validation. Part IV averages many big trees fitted to bootstrap samples (bagging). Part V makes the trees less alike by letting each split see only some of the inputs (a random forest). Part VI adds small trees one at a time, each fitted to what the others got wrong (boosting).

Part ITwenty questions with chess pieces

Which piece is it? Ask yes or no questions about how it moves

Each piece is described by four yes or no features: does it move in a straight line, diagonally, horizontally, and can it move many squares at once. The book's tree first asks about a straight line, which sets the knight apart, then asks about diagonals, then horizontal moves, and last about many squares. Its six leaves name the six pieces. Click a node that still holds several pieces and choose the question to ask there, or load the book's tree.

A good question splits the pieces into groups that are as pure as possible. Trees measure this with the entropy \(H = -\sum_k p_k \log_2 p_k\) (in bits) or the Gini index \(G = \sum_k p_k (1 - p_k)\) of the pieces in a node. The information gain of a question is the drop in entropy, the parent's entropy minus the size-weighted entropy of the two children. A split into 3 and 3 gains a full bit; setting the knight apart (5 and 1) gains only 0.65 bits.

Part IIA simple tree: recursive binary splitting

Grow a regression tree one split at a time

Now the input is a number, \(x\) = percent of lower-status residents in a neighborhood, and the output is \(y\) = median home value in thousands of dollars (100 simulated neighborhoods). A regression tree cuts the \(x\) axis into intervals and predicts the average \(y\) in each, so its fit is a step function. To choose a split, try every cut point \(s\) in a leaf and keep the one that most reduces the residual sum of squares (RSS):

\[ \min_{j,\,s}\ \Big[ \sum_{i:\,x_{ij} < s} (y_i - \bar y_{1})^2 + \sum_{i:\,x_{ij} \ge s} (y_i - \bar y_{2})^2 \Big] \]

The lower panel draws that RSS reduction against \(s\) for every current leaf. The highest peak is the next split. Finding the best tree of a given size is NP-hard, so CART grows greedily like this and never revisits a split.

Part IIIPruning: grow big, then cut back

Cost-complexity pruning, with cross-validation choosing the size

Stopping early is myopic, since a weak split can lead to a strong one. So grow a large tree \(T_0\) (leaves down to 2 points), then prune. For each \(\alpha \ge 0\), keep the subtree \(T \subset T_0\) that minimizes

\[ \sum_{m=1}^{|T|} \sum_{i:\,x_i \in R_m} (y_i - \bar y_m)^2 + \alpha\,|T| . \]

As \(\alpha\) grows, the weakest link goes first: the split whose RSS reduction per extra leaf, \(g(t) = \big(\mathrm{RSS}(t) - \mathrm{RSS}(T_t)\big) / (|T_t| - 1)\), is smallest. This gives a nested sequence of subtrees, from \(T_0\) down to the root alone. Ten-fold cross-validation estimates the test error of each one without touching the test set. The one-standard-error rule takes the smallest tree whose CV error is within one standard error of the minimum.

Part IVBagging: average many big trees

Bootstrap the data, grow a big tree on each sample, and average

A big tree has low bias and high variance: a slightly different sample gives a quite different step function. Bagging (Breiman, 1996) treats the sample as the population. It draws \(B\) bootstrap samples of size \(n\) with replacement, grows an unpruned tree on each (leaves of at least 5 points), and averages them, \(\hat f_{\text{bag}}(x) = \frac1B \sum_b \hat f_b(x)\). Each bootstrap sample leaves out about \((1 - 1/n)^n \approx e^{-1} = 37\%\) of the points. Predicting each point with only the trees that did not see it gives the out-of-bag (OOB) error, a test error estimate that needs no held-out data.

Part VRandom forest: make the trees disagree

Let each split see only m of the p inputs

Bagged trees look alike, because the strongest input wins the top splits in almost every bootstrap sample. Averaging alike trees removes little variance. If each tree's error has mean square \(\sigma^2\) and two trees' errors have correlation \(\rho\), the average of \(B\) trees has mean squared error

\[ \rho\,\sigma^2 + \frac{1 - \rho}{B}\,\sigma^2 , \]

which falls to \(\rho\sigma^2\), not to zero, as \(B\) grows. A random forest (Breiman, 2001) lowers \(\rho\): at each split it picks \(m\) of the \(p\) inputs at random and splits only on those. Smaller \(m\) makes the trees less alike. But a split that may only choose among a few inputs, some of them noise, can make single trees worse (larger \(\sigma^2\)), so the best \(m\) is usually in between; \(m = p\) is plain bagging. The simulated data show where this matters most: 300 points with \(p = 10\) inputs. Inputs \(x_1\) to \(x_6\) are six noisy readings of one hidden quantity \(z\), each \(x_j = 0.6\,z + 0.4\,u_j\) with \(z\) and \(u_j\) uniform on \([0,1]\), like six survey questions that measure the same trait. Inputs \(x_7\) to \(x_{10}\) are pure noise. The response is \(y = 5(x_1 + \dots + x_6) + \varepsilon\), \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, 3^2)\). Bagged trees keep splitting on whichever reading looks best, so they share its measurement error. A forest forces different trees onto different readings, and averaging them averages the readings. Errors here are measured against the true function on 1,000 test points, so they exclude the noise. In the formula, \(\sigma^2\) and \(\rho\) are measured over the test points with the training sample held fixed, and with those definitions it is an exact identity.

Part VIBoosting: fit what is left, a little at a time

Add small trees, each fitted to the residuals of the sum so far

Boosting builds the model in sequence. Start from the mean of \(y\) (as gbm does; the book's version starts from 0, which costs only a few extra trees). Then repeat \(B\) times: fit a small tree \(\hat f^b\) of depth \(d\) to the current residuals \(r_i\), add a shrunken copy to the model, and update the residuals,

\[ \hat f(x) \leftarrow \hat f(x) + \lambda \hat f^{b}(x), \qquad r_i \leftarrow r_i - \lambda \hat f^{b}(x_i) . \]

For squared loss the residual is minus the gradient of the loss at the current fit, so boosting is gradient descent in the space of functions, with each tree a step and \(\lambda\) the step size. Small \(\lambda\) learns slowly and needs many trees; large \(\lambda\) learns fast and starts fitting noise sooner. Unlike bagging, more trees can hurt: watch the test error turn up.

What to take away

  1. A tree is a list of questions

    Each internal node asks about one input, each leaf gives one answer. For a number the answer is the leaf's average, so the fit is a step function over boxes in the input space.

  2. Score a question by how pure it leaves the groups

    Entropy and the Gini index for classes, the RSS for numbers. Greedy splitting picks the best question now, which is fast and usually good, but not always the best tree.

  3. Size is the knob

    One leaf underfits, a leaf per point overfits. Grow big, then prune with a penalty α per leaf, and let cross-validation choose α.

  4. Averaging cuts variance, but only the part the trees do not share

    Bagging averages big trees on bootstrap samples; its floor is ρσ². A random forest lowers ρ by showing each split only m inputs.

  5. Boosting cuts bias, one small step at a time

    Each new tree fits the residuals, a gradient step in function space. The learning rate and the number of trees trade off, and too many trees overfit.

\[ H(\text{node}) - \tfrac{n_L}{n} H(L) - \tfrac{n_R}{n} H(R) \]
Cost complexity
\[ C_\alpha(T) = \mathrm{RSS}(T) + \alpha |T| \]

Why the chess tree is not optimal

The book's tree asks 3 questions on average when the pieces come up equally often. Asking about diagonals first splits the pieces 3 and 3 and gives 2.67, the best possible with these four questions. Entropy says no yes or no questions at all can average fewer than \(\log_2 6 = 2.58\). When the pieces come up as on the board, pawns are half of them, and the best tree changes: it should reach the pawn fast.

Trees in more dimensions

With several inputs the search runs over every input \(j\) and every cut \(s\), and the leaves become boxes. A tree handles interactions on its own (a split on \(x_2\) below a split on \(x_1\)) and ignores inputs it never splits on, which is why the noise inputs in Part V get near zero importance. Its weakness is that boxes give a jagged fit to smooth or diagonal shapes.

  9. Part VI: with λ = 1, find the B where test error is lowest and then move B to 1000. Repeat with λ = 0.01. Which setting gives the lowest test error, and at what cost in trees?
