Needle in a Haystack

A study measures the expression of 1,000 genes on 100 patients. Only a handful of genes move the disease score: those are the needles, the rest is hay. Compare what each gene shows on its own with least squares, ridge, the lasso and a spike-and-slab prior, all fitted to the same simulated data, and find out when the needles can no longer be found.

Three ways to shrink a noisy estimate

Least squares: no shrinkage Ridge Lasso Spike-and-slab posterior mean Bonferroni |t|

Suppose the genes were uncorrelated, so each gene's raw evidence is a z-score \(z = \hat\beta_{\text{LS}}\sqrt{n} / \sigma\). Each method then becomes a function of \(z\) alone. The lasso curve uses the \(\lambda\) chosen above, so moving the lasso slider moves it; ridge has its own slider here.

0.50
Raw zRidgeLassoSlab

Ridge multiplies every \(z\) by the same factor \(1/(1 + \lambda)\), so nothing is ever exactly zero. The lasso subtracts the same amount \(\lambda\sqrt{n}/\sigma\) from every \(z\), so small ones become exactly zero and big ones are biased toward zero by a constant. The spike-and-slab posterior mean is close to zero for noise-sized \(z\) and close to \(z\) for big ones: it shrinks hay hard and leaves needles alone.

Geometry of the penalty

Why the lasso gives exact zeros and ridge does not

Penalised least squares is the same as least squares inside a budget: \(\|\beta\|_1 \le t\) for the lasso (a diamond) or \(\|\beta\|_2 \le t\) for ridge (a circle). The solution is where the smallest error ellipse around the least-squares point first touches the budget. A diamond has corners on the axes, and the ellipse usually touches a corner, where one coefficient is exactly zero. A circle has no corners. Drag on the plot to move the least-squares point.

The model
\(\displaystyle y = X\beta + \varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I), \quad \sigma = 1\)
\(\displaystyle \beta_j = \pm\text{signal for } k \text{ needles, } 0 \text{ for the other } p - k \text{ genes}\)
Lasso, the scaling used here and in scikit-learn
\(\displaystyle \hat\beta = \operatorname{argmin}\ \frac{1}{2n}\|y - X\beta\|^2 + \lambda\|\beta\|_1\)
\(\displaystyle \text{coordinate step: } \beta_j \leftarrow S(x_j^\top r_j/n, \lambda), \quad S(z, \lambda) = \operatorname{sign}(z)\,(|z| - \lambda)_+\)
Ridge, closed form
\(\displaystyle \hat\beta = (X^\top X + n\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y = X^\top(XX^\top + n\lambda I)^{-1}y\)
Lasso is a posterior mode under a Laplace prior
\(\displaystyle p(\beta_j) \propto \exp(-|\beta_j|/s) \;\Rightarrow\; -\log p(\beta \mid y) = \|y - X\beta\|^2/2\sigma^2 + \|\beta\|_1/s\)
\(\displaystyle \text{so the mode is the lasso with } \lambda = \sigma^2/(n s)\)
Spike-and-slab prior fitted here (Gibbs sampler)
\(\displaystyle \beta_j \sim (1 - \pi)\,\delta_0 + \pi\,N(0, \sigma^2\tau^2), \quad \tau = 1, \quad \pi \sim \text{Beta}(1, 1), \quad p(\sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2\)
\(\displaystyle \text{PIP}_j = P(\beta_j \ne 0 \mid y)\)

Takeaway for students. With more genes than patients, the data alone cannot pin down \(\beta\): infinitely many coefficient vectors fit perfectly. A sparsity assumption, written as an L1 penalty or as a prior that puts most genes at exactly zero, is what makes the problem solvable, and it only works when the needles are strong enough relative to \(\sigma\sqrt{2 \log p / n}\).

Why \(p \gg n\) needs a sparsity assumption

With p = 1,000 genes and n = 100 patients, \(X^\top X\) is a \(1{,}000 \times 1{,}000\) matrix of rank at most 99. The normal equations have infinitely many solutions, and every one of them fits the training data exactly. Least squares cannot choose between them.

If only k genes matter, there are only k unknowns to find, plus the job of finding which ones. That is possible when n is of order \(k \log(p/k)\), far smaller than p.

L1 versus L2

\[ \text{ridge: } \hat\beta = z/(1 + \lambda) \qquad \text{lasso: } \hat\beta = S(z, \lambda) \]

Ridge shrinks every coefficient by the same proportion and keeps all genes. That helps prediction a little but finds no needles. The lasso shrinks every coefficient by the same amount and sets the small ones to exactly zero, so it selects genes. The price is bias: big needles are pulled toward zero too.

The lasso is a Bayesian posterior mode

\[ \text{Laplace prior } p(\beta) \propto e^{-|\beta|/s} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{mode} = \text{lasso} \]

The Laplace density has a sharp peak at zero, which is what produces exact zeros at the mode. But its posterior mean is never exactly zero, and its tails are only exponential, so it cannot leave big signals alone. The penalty is a prior in disguise.

Most genes are exactly hay, a few are big

Spike-and-slab says this literally: each gene is either exactly zero (the spike) or drawn from a wide normal (the slab). The horseshoe prior says it smoothly, with a density that has a pole at zero and heavy Cauchy-like tails.

Both give the shrinkage profile in the plot above: noise-sized estimates are pulled to zero, big ones are left almost untouched. Inclusion probabilities give an honest answer to "is this gene a needle?"

Multiple testing: screening versus joint modelling

\[ \text{Bonferroni: } p_j < 0.05/p \qquad \text{BH: } p_{(i)} \le 0.05 \cdot i/p \]

Testing genes one at a time is marginal screening. At p = 0.05 about 50 of 1,000 hay genes pass by chance. Bonferroni controls the chance of any false alarm; Benjamini–Hochberg controls the expected share of false alarms. Each gene's marginal \(|t|\) also carries noise from the other needles, which a joint model removes.

Correlated genes

When two genes are highly correlated, the lasso tends to pick one of them, and which one can flip with a few patients. A hay gene next to a needle can take the needle's place. Marginal screening has the opposite problem: hay neighbours of a needle light up too.

Spike-and-slab spreads the inclusion probability across the pair, saying "one of these two" with roughly 0.5 each.
