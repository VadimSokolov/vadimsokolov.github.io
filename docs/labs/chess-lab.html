Chess Lab: a ten-layer ReLU network that plays

In 2016 Silver and colleagues at DeepMind beat the best human Go players with three ingredients: a policy network that proposes moves, a value network that judges positions, and a tree search that lets the two check each other. This lab rebuilds that recipe for chess at a size your browser can run. One network with ten ReLU layers reads the board and outputs both a probability for every move and a probability of winning. Play against it, watch what search adds to the bare network, and look inside the ten layers while it thinks.

← All labs

Recipe: Silver et al., Mastering the game of Go with deep neural networks and tree search, Nature 529, 484–489 (2016); the move-selection rule is the paper's PUCT, and as in AlphaGo Zero (Nature 550, 2017) the search uses the value network alone, without rollouts. AlphaZero brought the recipe to chess (Science 362, 2018). The network here was trained for this lab on 600,000 positions from engine games with random openings, each scored by Stockfish 18 at depth 9: ten fully connected ReLU layers (nine of 256 units and a last one of 128), with skip connections around the eight 256-to-256 layers, 1.29 million parameters, 15 passes over the data on a laptop. The browser runs it with no server.

Policy: which moves are worth a look?

\(p_\theta(a \mid s)\), a probability over the legal moves in position \(s\). AlphaGo learned it from 30 million positions of expert games. Ours learned to predict the move Stockfish prefers. A strong policy alone already plays legal, sensible chess, and it is instant.

Value: who is winning?

\(v_\theta(s)\), the probability that the side to move wins. AlphaGo trained it on the outcomes of self-play games. Ours is trained on Stockfish's evaluation turned into a win probability, \(1/(1 + 10^{-\text{cp}/400})\). It replaces looking ahead to the end of the game.

Search: let the two check each other

Monte Carlo tree search walks down the game tree choosing \(a = \arg\max_a Q(s,a) + c\,P(s,a)\,\frac{\sqrt{N(s)}}{1 + N(s,a)}\): exploit moves that have looked good, explore moves the policy likes but has not tried. At the leaves the value network takes over.

What carries over

  1. Two heads, one trunk

    The ten layers compute one representation of the board; a linear head turns it into 4,096 move scores (from square × to square, masked to the legal ones and passed through a softmax) and another into a win probability. Sharing the trunk means each task teaches the other what features of the board matter.

  2. The network replaces calculation with recognition

    Stockfish's label at depth 9 summarizes millions of positions searched. The network learns to recognize the result from the board alone, in one pass of about a million multiply–adds. It is good at familiar shapes and bad at tactics it cannot see without looking ahead.

  3. Search fixes what recognition misses

    A few hundred simulations let the value network check the policy's suggestions a few moves deep. Blunders that hang a piece in two moves mostly disappear. This is the paper's central point: neither network alone, nor search alone, but the combination.

  4. Depth is not free

    Trained plainly, the ten-layer network learned worst of all: deep stacks of ReLUs are hard to optimize. Skip connections, \(h \leftarrow h + \mathrm{ReLU}(Wh + b)\), fix the optimization; they are the trick behind residual networks and AlphaGo Zero. Even then, on this task a two-layer network of the same size is as good or better. Depth pays when the problem has structure to compose; it is not a free upgrade.

What differs from AlphaGo

AlphaGo's networks were convolutional, 13 layers of 3 × 3 filters on a 19 × 19 board, trained first on human games and then by self-play reinforcement learning on thousands of processors. Ours is fully connected, learns from an engine's labels instead of from self-play, and was trained in minutes on a laptop. The search is the same idea at a smaller scale.

Why fully connected

A convolution shares weights across the board, which suits Go, where a shape means the same thing anywhere. In chess the meaning of a piece depends on long lines across the board. Strong small chess networks, such as Stockfish's NNUE, are fully connected on sparse piece–square inputs like ours, which also makes the first layer cheap: only the 20 to 32 occupied squares contribute.

Mirroring the board

The network always sees the position from the side to move: when Black is to move the board is flipped top to bottom and the colours swapped. One network then serves both sides, and a position and its mirror image are the same training example. The value is always "my chance of winning".

Try this in class

  1. Set "Policy only" and play a quick game. Where does it play well, and when does it first leave a piece where it can be taken? Replay the position with "Search" at 300 simulations.
  2. Load the position after 1. e4 e5 2. Qh5 Nc6 3. Bc4, r1bqkbnr/pppp1ppp/2n5/4p2Q/2B1P3/8/PPPP1PPP/RNB1K1NR b KQkq - 3 3, and press "What would it play?" with each method. Which defends f7 with g6 or Qe7, and which plays the natural-looking Nf6, allowing Qxf7 mate?
  3. Watch the candidate table during a search: how do the visit counts compare with the policy's prior? Find a move the search ends up preferring that the policy rated low.
  4. In Part II, make one quiet pawn move and count the units that switch in each layer. Then capture a piece. Which change moves the position further, and in which layers?
  5. In Part III, the two-layer network beats the plain ten-layer one of the same size, and the skip connections close most of the gap. Why is a deep plain ReLU stack harder to train? What kind of chess knowledge would you expect depth to help with, and why might 600,000 positions not be enough to show it?
  6. The value is trained on engine evaluations, not on game results. What would change if we trained it on who actually won, as AlphaGo did? Think about the noise in a single game's result.
