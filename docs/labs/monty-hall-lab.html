Monty's action carries information

Your first pick is right 1/3 of the time, and nothing Monty does can change that. What changes is the other closed door. Monty knows where the car is and is never allowed to show it, so when your pick is wrong he is forced to leave the car behind the door he skips.

  1. Prior

    The car is equally likely behind each door: P(A) = P(B) = P(C) = 1/3. You pick door A.

  2. Likelihood of "Monty opens B"

    If the car is at A, Monty can open B or C and picks one at random: 1/2. If it is at B, he cannot open B: 0. If it is at C, he must open B: 1.

  3. Posterior

    Multiply prior by likelihood and rescale. P(A | MB) = (1/6) / (1/2) = 1/3 and P(C | MB) = (1/3) / (1/2) = 2/3. Switching doubles your chance.

  4. Remove the knowledge and the edge disappears

    If Monty opens a door at random, "opens B and shows a goat" is equally likely whether the car is at A or C. The likelihoods match, so the posteriors match: 1/2 and 1/2. Games where he shows the car are thrown out.

Bayes' rule
P(A | MB) = (1/2)(1/3) / [(1/2)(1/3) + 0 + (1)(1/3)]
= (1/6) / (1/2) = 1/3
P(C | MB) = (1)(1/3) / (1/2) = 2/3
n doors, Monty opens n − 2 goats
P(win | stay) = 1/n
P(win | switch) = (n − 1)/n
Monty opens at random, car not shown
P(no car shown) = 2/n
P(win | stay) = P(win | switch) = 1/2

Takeaway for students. When someone with private information takes an action, the action itself is data. Infer from what they chose to do, not only from what was revealed. The same reasoning drives signalling, adverse selection and game theory.

Try this in class

  1. Have every student play five games with the doors, always switching or always staying by row of the room. Pool the tallies on the board before you run the simulation.
  2. Press "Simulate 1,000 games" and point out how far the lines wander in the first 50 games. Ask how many games a skeptic would need to watch before being convinced.
  3. Set 10 doors. Monty opens 8 goats and leaves one door closed. Ask whether students would still keep their first pick, then read the 9/10 off the table.
  4. Tick "Monty doesn't know". Both lines move to 1/2 and about a third of the games are discarded. Ask which line in the Bayes table changed.
  5. With 10 doors and an ignorant Monty, 80% of games are discarded. Ask why the games he gets through without showing the car are so evenly split.
