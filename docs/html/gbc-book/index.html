Preface

This book presents Generative Bayesian Computation (GBC) — a framework for Bayesian inference, prediction, and decision-making that replaces Markov chain Monte Carlo with deep quantile neural networks trained by stochastic gradient descent.

The core idea is simple: if you can simulate from a model, you can learn the posterior, the predictive distribution, or the optimal decision — all via the noise outsourcing theorem and implicit quantile networks. No likelihood evaluation, no convergence diagnostics, no chains.

Who is this book for?

We wrote this book for three overlapping communities, and we have tried to make it useful to each without assuming prior knowledge from the others.

Statisticians and Bayesians will find here a fresh computational approach to problems they already care about. If you have spent time tuning Metropolis–Hastings proposals, diagnosing \(\hat{R}\) convergence statistics, or worrying about whether your variational family is flexible enough, then GBC offers an alternative that sidesteps these difficulties entirely. We develop the theory carefully, starting from the quantile function as a fundamental representation of uncertainty (3  Quantile Functions and the Pinball Loss), proceeding through the noise outsourcing theorem that justifies our approach (4  The Noise Outsourcing Theorem), and arriving at implicit quantile networks as the computational engine (5  Implicit Quantile Networks). The Bayesian inference chapter (10  Bayesian Inference Without MCMC) is the pivot of the book: it shows that once you can simulate pairs \((\theta, y)\) from the joint distribution, a neural network can learn the posterior quantile function directly. You get calibrated posterior intervals for any new observation via a single forward pass. The noise outsourcing theorem (4  The Noise Outsourcing Theorem) guarantees that the quantile function representation is theoretically exact: any conditional distribution has a quantile function representation, and no information is lost. What introduces approximation is the neural network’s finite capacity and the finite training set — the same sources of error as in any other statistical estimator. 14  When IQN Fails: Calibration and Remedies documents honestly where those approximations can fail and how to detect and correct them.

Machine learning researchers, particularly those working in distributional reinforcement learning, generative modeling, or uncertainty quantification, will discover that their tools have deep Bayesian roots. The implicit quantile network of Dabney et al. (2018), originally designed for distributional RL, turns out to be a general-purpose Bayesian engine. We trace this connection explicitly in 6  Distributional Reinforcement Learning, showing that the same Wasserstein contraction arguments that justify quantile temporal-difference learning also justify GBC surrogates and posterior inference. If you have implemented IQN for Atari games, you already have most of the machinery needed for likelihood-free Bayesian computation. What we add is the statistical theory — calibration guarantees, conformal wrappers, and connections to classical Bayesian decision theory — that turns a prediction algorithm into a principled inference framework.

Applied scientists and engineers who use computer simulators will find practical tools that scale where Gaussian processes cannot. If you run expensive simulations — climate models, finite-element codes, epidemiological ABMs, stochastic differential equations — and need to quantify uncertainty in the outputs, GBC surrogates (7  GBC Surrogates for Computer Experiments) provide a drop-in replacement for GP emulators that trains in \(O(n)\) time rather than \(O(n^3)\). We devote an entire chapter to jump discontinuities (8  Boundary Augmentation for Jump Processes), which are common in engineering simulators and notoriously difficult for smooth GP kernels. The active learning chapter (9  Sequential Design and Active Learning) shows how to design sequential experiments that allocate simulator evaluations where they are most needed. We have tried to make every method accessible through the gbc Python package with minimal boilerplate, so you can move from reading to running code in minutes.

What makes this book different

The standard workflow for applied Bayesian statistics is: specify a prior \(\pi(\theta)\), write down a likelihood \(p(y \mid \theta)\), and compute the posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid y)\) via Markov chain Monte Carlo (see, e.g., Gelman et al. 2013; Robert and Casella 2004). This is a powerful and general framework, and we have no quarrel with the Bayesian foundations. Our quarrel is with the computation. MCMC requires evaluating the likelihood at every proposed parameter value, which is impossible for simulator-based models where the likelihood is intractable. Even when the likelihood is available, MCMC chains can be slow to mix, difficult to diagnose, and expensive to run for high-dimensional posteriors. GBC replaces the entire MCMC pipeline with a single training phase: simulate \((\theta, y)\) pairs from the prior predictive distribution, train an IQN on these pairs, and obtain posterior quantiles for any new observation by forward pass. The Bayesian answers are the same — we are computing the same posterior — but the computation is fundamentally different.

The probabilistic machine learning textbooks by Bishop and by Murphy provide comprehensive treatments of approximate inference: variational methods, expectation propagation, and normalizing flows. These are elegant algorithms, but they require specifying and differentiating through a likelihood function or a variational family. GBC is simulation-based at its core. If your generative model is a black-box simulator written in Fortran, a stochastic agent-based model, or a system of SDEs solved by Euler–Maruyama, GBC works without modification. You need only the ability to run the simulator forward — the neural network learns the inverse map from data to parameters.

Bobby Gramacy’s Surrogates is the closest book in spirit to ours, and we have consciously followed its conversational, code-integrated style. Where Surrogates builds on Gaussian process emulators for deterministic and stochastic computer experiments, we build on implicit quantile networks. The two approaches share the goal of replacing expensive simulators with fast statistical approximations, but they differ in scalability and in their treatment of distributional outputs. We compare GBC surrogates to GP emulators head-to-head on several benchmark problems throughout the book, and the results consistently favor GBC when the response surface has jump discontinuities, when the input dimension exceeds about ten, or when full predictive distributions (not just means and variances) are required.

Notation and conventions

We use a consistent notation throughout the book, and we collect the most important symbols here for reference. The quantile level is always denoted \(\tau \in (0,1)\), and we think of \(\tau\) as a draw from the standard uniform distribution. The central object of our framework is the conditional quantile function \(G^*(\tau, x)\), which maps a quantile level \(\tau\) and a covariate (or conditioning variable) \(x\) to the \(\tau\)th quantile of the conditional distribution of \(Y\) given \(X = x\). When \(x\) is a dataset \(y\) and the response is a parameter \(\theta\), the same object becomes the posterior quantile function \(G^*(\tau, y)\) — the \(\tau\)th quantile of \(\pi(\theta \mid y)\).

The loss function that drives all of our learning is the pinball loss, also called the check loss or the asymmetric absolute loss. For a single observation \(y\) and a quantile estimate \(\hat{q}\), the pinball loss at level \(\tau\) is \(\rho_\tau(y - \hat{q}) = (y - \hat{q})(\tau - \mathbf{1}(y - \hat{q} < 0))\). This loss penalizes overestimates and underestimates asymmetrically: when \(\tau = 0.9\), underprediction is penalized nine times as heavily as overprediction. Minimizing the expected pinball loss over all \(\tau \in (0,1)\) simultaneously is equivalent to learning the entire conditional distribution, a fact we prove in 3  Quantile Functions and the Pinball Loss and exploit in every subsequent chapter.

On the Bayesian side, we write \(\pi(\theta)\) for the prior density on the parameter \(\theta\) and \(\pi(\theta \mid y)\) for the posterior density given observed data \(y\). The likelihood is \(p(y \mid \theta)\), and the prior predictive (marginal likelihood) is \(m(y) = \int p(y \mid \theta) \pi(\theta) \, d\theta\). We emphasize that GBC never evaluates the likelihood directly — we only require the ability to sample \(y \sim p(\cdot \mid \theta)\) for any given \(\theta\). Throughout the book, neural network parameters are denoted \(\phi\) (to distinguish them from model parameters \(\theta\)), and we write \(G_\phi(\tau, x)\) for the IQN’s learned approximation to the true quantile function \(G^*(\tau, x)\).

How to read this book

The book has three parts, and we have designed them so that readers with different goals can chart their own path through the material.

Part I (Chapters 1–5) builds the foundations that everything else rests on. We begin with a motivational overview of the GBC philosophy (1  Why Generative Bayesian Computation?) and introduce the datasets that serve as running examples throughout the book (2  Motivating Datasets). We then develop the mathematical theory: quantile functions and the pinball loss (3  Quantile Functions and the Pinball Loss), the noise outsourcing theorem that guarantees the existence of a quantile representation for any conditional distribution (4  The Noise Outsourcing Theorem), and implicit quantile networks that learn this representation from data (5  Implicit Quantile Networks). These five chapters should be read in order, as each builds directly on the previous one.

Part II (Chapters 6–9) connects distributional reinforcement learning to surrogate modeling. We begin with the RL origins of IQN (6  Distributional Reinforcement Learning), which provides both historical context and theoretical depth. The surrogates chapter (7  GBC Surrogates for Computer Experiments) shows how IQN replaces Gaussian processes for computer experiments. The boundary augmentation chapter (8  Boundary Augmentation for Jump Processes) tackles jump discontinuities that defeat smooth emulators. The active learning chapter (9  Sequential Design and Active Learning) develops sequential experimental design strategies. After reading 6  Distributional Reinforcement Learning, the remaining chapters of Part II can be read in any order.

Part III (Chapters 10–14) develops inference and applications. The Bayesian inference chapter (10  Bayesian Inference Without MCMC) is the pivot of the entire book, showing how to perform posterior computation without MCMC. This leads naturally to generative prediction (11  Generative Bayesian Prediction) and maximum expected utility (12  Maximum Expected Utility), which extend the framework to forecasting and decision-making. The causal inference chapter (13  Causal Inference via GBC) applies GBC to treatment effect estimation and requires 10  Bayesian Inference Without MCMC as a prerequisite. The calibration chapter (14  When IQN Fails: Calibration and Remedies) is the honest self-assessment: it examines when IQN fails and develops conformal and recalibration remedies.

For a short course on GBC surrogates, we recommend Chapters 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7. For a short course on simulation-based Bayesian inference, the path is Chapters 1, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, and 11. All readers should include 14  When IQN Fails: Calibration and Remedies (Chapter 14) regardless of path: it documents the known failure mode of the multiplicative τ-architecture and the conformal remedy, and this context is essential before deploying GBC on real problems.

pip install git+https://github.com/VadimSokolov/gbc.git
import gbc

The package provides modules for implicit quantile networks (gbc.iqn), the pinball loss (gbc.loss), ensemble methods (gbc.ensemble), boundary augmentation (gbc.augment), active learning (gbc.active_learning), causal inference (gbc.causal), conformal calibration (gbc.conformal), and plotting utilities (gbc.plotting). Every code block in this book imports from gbc and can be reproduced by installing the package and running the .qmd source files with Quarto.
