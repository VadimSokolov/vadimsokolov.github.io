1  Why Generative Bayesian Computation?

“All models are wrong, but some are useful.” – George Box

Bayesian statistics provides a coherent framework for learning from data under uncertainty. You specify a prior \(\pi(\theta)\) and a likelihood \(p(y \mid \theta)\), and Bayes’ theorem delivers the posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid y)\). The open question is computation: how do we actually get our hands on the posterior?

For the past three decades, the dominant answer has been Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). You construct a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior, run it for a very long time, and hope that the samples you collect are representative. The Metropolis algorithm dates to 1953 (Metropolis et al. 1953), but MCMC became a practical tool for Bayesian inference only after Gelfand and Smith (1990) demonstrated its power on standard statistical models. Since then, MCMC has transformed applied statistics, from Gibbs sampling through Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and NUTS that power modern probabilistic programming.

But MCMC comes with a cost: convergence diagnostics, mixing failures in high dimensions, and the need to rerun the chain from scratch for every new dataset. In a world where models are growing more complex, datasets are growing larger, and inference must happen in real time, these bottlenecks become increasingly binding.

This book develops an alternative. Generative Bayesian Computation (GBC) replaces MCMC with a neural network trained by stochastic gradient descent. The idea: simulate a large collection of (parameter, data) pairs from the prior and likelihood, then train an implicit quantile network (IQN) to learn the map from data to posterior quantiles. Once trained, the network produces posterior samples for any observed dataset via a single forward pass — no chains, no burn-in, no convergence diagnostics. The posterior quantile function is the fundamental object: it encodes everything we need to know about the posterior distribution, and it can be learned from simulated data using standard supervised learning techniques.

The theoretical foundation is the noise outsourcing theorem (Kallenberg 1997), which guarantees that any conditional distribution \(Y \mid X\) can be represented as \(Y = G^*(U, X)\) where \(U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\) and \(G^*(\tau, x) = Q_{Y|X}(\tau \mid x)\) is the conditional quantile function. The computational engine is the Implicit Quantile Network (IQN), borrowed from distributional reinforcement learning (Dabney et al. 2018) and repurposed for statistical inference. The IQN takes \(\tau \in (0,1)\) and the input \(x\) and produces the conditional quantile \(\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)\), trained using the pinball loss on simulated data.

Consider a concrete example that will recur throughout this book. NASA’s Langley Glide-Back Booster (LGBB) is a reusable rocket concept whose aerodynamic drag coefficient depends on Mach number, angle of attack, and sideslip angle. Engineers run a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulator to predict drag, but each simulation takes hours on a cluster. MCMC fails here because the CFD simulator is a black box — there is no closed-form likelihood to evaluate. GBC takes a different route: simulate thousands of (input, drag) pairs, train an IQN on these pairs, and produce posterior quantiles for any new observation via a single forward pass. We introduce this example in Chapter 2, build a surrogate in Chapter 7, and develop the active learning strategy in Chapter 9, where GBC achieves an RMSE of 0.00723, a factor of \(2.95\times\) better than deep Gaussian processes.

1.1 Three Paradigms for Bayesian Computation

There are three main strategies for turning the abstract formula \[ \pi(\theta \mid y) \propto p(y \mid \theta) \, \pi(\theta) \tag{1.1}\] into a usable computational object. Each makes a different trade-off between generality, accuracy, and computational cost. We describe each in turn, then work through the normal-normal conjugate model to see all three in action.

MCMC: The Workhorse.

Markov chain Monte Carlo constructs a Markov chain \(\theta^{(1)}, \theta^{(2)}, \ldots\) whose stationary distribution is the posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid y)\). After a sufficient burn-in period, the remaining samples approximate the posterior, and expectations can be estimated by sample averages: \(E[g(\theta) \mid y] \approx T^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^T g(\theta^{(t)})\). Standard textbook treatments (e.g., Gelman et al. 2013; Robert and Casella 2004) provide comprehensive coverage, and probabilistic programming languages — Stan (Carpenter et al. 2017), PyMC, NumPyro — have made MCMC accessible across the sciences.

But MCMC has structural limitations that become increasingly apparent as problems grow in scale and complexity.

First, there is no general-purpose criterion that guarantees a chain has converged. We run multiple chains, compute \(\hat{R}\) statistics, and inspect trace plots — all heuristic. The split-\(\hat{R}\) diagnostic of Vehtari et al. (2021) is the current state of the art, but it provides only a necessary condition for convergence, not a sufficient one. A chain can pass all diagnostics and still have failed to explore the full posterior.

Second, posterior geometry becomes treacherous as dimension grows. Multimodal posteriors require the chain to jump between modes, which happens exponentially rarely for random-walk proposals. Funnel geometries — common in hierarchical models — cause the chain to get stuck in the narrow end. These are not hypothetical concerns; they arise routinely in applied Bayesian work.

Third, each observed dataset requires a fresh MCMC run. Fitting the same model to 10,000 sensors means 10,000 separate chains, each costing \(O(Tn)\) where \(T\) is the chain length and \(n\) is the sample size. There is no way to reuse the work done for one dataset when analyzing the next.

Finally, at every iteration MCMC must evaluate the likelihood \(p(y \mid \theta)\). For simulator-based models — agent-based models, climate simulators, cosmological codes — the likelihood has no closed form, and standard MCMC simply does not apply. The model can generate data given parameters, but it cannot evaluate the probability of observed data given parameters. This is the situation GBC was designed for.

ABC: The First Likelihood-Free Method.

Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) was the first systematic attempt to perform Bayesian inference without evaluating the likelihood. The conceptual idea, articulated by Rubin (1984), is to recast Bayesian inference as a pure simulation exercise: draw \(\theta\) from the prior, simulate \(y^{\text{sim}}\) from the model, and accept \(\theta\) whenever \(y^{\text{sim}}\) is “close” to the observed data \(y_{\text{obs}}\). If the simulator faithfully represents reality, then parameters that produce data similar to what we observed are the ones that are plausible given the data — which is exactly what the posterior captures.

In its simplest form (rejection ABC), one draws \(\theta\) from the prior, simulates \(y^{\text{sim}} \mid \theta\), and accepts \(\theta\) if \(y^{\text{sim}} = y_{\text{obs}}\). For continuous data this acceptance probability is zero, so in practice one compares low-dimensional summary statistics \(S(y^{\text{sim}})\) and \(S(y_{\text{obs}})\), accepting when \(\|S(y^{\text{sim}}) - S(y_{\text{obs}})\| < \varepsilon\) for some tolerance \(\varepsilon > 0\). The resulting samples approximate \(\pi(\theta \mid S(y_{\text{obs}}))\) — the posterior conditioned on the summary statistic, not the full data. More sophisticated variants — MCMC-ABC, sequential Monte Carlo ABC (SMC-ABC), and regression-adjusted ABC — improve efficiency but share the same fundamental structure. The method was formalized for population genetics by Tavaré et al. (1997) and Beaumont, Zhang, and Balding (2002), and has since been applied across biology, ecology, and the physical sciences (Marin et al. 2012).

As the dimension of \(S\) grows, the volume of the acceptance region shrinks exponentially, and the acceptance rate collapses. As \(\varepsilon \to 0\), the approximation improves but the computational cost explodes. With 10 summary statistics, even \(\varepsilon = 0.1\) yields an acceptance rate of roughly \(0.1^{10} = 10^{-10}\), requiring \(10^{10}\) simulations to get a single posterior sample. There is also the question of which summary statistic to use: if \(S\) is not sufficient for \(\theta\), then ABC targets \(\pi(\theta \mid S(y_{\text{obs}}))\), not \(\pi(\theta \mid y_{\text{obs}})\), and information is lost (Fearnhead and Prangle 2012). GBC handles this differently: the summary can be learned as part of training, using a neural network to approximate \(\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y]\), which serves as an approximately sufficient statistic (Fearnhead and Prangle 2012); see Section 4.5 for the formal connection. We develop ABC more formally in Section 10.2.

GBC: From Kernels to Neural Networks.

Generative Bayesian Computation replaces ABC’s local kernel with a global neural network. Both share the same first step — simulate \((\theta_i, y_i)\) pairs from the prior and likelihood — but differ in how they use the simulated data.

ABC uses the simulations locally: for each new observed dataset \(y_{\text{obs}}\), it finds the simulated pairs whose summary statistics are close to \(S(y_{\text{obs}})\) and uses them as approximate posterior samples. This is kernel smoothing: each new query requires scanning the entire simulated database, and the quality depends on how many simulations happen to fall near the observed summary.

GBC uses the simulations globally: it trains a neural network on all simulated pairs to learn the conditional quantile function \(Q_{\theta \mid S}(\tau \mid s)\) over the entire summary-statistic space. Training happens once. At inference time, the network is evaluated at \(S(y_{\text{obs}})\) — a single forward pass, regardless of how many simulated pairs exist.

This distinction has concrete consequences. ABC suffers the curse of dimensionality in the summary statistic space because kernel estimators do. In \(k\) dimensions, a kernel estimator requires \(O(\varepsilon^{-k})\) samples to achieve tolerance \(\varepsilon\), which becomes prohibitive for \(k > 5\) or so. GBC breaks this curse when the underlying function has compositional structure, because deep ReLU networks exploit hierarchy that flat kernels cannot. The result, due to Schmidt-Hieber (2020), is that the minimax rate for a deep network approximating a compositional function depends on the effective dimension at each layer, not the ambient dimension of the input. This is the theoretical basis for GBC’s scalability, and we develop it rigorously in Appendix A.

To make this concrete, let us work through the simplest possible Bayesian problem — the normal-normal conjugate model — and see all three paradigms side by side.

1.1.1 A Mini-Example: Normal-Normal Inference

Suppose we observe \(n\) data points \(y_1, \ldots, y_n\) from a normal model with unknown mean \(\theta\) and known variance \(\sigma^2 = 1\): \[ y_i \mid \theta \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(\theta, 1), \quad i = 1, \ldots, n. \] We place a conjugate normal prior \(\theta \sim N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\) with \(\mu_0 = 0\) and \(\sigma_0^2 = 1\). This is the simplest nontrivial Bayesian model, and it is one of the few where we can work out the answer by hand. We use it here as a calibration device: since we know the correct posterior analytically, we can verify that GBC recovers it. The strategy of testing on problems with known answers before trusting the method on problems without known answers is a recurring theme in this book.

Analytic Solution. The posterior is available in closed form. The prior precision is \(1/\sigma_0^2 = 1\) and the data precision is \(n/\sigma^2 = n\). The posterior precision is their sum, \(n + 1\), and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the data mean: \[ \theta \mid y_{1:n} \sim N\!\left(\frac{n \bar{y}}{n + 1}, \; \frac{1}{n + 1}\right), \tag{1.2}\] where \(\bar{y} = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\). The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean \(\mu_0 = 0\) and the data mean \(\bar{y}\), with weights proportional to their respective precisions. The posterior variance \(1/(n+1)\) shrinks as \(n\) grows, reflecting increasing certainty about \(\theta\). For \(n = 10\) and \(\bar{y} = 2.3\), the posterior mean is \(10 \times 2.3 / 11 = 2.09\), the posterior standard deviation is \(1/\sqrt{11} = 0.302\), and the 95% credible interval is approximately \([1.50, \, 2.68]\).

MCMC (Metropolis–Hastings). Even though we know the answer, let us pretend we do not and run a Metropolis–Hastings random walk. At each iteration \(t\), we propose a new value \(\theta'\) by adding Gaussian noise to the current value: \[ \theta' = \theta^{(t)} + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim N(0, \delta^2), \] for some step size \(\delta\). We then compute the acceptance probability \[ \alpha = \min\!\left(1, \; \frac{p(y \mid \theta') \, \pi(\theta')}{p(y \mid \theta^{(t)}) \, \pi(\theta^{(t)})}\right), \] and set \(\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta'\) with probability \(\alpha\), otherwise \(\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^{(t)}\). After discarding a burn-in of 1,000 iterations and thinning every 10th sample from a chain of length 10,000, we get approximately 900 effectively independent posterior samples.

This works well for a one-dimensional problem. But notice: at every single iteration we had to evaluate the likelihood \(p(y \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n \phi(y_i - \theta)\). For our normal model this is cheap — a few floating-point operations. But for a climate simulator or a CFD code, each “likelihood evaluation” is a full simulation that might take hours. That likelihood-evaluation requirement is precisely what GBC eliminates.

GBC (Implicit Quantile Network). First, simulate training data: for \(i = 1, \ldots, N\) with \(N = 100{,}000\), draw \(\theta_i \sim N(0, 1)\), then \(y_{i,1}, \ldots, y_{i,n} \sim N(\theta_i, 1)\), and compute \(z_i = \bar{y}_i\). We have simulated from the model but never evaluated the likelihood as a function.

Second, train an IQN \(G_\phi(\tau, z)\) on these pairs using the pinball loss. During each training iteration, we sample a batch of pairs \(\{(\theta_i, z_i)\}\) and, for each pair, draw a random quantile level \(\tau_i \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\). The loss for each pair is \(\rho_{\tau_i}(\theta_i - G_\phi(\tau_i, z_i))\), where \(\rho_\tau\) is the pinball loss. We minimize the average loss over the batch using the Adam optimizer. Over many epochs, the network learns to map any summary \(z\) and quantile level \(\tau\) to the corresponding posterior quantile. Training takes a few minutes on a laptop for this simple problem.

Third, for \(\bar{y}_{\text{obs}} = 2.3\), produce posterior samples by drawing \(\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_B \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\) and returning \(\{G_\phi(\tau_b, 2.3)\}_{b=1}^B\). The resulting posterior quantiles match the analytic solution — the median is near \(2.09\), the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles near \(1.50\) and \(2.68\) — but the computation is a single forward pass through a trained network, taking under 0.05 seconds.

More importantly, the same trained network works for any other observed \(\bar{y}\): plug in \(\bar{y} = 0.5\) or \(\bar{y} = -1.7\) and get the corresponding posterior instantly. MCMC would require rerunning the entire chain from scratch for each new dataset. This is what we mean by amortized inference: the cost of training is paid once, and then inference for any number of future datasets is essentially free. For a sensor network with 10,000 sensors, GBC trains once and produces 10,000 posteriors in under a second. MCMC would need 10,000 separate chains.

The normal-normal example is deliberately simple — it is one where MCMC works perfectly and GBC offers no practical advantage. The point is methodological: all three paradigms give the same answer, confirming that GBC targets the correct posterior.

GBC returns something qualitatively different. MCMC gives a collection of correlated posterior samples whose effective sample size is usually much smaller than the nominal count. ABC gives approximate samples (approximate because of the \(\varepsilon\)-tolerance) that target a smoothed version of the posterior. GBC gives the posterior quantile function \(G_\phi(\tau, z)\) as a learned object — a function you can evaluate at any \(\tau \in (0,1)\) for any summary \(z\). From the quantile function, you can produce independent samples, compute any quantile directly, compute credible intervals, and compute posterior expectations via \(E[g(\theta) \mid z] = \int_0^1 g(G_\phi(\tau, z)) \, d\tau\). The quantile function is a richer object than a bag of samples, and this richness is exploited throughout the book, particularly in the decision-theoretic applications of Chapter 12.

The advantages of GBC emerge when the likelihood has no closed form, when amortization over many datasets matters, or when the posterior has complex structure that challenges MCMC mixing.

TipAlgorithm 1.1: Generative Bayesian Computation

Input: Prior \(\pi(\theta)\), simulator \(p(y \mid \theta)\), summary statistic \(S(\cdot)\).

  1. Simulate. Draw \(\theta_i \sim \pi(\theta)\) and \(y_i \sim p(y \mid \theta_i)\) for \(i = 1, \ldots, N\).

  2. Compute features. Set \(z_i = S(y_i)\). If sufficient statistics are unknown, learn \(S\) via a neural network (Jiang et al. 2017).

  3. Train IQN. Learn \(G_\phi(\tau, z) \approx Q_{\theta \mid z}(\tau)\) by minimizing the pinball loss over the simulated pairs \(\{(\theta_i, z_i)\}_{i=1}^N\).

  4. Inference. For observed data \(y_{\text{obs}}\), compute \(z_{\text{obs}} = S(y_{\text{obs}})\), draw \(\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_B \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Uniform}(0,1)\), and return posterior samples \(\{G_\phi(\tau_b, z_{\text{obs}})\}_{b=1}^B\).

TipThe “No MCMC” Thesis

GBC produces the same Bayesian answers as MCMC — posterior quantiles, credible intervals, point estimates, predictive distributions — but via a fundamentally different computation. There are no Markov chains, no convergence diagnostics, and no burn-in. There is no likelihood evaluation at test time. The approach is amortized: train once, then query for any observed dataset in milliseconds. And it scales linearly in sample size, at cost \(O(NLw^2)\) where \(N\) is the number of simulated pairs, \(L\) is the network depth, and \(w\) is the width.

GBC is not anti-Bayesian. It is a different computational strategy for the same inferential target.

1.2 The IQN Architecture: A Preview

The computational engine of GBC is the Implicit Quantile Network (IQN), introduced by Dabney et al. (2018) in the context of distributional reinforcement learning. The IQN was originally designed to learn the distribution of future rewards in an RL agent playing Atari games. We repurpose it for statistical inference, learning the distribution of parameters given data — the posterior. We give a brief preview here; full details are in Chapter 5.

The IQN takes two inputs — a covariate vector \(x\) (or summary statistic \(z\)) and a quantile level \(\tau \in (0,1)\) — and outputs an estimate of the \(\tau\)th conditional quantile \(\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)\). What makes the IQN “implicit” is that \(\tau\) is treated as a continuous input, not a fixed grid. During training, \(\tau\) values are sampled uniformly from \((0,1)\), so the network learns the entire quantile function simultaneously. This is a departure from classical quantile regression, which fits a separate model for each quantile level. If you want the median and a 95% interval using classical quantile regression, you fit three separate models (at \(\tau = 0.025, 0.5, 0.975\)). With an IQN, you train a single model and evaluate it at any \(\tau\) you like — and the quantile-crossing problem that plagues classical quantile regression is largely resolved.

The architecture has three components. First, the scalar \(\tau\) is embedded via a cosine basis \(\varphi(\tau) = (\cos(\pi \tau \cdot 1), \ldots, \cos(\pi \tau \cdot M))\), giving the network a rich representation of the quantile level. Second, the input \(x\) is processed by a standard MLP into a feature vector \(f(x) \in \mathbb{R}^M\) of the same dimension. Third, the features and the quantile embedding are combined via elementwise multiplication \(h = f(x) \odot \varphi(\tau)\) and passed through output layers. The elementwise multiplication creates interactions between covariate features and the quantile level, allowing the network to learn how the shape of the conditional distribution varies with \(x\). Homoscedastic data (constant variance) produces feature vectors that are nearly independent of \(x\); heteroscedastic data produces feature vectors that modulate the quantile embedding differently at different \(x\) values.

Training minimizes the pinball loss (also called the check loss or asymmetric absolute loss): \[ \rho_\tau(u) = u \cdot (\tau - \mathbf{1}_{u < 0}) = \max(\tau u, \; (\tau - 1)u). \] For each training sample \((x_i, y_i)\) and a randomly drawn \(\tau\), the loss penalizes under-prediction at rate \(\tau\) and over-prediction at rate \(1 - \tau\). Minimizing the expected pinball loss yields the true conditional quantile — a classical result from Koenker and Bassett (1978), extended here from linear models to deep networks. In practice, the raw pinball loss is supplemented by a location anchor (penalizing deviation from the conditional mean) and a soft non-crossing penalty (enforcing monotonicity in \(\tau\)) — the three-term composite loss detailed in Section 5.3.

1.3 Five Things GBC Does

The same IQN architecture applies across five application domains. In every case, the posterior or predictive distribution is learned as a quantile function from simulated data, and inference is a forward pass.

Surrogates for computer experiments. Computer simulators — finite element codes, climate models, aerodynamic solvers — are expensive to run. A single evaluation of a CFD code for the NASA LGBB rocket takes hours on a high-performance cluster. The traditional surrogate is the Gaussian process (GP) (Rasmussen and Williams 2006; Santner, Williams, and Notz 2018; Gramacy 2020), but GPs have \(O(n^3)\) scaling that makes them infeasible for large datasets, and their Gaussian predictive distribution is restrictive for heteroscedastic or multimodal outputs. GBC replaces the GP with an IQN that scales linearly in \(n\), handles heteroscedasticity and discontinuities natively, and improves CRPS by 11–46% on the BGP benchmark suite. For smooth surfaces in low dimensions (\(n < 2{,}000\), \(d \leq 4\)), GPs retain a slight edge because their strong inductive bias is correct for these problems. But GBC scales comfortably to \(n = 90{,}000\) where dense-covariance GPs are infeasible. For surfaces with sharp discontinuities, the Aug-IQN architecture — an augmented IQN with boundary detection — produces sharp, well-calibrated predictive distributions on both sides of the boundary, while the GP’s stationarity assumption causes it to smooth across. On the Phantom benchmark, Aug-IQN achieves RMSE 0.044 versus the mixed jump GP’s 0.053. Details in Chapter 7 and Chapter 8.

Bayesian inference. GBC learns the posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid y)\) from simulated (parameter, data) pairs, turning Bayesian inference into a supervised learning problem. The framework handles both parametric models where the likelihood is expensive to evaluate and simulator-based models where no likelihood exists at all. The learned map \(G_\phi(\tau, z)\) takes a quantile level and data summary and returns the posterior quantile. Drawing \(\tau\) uniformly produces posterior samples; evaluating at \(\tau = 0.5\) gives the posterior median; evaluating at \(\tau = 0.025\) and \(\tau = 0.975\) gives a 95% credible interval. The quantile function captures multimodal posteriors automatically: different \(\tau\) values map to different modes, and the monotonicity of the quantile function guarantees that it cannot miss either one. When the posterior has two well-separated modes, a random-walk Metropolis chain can get trapped in one mode for very long periods, producing a biased sample that misrepresents the posterior. Running multiple chains from different starting points helps but requires careful initialization. The trained IQN, by contrast, captures both modes automatically: low \(\tau\) values map to one mode and high \(\tau\) values to the other, with intermediate \(\tau\) capturing the valley between them. This is a consequence of the quantile function’s monotonicity: it must pass through both modes on its way from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\), so it cannot miss either one. Full development in Chapter 10.

Prediction. Given observations \(Z_{1:n} = (X_1, Y_1), \ldots, (X_n, Y_n)\), predict \(Y_{n+1}\) at covariates \(x_{n+1}\). GBC learns the predictive quantile function directly: \[ Y_{n+1} = Q(\tau, x_{n+1}, S(Z_{1:n})), \quad \tau \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1), \] bypassing explicit computation of the prior, likelihood, and the marginalizing integral. The summary statistic \(S(Z_{1:n})\) compresses the training data into a fixed-dimensional vector that captures the relevant information for prediction. The de Finetti–Ressel representation theorem (Ressel 1985) provides the theoretical justification: for exchangeable data, there exists an implicit prior and likelihood such that the predictive distribution is a proper Bayesian predictive. The Bayesian validity comes from the exchangeability assumption and the representation theorem, not from a specific choice of prior.

The predictive quantile function directly gives prediction intervals: for any coverage level \(1 - \alpha\), the interval \([Q(\alpha/2, x_{n+1}, S), \; Q(1 - \alpha/2, x_{n+1}, S)]\) is an approximate \((1-\alpha)\) prediction interval. Unlike conformal prediction intervals, which guarantee only marginal coverage, the GBC intervals aim for conditional coverage (valid for each specific \(x_{n+1}\)). See Chapter 11.

Maximum expected utility. Decision theory is the natural companion to Bayesian inference. Having computed the posterior, we want to use it to make optimal decisions. The foundational principle, due to Savage (1954): choose the action \(a\) that maximizes expected utility \(E[U(a, Y) \mid y_{\text{obs}}]\), where the expectation is over the posterior predictive distribution. The classical approach requires integrating the utility function over the predictive distribution — a double integral (over parameters and outcomes) that is often intractable. Even with MCMC, computing expected utility requires nested Monte Carlo that introduces variance and computational cost. GBC sidesteps this via a quantile identity. For any random variable \(X\) with quantile function \(Q_X(\tau)\): \[ E[X] = \int_0^1 Q_X(\tau) \, d\tau. \tag{1.3}\] For monotone utility \(U\), \(E[U(X)] = \int_0^1 U(Q_X(\tau)) \, d\tau\). Since the IQN directly produces quantile functions, expected utility is a one-dimensional integral over \(\tau\) — no density estimation, no posterior sampling, no nested Monte Carlo. The integral is computed by simple numerical quadrature (trapezoidal rule or Gauss-Legendre) on a grid of \(\tau\) values, costing negligible computation compared to the MCMC-based alternative. This reflects a connection between quantile-based inference and decision theory that traces back to Yaari’s dual theory of choice under risk (Yaari 1987). Wang’s distortion operator (Wang 2000) formalizes the Bayesian updating step in this quantile framework: the posterior quantile function is obtained by composing the prior quantile function with a learned distortion, exactly the operation the IQN performs. See Chapter 12.

Causal inference. Estimating treatment effects from observational data requires controlling for confounders — the fundamental problem being that we never observe both potential outcomes for the same individual (Holland 1986). Under unconfoundedness, the conditional average treatment effect \(\tau(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]\) can be identified from observational data, but estimating it well requires simultaneously learning the propensity score \(e(x) = P(Z = 1 \mid X = x)\), the conditional outcome distribution \(P(Y \mid X, Z)\), and the treatment effect itself. GBC extends naturally to this setting: a propensity network estimates \(e(x)\) via binary cross-entropy, and an IQN learns the conditional quantile function of outcomes using a composite loss (pinball + MAE anchor) with the estimated propensity as an additional covariate. Because the IQN produces the entire conditional quantile function of outcomes, we can compute not just the average treatment effect but quantile treatment effects (QTEs) — how does the treatment affect the 10th percentile of outcomes? The 90th percentile? These reveal heterogeneity invisible to mean-based estimators. On the LaLonde job-training benchmark, GBC detects that individuals in the top quartile of predicted benefit gain roughly three times more from the program than those in the bottom quartile — a finding with direct policy implications for targeting interventions. See Chapter 13.

1.4 GBC versus Classical Bayesian Computation

How does this book relate to the classical Bayesian computational toolbox — MCMC, variational inference, expectation propagation, and Gaussian process emulation (see, e.g., Gelman et al. 2013; Robert and Casella 2004; Brooks et al. 2011)? The relationship is complementary, not adversarial. Classical methods and GBC share the same inferential goals — posterior distributions, predictive distributions, and decision analysis — but differ in their computational strategy. Classical Bayesian computation shines when the model is explicit — when you can write down \(p(y \mid \theta)\) and \(\pi(\theta)\) and compute the posterior via MCMC or analytical approximation. The standard workflow is well-established: specify the model, code it in Stan or PyMC, run MCMC, check convergence, interpret the posterior. GBC shines when the model is implicit — when you have a forward simulator but no closed-form likelihood. The GBC workflow is different: specify the prior, write a simulator, generate training pairs, train an IQN, and query the trained network. In the overlap region where both apply — models with explicit likelihoods and moderate dimension — they should give the same answers. We verify this explicitly in Chapter 10 on the normal-normal conjugate model and a bimodal posterior. The choice between them is then a matter of computational efficiency and workflow: MCMC for single-dataset analyses where convergence is fast, GBC for multi-dataset deployments where amortization pays off.

TipClassical Bayes vs GBC: What You Need

Classical Bayesian computation assumes you can write down the likelihood \(p(y \mid \theta)\) and evaluate it pointwise. This is essential for MCMC, which proposes parameter values and accepts or rejects based on the likelihood ratio.

GBC assumes you can simulate from the likelihood. You need a forward model that generates synthetic data given parameters, but you never need to evaluate \(p(y \mid \theta)\) as a function. Simulation is strictly weaker than evaluation — and it is often all you have.

When should you use MCMC, and when should you use GBC?

MCMC is better when the model is low-dimensional (say \(d \leq 5\)) with a well-behaved posterior, you need detailed diagnostics such as effective sample size and divergent transitions, and you have a single dataset with a closed-form likelihood. If you have no need for amortization and the cost of running a single chain is modest, MCMC lets you exploit conjugacy or gradient information via HMC.

GBC is better when the likelihood has no closed form — as in simulator-based and agent-based models — or when you need amortized inference across many datasets. If a lab needs to fit the same model to data from 5,000 sensors, MCMC requires 5,000 separate chains (at 30 seconds each, over 40 hours), while GBC trains once (say, 2 hours) and then runs 5,000 forward passes in about 4 minutes total. GBC is also the right choice when the model involves high-dimensional data demanding linear scaling, or when decision-making requires expected utilities expressed through quantile functions.

There is no universal winner. GBC is not a replacement for careful model building — you still need to choose the right prior and simulator. It is not a black box that eliminates the need for statistical thinking — you still need to choose summary statistics, check calibration, and validate results. And it is not a magic bullet for high-dimensional parameter spaces — the number of training points needed for accurate posterior estimation grows with the dimension of \(\theta\), just as it does for any nonparametric method.

What GBC is is a computational framework that replaces MCMC’s Markov chain with a trained neural network, ABC’s local kernel with a global quantile function, and variational inference’s parametric family with a nonparametric quantile representation. Variational methods (Blei, Kucukelbir, and McAuliffe 2017) approximate the posterior by optimizing over a tractable family \(q(\theta)\), but they require evaluating the likelihood (or its gradient) at every optimization step, and the posterior is constrained to the variational family (typically a factorized Gaussian), which may miss multimodality and complex correlation structure. GBC avoids both limitations: it does not evaluate the likelihood, and the quantile function representation is nonparametric — it can represent any continuous distribution, not just members of a specified parametric family. Where variational inference approximates \(\pi(\theta \mid y)\) with a tractable distribution, GBC approximates the quantile function \(Q_{\theta \mid y}(\tau)\) with a neural network, and the approximation error is controlled by the network’s capacity, not by the expressiveness of a parametric family. Normalizing flows (Rezende and Mohamed 2015) address some of variational inference’s expressiveness limitations by using invertible neural networks, but they still require likelihood evaluation. GBC requires only simulation.

1.5 Historical Note

The ideas behind GBC have roots in three distinct intellectual traditions. Tracing these lineages helps explain why the framework arrived when it did and not earlier.

Parzen’s quantile philosophy. Emanuel Parzen argued throughout his career that quantile functions — not densities — are the natural language of statistics. In his influential 1979 paper and later work (Parzen 2004), Parzen developed the “quantile approach”: every distributional question should be formulated in terms of \(Q(\tau) = F^{-1}(\tau)\), and every statistical operation should be expressed as a transformation of quantile functions. The compositional identity \(Q_{g(Y)}(\tau) = g(Q_Y(\tau))\) for monotone \(g\) connects quantile functions to deep learning, where function composition is the defining principle. Parzen’s “comparison distribution” \(D(\tau, x)\) that decomposes conditional quantiles as \(Q_{Y|X}(\tau \mid x) = Q_Y(D(\tau, x))\) anticipates Wang’s distortion operator and the IQN’s learned transport map. The comparison distribution tells us how conditioning on \(X = x\) distorts the unconditional quantile function — it is a learned warping of the probability scale, and it is the natural representation of Bayesian updating when you think in quantiles rather than densities. Around the same time, Koenker and Bassett (1978) developed linear quantile regression, showing that the \(\tau\)th conditional quantile can be estimated by minimizing the asymmetric absolute loss (the pinball loss). This gave Parzen’s quantile philosophy a concrete computational method, one that we extend from linear models to deep networks throughout this book.

Rubin’s simulation principle. Rubin (1984) showed that Bayesian inference can be understood as a simulation exercise. Consider the joint distribution of \((\theta, y)\) where \(\theta \sim \pi(\theta)\) and \(y \mid \theta \sim p(y \mid \theta)\). The conditional distribution of \(\theta\) given \(y = y_{\text{obs}}\) is, by definition, the posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid y_{\text{obs}})\). So if we simulate many pairs \((\theta_i, y_i)\) from the joint and keep only those where \(y_i \approx y_{\text{obs}}\), we obtain posterior samples. This insight — that simulation is sufficient for Bayesian inference — is the foundational principle of both ABC and GBC. The difference is in how “matching” is defined: ABC uses a local kernel around the observed summary statistics, while GBC learns a global quantile transport map that works for all possible observed datasets simultaneously. Rubin’s paper was primarily about the interpretation of frequentist procedures in a Bayesian framework, and the computational implications were not its main focus. The computational exploitation of Rubin’s insight had to wait for the development of ABC in the late 1990s (Tavaré et al. 1997; Beaumont, Zhang, and Balding 2002) and for GBC in the 2020s.

The IQN from distributional RL. In 2017, Bellemare, Dabney, and Munos (2017) introduced distributional RL, learning the distribution of future returns in an RL agent, not just the expected return. This was a departure from classical RL, where the value function \(V(s) = E[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t \mid s]\) is a scalar. Distributional RL replaces \(V(s)\) with a random variable \(Z(s)\) whose distribution captures the full uncertainty about future outcomes. The motivation was practical — risk-sensitive control requires knowing the full distribution of returns, not just the mean — but the mathematical framework turned out to be broadly useful. Dabney et al. (2017) showed that the 1-Wasserstein distance between distributions is minimized by quantile regression, providing the theoretical link between distributional RL and quantile estimation.

Dabney et al. (2018) then proposed the IQN — cosine embedding of \(\tau\), elementwise combination with state features, training via the pinball loss — designed for Atari games, but it is precisely the architecture GBC needs for posterior quantile estimation. The connection is not accidental: both problems involve learning a conditional distribution from simulated experience. An Atari agent simulates many game trajectories and learns the distribution of returns conditional on state; a Bayesian analyst simulates many (parameter, data) pairs and learns the posterior conditional on data summaries. The mathematical structure is identical, and the IQN handles both.

The synthesis arrived in Polson, Ruggeri, and Sokolov (2024), which recognized that Parzen’s quantile philosophy, Rubin’s simulation principle, and Dabney’s IQN architecture could be woven into a unified framework for Bayesian inference. The noise outsourcing theorem (Kallenberg 1997) provides the theoretical guarantee that the conditional quantile function exists and is the right object to learn. The universal approximation theorem for deep ReLU networks (Schmidt-Hieber 2020) guarantees that the IQN can learn it. And the pinball loss provides a proper scoring rule for training.

GBC is thus not a single invention but a convergence of ideas from statistics, probability, and machine learning — each necessary, none sufficient alone. Parzen gave us the right mathematical objects (quantile functions). Rubin gave us the right computational principle (simulation is sufficient for Bayesian inference). Koenker gave us the right loss function (the pinball loss). Dabney gave us the right architecture (the IQN with cosine embedding). And Kallenberg gave us the right theorem (noise outsourcing). This book assembles these pieces into a coherent framework and develops the theory, algorithms, and applications that make it practical.

1.7 A Note on Style

This book follows the computational statistics tradition exemplified by Gramacy (2020): we develop theory alongside working code, we are honest about when methods fail, and we believe that understanding comes from running experiments, not just reading proofs. Bobby Gramacy’s Surrogates set a standard for how a technical monograph can be simultaneously rigorous and readable — conversational in tone, first-person throughout, with working code integrated into every chapter. Every claim is backed by a reproducible example. Failures are discussed as openly as successes.

We have tried to follow that model, adapted to the GBC framework and Python/PyTorch instead of R. When GBC works well, we give the numbers — CRPS, RMSE, coverage, wall-clock time. When it fails — as on high signal-to-noise data where the multiplicative \(h \odot \tau_h\) architecture conflates signal and noise, a problem we confront honestly in Chapter 14 — we say that too, and show what to do about it. We believe that a method paper that reports only successes is less useful than one that also maps the boundaries of applicability.

All code is in Python using PyTorch (Paszke et al. 2019). The gbc package provides modular implementations of every method; see Appendix B for the full module inventory and setup instructions.

We adopt Parzen’s philosophy that quantiles are the natural language of statistical modeling (Parzen 2004). Densities, likelihoods, and integrals are replaced wherever possible by quantile functions, pinball losses, and function composition. When you think of a distribution as a density, Bayesian updating is an integral — and computation requires evaluating that integral (MCMC, variational inference). When you think of a distribution as a quantile function, Bayesian updating is a composition of functions (the Wang distortion) — and computation requires learning that composition (the IQN). The density perspective privileges analytical tractability: models with conjugate priors, exponential family likelihoods, and closed-form marginals are the easiest to work with. The quantile perspective privileges computational tractability: models where you can simulate (parameter, data) pairs are the easiest to work with, regardless of whether the likelihood has a closed form. Both perspectives are valid. This book is written from the quantile side, and we hope to convince the reader that this perspective is not just viable but often preferable.

# Why Generative Bayesian Computation? {#sec-introduction}

> "*All models are wrong, but some are useful.*" -- George Box

Bayesian statistics provides a coherent framework for learning from data under uncertainty. You specify a prior $\pi(\theta)$ and a likelihood $p(y \mid \theta)$, and Bayes' theorem delivers the posterior $\pi(\theta \mid y)$. The open question is *computation*: how do we actually get our hands on the posterior?

For the past three decades, the dominant answer has been Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC). You construct a Markov chain whose stationary distribution is the posterior, run it for a very long time, and hope that the samples you collect are representative. The Metropolis algorithm dates to 1953 [@metropolis1953equation], but MCMC became a practical tool for Bayesian inference only after @gelfand1990sampling demonstrated its power on standard statistical models. Since then, MCMC has transformed applied statistics, from Gibbs sampling through Hamiltonian Monte Carlo and NUTS that power modern probabilistic programming.

But MCMC comes with a cost: convergence diagnostics, mixing failures in high dimensions, and the need to rerun the chain from scratch for every new dataset. In a world where models are growing more complex, datasets are growing larger, and inference must happen in real time, these bottlenecks become increasingly binding.

This book develops an alternative. **Generative Bayesian Computation (GBC)** replaces MCMC with a neural network trained by stochastic gradient descent. The idea: simulate a large collection of (parameter, data) pairs from the prior and likelihood, then train an implicit quantile network (IQN) to learn the map from data to posterior quantiles. Once trained, the network produces posterior samples for *any* observed dataset via a single forward pass --- no chains, no burn-in, no convergence diagnostics. The posterior quantile function is the fundamental object: it encodes everything we need to know about the posterior distribution, and it can be learned from simulated data using standard supervised learning techniques.

The theoretical foundation is the **noise outsourcing theorem** [@kallenberg1997foundations], which guarantees that any conditional distribution $Y \mid X$ can be represented as $Y = G^*(U, X)$ where $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ and $G^*(\tau, x) = Q_{Y|X}(\tau \mid x)$ is the conditional quantile function. The computational engine is the **Implicit Quantile Network** (IQN), borrowed from distributional reinforcement learning [@dabney2018implicit] and repurposed for statistical inference. The IQN takes $\tau \in (0,1)$ and the input $x$ and produces the conditional quantile $\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)$, trained using the pinball loss on simulated data.

Consider a concrete example that will recur throughout this book. NASA's Langley Glide-Back Booster (LGBB) is a reusable rocket concept whose aerodynamic drag coefficient depends on Mach number, angle of attack, and sideslip angle. Engineers run a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulator to predict drag, but each simulation takes hours on a cluster. MCMC fails here because the CFD simulator is a black box --- there is no closed-form likelihood to evaluate. GBC takes a different route: simulate thousands of (input, drag) pairs, train an IQN on these pairs, and produce posterior quantiles for any new observation via a single forward pass. We introduce this example in @sec-datasets, build a surrogate in @sec-surrogates, and develop the active learning strategy in @sec-active-learning, where GBC achieves an RMSE of 0.00723, a factor of $2.95\times$ better than deep Gaussian processes.


## Three Paradigms for Bayesian Computation {#sec-intro-paradigms}

There are three main strategies for turning the abstract formula
$$
\pi(\theta \mid y) \propto p(y \mid \theta) \, \pi(\theta)
$$ {#eq-intro-bayes}
into a usable computational object. Each makes a different trade-off between generality, accuracy, and computational cost. We describe each in turn, then work through the normal-normal conjugate model to see all three in action.

*MCMC: The Workhorse.*

Markov chain Monte Carlo constructs a Markov chain $\theta^{(1)}, \theta^{(2)}, \ldots$ whose stationary distribution is the posterior $\pi(\theta \mid y)$. After a sufficient burn-in period, the remaining samples approximate the posterior, and expectations can be estimated by sample averages: $E[g(\theta) \mid y] \approx T^{-1} \sum_{t=1}^T g(\theta^{(t)})$. Standard textbook treatments [e.g., @gelman2013bayesian; @robert2004monte] provide comprehensive coverage, and probabilistic programming languages --- Stan [@carpenter2017stan], PyMC, NumPyro --- have made MCMC accessible across the sciences.

But MCMC has structural limitations that become increasingly apparent as problems grow in scale and complexity.

First, there is no general-purpose criterion that guarantees a chain has converged. We run multiple chains, compute $\hat{R}$ statistics, and inspect trace plots --- all heuristic. The split-$\hat{R}$ diagnostic of @vehtari2021rank is the current state of the art, but it provides only a necessary condition for convergence, not a sufficient one. A chain can pass all diagnostics and still have failed to explore the full posterior.

Second, posterior geometry becomes treacherous as dimension grows. Multimodal posteriors require the chain to jump between modes, which happens exponentially rarely for random-walk proposals. Funnel geometries --- common in hierarchical models --- cause the chain to get stuck in the narrow end. These are not hypothetical concerns; they arise routinely in applied Bayesian work.

Third, each observed dataset requires a fresh MCMC run. Fitting the same model to 10,000 sensors means 10,000 separate chains, each costing $O(Tn)$ where $T$ is the chain length and $n$ is the sample size. There is no way to reuse the work done for one dataset when analyzing the next.

Finally, at every iteration MCMC must evaluate the likelihood $p(y \mid \theta)$. For simulator-based models --- agent-based models, climate simulators, cosmological codes --- the likelihood has no closed form, and standard MCMC simply does not apply. The model can generate data given parameters, but it cannot evaluate the probability of observed data given parameters. This is the situation GBC was designed for.

*ABC: The First Likelihood-Free Method.*

Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) was the first systematic attempt to perform Bayesian inference without evaluating the likelihood. The conceptual idea, articulated by @rubin1984bayesianly, is to recast Bayesian inference as a pure simulation exercise: draw $\theta$ from the prior, simulate $y^{\text{sim}}$ from the model, and accept $\theta$ whenever $y^{\text{sim}}$ is "close" to the observed data $y_{\text{obs}}$. If the simulator faithfully represents reality, then parameters that produce data similar to what we observed are the ones that are plausible given the data --- which is exactly what the posterior captures.

In its simplest form (rejection ABC), one draws $\theta$ from the prior, simulates $y^{\text{sim}} \mid \theta$, and accepts $\theta$ if $y^{\text{sim}} = y_{\text{obs}}$. For continuous data this acceptance probability is zero, so in practice one compares low-dimensional summary statistics $S(y^{\text{sim}})$ and $S(y_{\text{obs}})$, accepting when $\|S(y^{\text{sim}}) - S(y_{\text{obs}})\| < \varepsilon$ for some tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$. The resulting samples approximate $\pi(\theta \mid S(y_{\text{obs}}))$ --- the posterior conditioned on the summary statistic, not the full data. More sophisticated variants --- MCMC-ABC, sequential Monte Carlo ABC (SMC-ABC), and regression-adjusted ABC --- improve efficiency but share the same fundamental structure. The method was formalized for population genetics by @tavare1997inferring and @beaumont2002approximate, and has since been applied across biology, ecology, and the physical sciences [@marin2012approximate].

As the dimension of $S$ grows, the volume of the acceptance region shrinks exponentially, and the acceptance rate collapses. As $\varepsilon \to 0$, the approximation improves but the computational cost explodes. With 10 summary statistics, even $\varepsilon = 0.1$ yields an acceptance rate of roughly $0.1^{10} = 10^{-10}$, requiring $10^{10}$ simulations to get a single posterior sample. There is also the question of *which* summary statistic to use: if $S$ is not sufficient for $\theta$, then ABC targets $\pi(\theta \mid S(y_{\text{obs}}))$, not $\pi(\theta \mid y_{\text{obs}})$, and information is lost [@fearnhead2012constructing]. GBC handles this differently: the summary can be learned as part of training, using a neural network to approximate $\mathbb{E}[\theta \mid y]$, which serves as an approximately sufficient statistic [@fearnhead2012constructing]; see @sec-no-kolmogorov for the formal connection. We develop ABC more formally in @sec-bi-abc.

*GBC: From Kernels to Neural Networks.*

Generative Bayesian Computation replaces ABC's local kernel with a global neural network. Both share the same first step --- simulate $(\theta_i, y_i)$ pairs from the prior and likelihood --- but differ in how they use the simulated data.

*ABC* uses the simulations locally: for each new observed dataset $y_{\text{obs}}$, it finds the simulated pairs whose summary statistics are close to $S(y_{\text{obs}})$ and uses them as approximate posterior samples. This is kernel smoothing: each new query requires scanning the entire simulated database, and the quality depends on how many simulations happen to fall near the observed summary.

*GBC* uses the simulations globally: it trains a neural network on *all* simulated pairs to learn the conditional quantile function $Q_{\theta \mid S}(\tau \mid s)$ over the entire summary-statistic space. Training happens once. At inference time, the network is evaluated at $S(y_{\text{obs}})$ --- a single forward pass, regardless of how many simulated pairs exist.

This distinction has concrete consequences. ABC suffers the curse of dimensionality in the summary statistic space because kernel estimators do. In $k$ dimensions, a kernel estimator requires $O(\varepsilon^{-k})$ samples to achieve tolerance $\varepsilon$, which becomes prohibitive for $k > 5$ or so. GBC breaks this curse when the underlying function has compositional structure, because deep ReLU networks exploit hierarchy that flat kernels cannot. The result, due to @schmidt-hieber2020nonparametric, is that the minimax rate for a deep network approximating a compositional function depends on the *effective* dimension at each layer, not the ambient dimension of the input. This is the theoretical basis for GBC's scalability, and we develop it rigorously in @sec-proofs.

To make this concrete, let us work through the simplest possible Bayesian problem --- the normal-normal conjugate model --- and see all three paradigms side by side.

### A Mini-Example: Normal-Normal Inference {#sec-intro-normal-normal}

Suppose we observe $n$ data points $y_1, \ldots, y_n$ from a normal model with unknown mean $\theta$ and known variance $\sigma^2 = 1$:
$$
y_i \mid \theta \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(\theta, 1), \quad i = 1, \ldots, n.
$$
We place a conjugate normal prior $\theta \sim N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)$ with $\mu_0 = 0$ and $\sigma_0^2 = 1$. This is the simplest nontrivial Bayesian model, and it is one of the few where we can work out the answer by hand. We use it here as a calibration device: since we know the correct posterior analytically, we can verify that GBC recovers it. The strategy of testing on problems with known answers before trusting the method on problems without known answers is a recurring theme in this book.

*Analytic Solution.* The posterior is available in closed form. The prior precision is $1/\sigma_0^2 = 1$ and the data precision is $n/\sigma^2 = n$. The posterior precision is their sum, $n + 1$, and the posterior mean is the precision-weighted average of the prior mean and the data mean:
$$
\theta \mid y_{1:n} \sim N\!\left(\frac{n \bar{y}}{n + 1}, \; \frac{1}{n + 1}\right),
$$ {#eq-intro-conjugate}
where $\bar{y} = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i$. The posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean $\mu_0 = 0$ and the data mean $\bar{y}$, with weights proportional to their respective precisions. The posterior variance $1/(n+1)$ shrinks as $n$ grows, reflecting increasing certainty about $\theta$. For $n = 10$ and $\bar{y} = 2.3$, the posterior mean is $10 \times 2.3 / 11 = 2.09$, the posterior standard deviation is $1/\sqrt{11} = 0.302$, and the 95% credible interval is approximately $[1.50, \, 2.68]$.

*MCMC (Metropolis--Hastings).* Even though we know the answer, let us pretend we do not and run a Metropolis--Hastings random walk. At each iteration $t$, we propose a new value $\theta'$ by adding Gaussian noise to the current value:
$$
\theta' = \theta^{(t)} + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim N(0, \delta^2),
$$
for some step size $\delta$. We then compute the acceptance probability
$$
\alpha = \min\!\left(1, \; \frac{p(y \mid \theta') \, \pi(\theta')}{p(y \mid \theta^{(t)}) \, \pi(\theta^{(t)})}\right),
$$
and set $\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta'$ with probability $\alpha$, otherwise $\theta^{(t+1)} = \theta^{(t)}$. After discarding a burn-in of 1,000 iterations and thinning every 10th sample from a chain of length 10,000, we get approximately 900 effectively independent posterior samples.

This works well for a one-dimensional problem. But notice: at every single iteration we had to evaluate the likelihood $p(y \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n \phi(y_i - \theta)$. For our normal model this is cheap --- a few floating-point operations. But for a climate simulator or a CFD code, each "likelihood evaluation" is a full simulation that might take hours. That likelihood-evaluation requirement is precisely what GBC eliminates.

*GBC (Implicit Quantile Network).* First, simulate training data: for $i = 1, \ldots, N$ with $N = 100{,}000$, draw $\theta_i \sim N(0, 1)$, then $y_{i,1}, \ldots, y_{i,n} \sim N(\theta_i, 1)$, and compute $z_i = \bar{y}_i$. We have simulated from the model but never *evaluated* the likelihood as a function.

Second, train an IQN $G_\phi(\tau, z)$ on these pairs using the pinball loss. During each training iteration, we sample a batch of pairs $\{(\theta_i, z_i)\}$ and, for each pair, draw a random quantile level $\tau_i \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$. The loss for each pair is $\rho_{\tau_i}(\theta_i - G_\phi(\tau_i, z_i))$, where $\rho_\tau$ is the pinball loss. We minimize the average loss over the batch using the Adam optimizer. Over many epochs, the network learns to map any summary $z$ and quantile level $\tau$ to the corresponding posterior quantile. Training takes a few minutes on a laptop for this simple problem.

Third, for $\bar{y}_{\text{obs}} = 2.3$, produce posterior samples by drawing $\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_B \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ and returning $\{G_\phi(\tau_b, 2.3)\}_{b=1}^B$. The resulting posterior quantiles match the analytic solution --- the median is near $2.09$, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles near $1.50$ and $2.68$ --- but the computation is a single forward pass through a trained network, taking under 0.05 seconds.

More importantly, the *same trained network* works for any other observed $\bar{y}$: plug in $\bar{y} = 0.5$ or $\bar{y} = -1.7$ and get the corresponding posterior instantly. MCMC would require rerunning the entire chain from scratch for each new dataset. This is what we mean by *amortized inference*: the cost of training is paid once, and then inference for any number of future datasets is essentially free. For a sensor network with 10,000 sensors, GBC trains once and produces 10,000 posteriors in under a second. MCMC would need 10,000 separate chains.

The normal-normal example is deliberately simple --- it is one where MCMC works perfectly and GBC offers no practical advantage. The point is methodological: all three paradigms give the same answer, confirming that GBC targets the correct posterior.

GBC *returns* something qualitatively different. MCMC gives a collection of correlated posterior samples whose effective sample size is usually much smaller than the nominal count. ABC gives approximate samples (approximate because of the $\varepsilon$-tolerance) that target a smoothed version of the posterior. GBC gives the posterior *quantile function* $G_\phi(\tau, z)$ as a learned object --- a function you can evaluate at any $\tau \in (0,1)$ for any summary $z$. From the quantile function, you can produce independent samples, compute any quantile directly, compute credible intervals, and compute posterior expectations via $E[g(\theta) \mid z] = \int_0^1 g(G_\phi(\tau, z)) \, d\tau$. The quantile function is a richer object than a bag of samples, and this richness is exploited throughout the book, particularly in the decision-theoretic applications of @sec-meu.

The advantages of GBC emerge when the likelihood has no closed form, when amortization over many datasets matters, or when the posterior has complex structure that challenges MCMC mixing.

::: {.callout-tip appearance="simple" title="Algorithm 1.1: Generative Bayesian Computation"}
*Input:* Prior $\pi(\theta)$, simulator $p(y \mid \theta)$, summary statistic $S(\cdot)$.

1. *Simulate.* Draw $\theta_i \sim \pi(\theta)$ and $y_i \sim p(y \mid \theta_i)$ for $i = 1, \ldots, N$.

2. *Compute features.* Set $z_i = S(y_i)$. If sufficient statistics are unknown, learn $S$ via a neural network [@jiang2017learning].

3. *Train IQN.* Learn $G_\phi(\tau, z) \approx Q_{\theta \mid z}(\tau)$ by minimizing the pinball loss over the simulated pairs $\{(\theta_i, z_i)\}_{i=1}^N$.

4. *Inference.* For observed data $y_{\text{obs}}$, compute $z_{\text{obs}} = S(y_{\text{obs}})$, draw $\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_B \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \text{Uniform}(0,1)$, and return posterior samples $\{G_\phi(\tau_b, z_{\text{obs}})\}_{b=1}^B$.
:::

::: {.callout-tip appearance="simple" title="The \"No MCMC\" Thesis"}
GBC produces the *same Bayesian answers* as MCMC --- posterior quantiles, credible intervals, point estimates, predictive distributions --- but via a fundamentally different computation. There are no Markov chains, no convergence diagnostics, and no burn-in. There is no likelihood evaluation at test time. The approach is amortized: train once, then query for any observed dataset in milliseconds. And it scales linearly in sample size, at cost $O(NLw^2)$ where $N$ is the number of simulated pairs, $L$ is the network depth, and $w$ is the width.

GBC is not anti-Bayesian. It is a different *computational strategy* for the same inferential target.

## The IQN Architecture: A Preview {#sec-intro-iqn-preview}

The computational engine of GBC is the **Implicit Quantile Network** (IQN), introduced by @dabney2018implicit in the context of distributional reinforcement learning. The IQN was originally designed to learn the distribution of future rewards in an RL agent playing Atari games. We repurpose it for statistical inference, learning the distribution of parameters given data --- the posterior. We give a brief preview here; full details are in @sec-iqn.

The IQN takes two inputs --- a covariate vector $x$ (or summary statistic $z$) and a quantile level $\tau \in (0,1)$ --- and outputs an estimate of the $\tau$th conditional quantile $\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)$. What makes the IQN "implicit" is that $\tau$ is treated as a continuous input, not a fixed grid. During training, $\tau$ values are sampled uniformly from $(0,1)$, so the network learns the *entire* quantile function simultaneously. This is a departure from classical quantile regression, which fits a separate model for each quantile level. If you want the median and a 95% interval using classical quantile regression, you fit three separate models (at $\tau = 0.025, 0.5, 0.975$). With an IQN, you train a single model and evaluate it at any $\tau$ you like --- and the quantile-crossing problem that plagues classical quantile regression is largely resolved.

The architecture has three components. First, the scalar $\tau$ is embedded via a cosine basis $\varphi(\tau) = (\cos(\pi \tau \cdot 1), \ldots, \cos(\pi \tau \cdot M))$, giving the network a rich representation of the quantile level. Second, the input $x$ is processed by a standard MLP into a feature vector $f(x) \in \mathbb{R}^M$ of the same dimension. Third, the features and the quantile embedding are combined via elementwise multiplication $h = f(x) \odot \varphi(\tau)$ and passed through output layers. The elementwise multiplication creates interactions between covariate features and the quantile level, allowing the network to learn how the shape of the conditional distribution varies with $x$. Homoscedastic data (constant variance) produces feature vectors that are nearly independent of $x$; heteroscedastic data produces feature vectors that modulate the quantile embedding differently at different $x$ values.

Training minimizes the pinball loss (also called the check loss or asymmetric absolute loss):
$$
\rho_\tau(u) = u \cdot (\tau - \mathbf{1}_{u < 0}) = \max(\tau u, \; (\tau - 1)u).
$$
For each training sample $(x_i, y_i)$ and a randomly drawn $\tau$, the loss penalizes under-prediction at rate $\tau$ and over-prediction at rate $1 - \tau$. Minimizing the expected pinball loss yields the true conditional quantile --- a classical result from @koenker1978regression, extended here from linear models to deep networks. In practice, the raw pinball loss is supplemented by a location anchor (penalizing deviation from the conditional mean) and a soft non-crossing penalty (enforcing monotonicity in $\tau$) --- the three-term composite loss detailed in @sec-iqn-loss.



## Five Things GBC Does {#sec-intro-five-applications}

The same IQN architecture applies across five application domains. In every case, the posterior or predictive distribution is learned as a quantile function from simulated data, and inference is a forward pass.

*Surrogates for computer experiments.* Computer simulators --- finite element codes, climate models, aerodynamic solvers --- are expensive to run. A single evaluation of a CFD code for the NASA LGBB rocket takes hours on a high-performance cluster. The traditional surrogate is the Gaussian process (GP) [@rasmussen2006gaussian; @santner2018design; @gramacy2020surrogates], but GPs have $O(n^3)$ scaling that makes them infeasible for large datasets, and their Gaussian predictive distribution is restrictive for heteroscedastic or multimodal outputs. GBC replaces the GP with an IQN that scales linearly in $n$, handles heteroscedasticity and discontinuities natively, and improves CRPS by 11--46% on the BGP benchmark suite. For smooth surfaces in low dimensions ($n < 2{,}000$, $d \leq 4$), GPs retain a slight edge because their strong inductive bias is correct for these problems. But GBC scales comfortably to $n = 90{,}000$ where dense-covariance GPs are infeasible. For surfaces with sharp discontinuities, the Aug-IQN architecture --- an augmented IQN with boundary detection --- produces sharp, well-calibrated predictive distributions on both sides of the boundary, while the GP's stationarity assumption causes it to smooth across. On the Phantom benchmark, Aug-IQN achieves RMSE 0.044 versus the mixed jump GP's 0.053. Details in @sec-surrogates and @sec-jumps.

*Bayesian inference.* GBC learns the posterior $\pi(\theta \mid y)$ from simulated (parameter, data) pairs, turning Bayesian inference into a supervised learning problem. The framework handles both parametric models where the likelihood is expensive to evaluate and simulator-based models where no likelihood exists at all. The learned map $G_\phi(\tau, z)$ takes a quantile level and data summary and returns the posterior quantile. Drawing $\tau$ uniformly produces posterior samples; evaluating at $\tau = 0.5$ gives the posterior median; evaluating at $\tau = 0.025$ and $\tau = 0.975$ gives a 95% credible interval. The quantile function captures multimodal posteriors automatically: different $\tau$ values map to different modes, and the monotonicity of the quantile function guarantees that it cannot miss either one. When the posterior has two well-separated modes, a random-walk Metropolis chain can get trapped in one mode for very long periods, producing a biased sample that misrepresents the posterior. Running multiple chains from different starting points helps but requires careful initialization. The trained IQN, by contrast, captures both modes automatically: low $\tau$ values map to one mode and high $\tau$ values to the other, with intermediate $\tau$ capturing the valley between them. This is a consequence of the quantile function's monotonicity: it must pass through both modes on its way from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, so it cannot miss either one. Full development in @sec-bayesian-inference.

*Prediction.* Given observations $Z_{1:n} = (X_1, Y_1), \ldots, (X_n, Y_n)$, predict $Y_{n+1}$ at covariates $x_{n+1}$. GBC learns the predictive quantile function directly:
$$
Y_{n+1} = Q(\tau, x_{n+1}, S(Z_{1:n})), \quad \tau \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1),
$$
bypassing explicit computation of the prior, likelihood, and the marginalizing integral. The summary statistic $S(Z_{1:n})$ compresses the training data into a fixed-dimensional vector that captures the relevant information for prediction. The de Finetti--Ressel representation theorem [@ressel1985finettitype] provides the theoretical justification: for exchangeable data, there exists an implicit prior and likelihood such that the predictive distribution is a proper Bayesian predictive. The Bayesian validity comes from the exchangeability assumption and the representation theorem, not from a specific choice of prior.

The predictive quantile function directly gives prediction intervals: for any coverage level $1 - \alpha$, the interval $[Q(\alpha/2, x_{n+1}, S), \; Q(1 - \alpha/2, x_{n+1}, S)]$ is an approximate $(1-\alpha)$ prediction interval. Unlike conformal prediction intervals, which guarantee only *marginal* coverage, the GBC intervals aim for *conditional* coverage (valid for each specific $x_{n+1}$). See @sec-prediction.

*Maximum expected utility.* Decision theory is the natural companion to Bayesian inference. Having computed the posterior, we want to use it to make optimal decisions. The foundational principle, due to @savage1954foundations: choose the action $a$ that maximizes expected utility $E[U(a, Y) \mid y_{\text{obs}}]$, where the expectation is over the posterior predictive distribution. The classical approach requires integrating the utility function over the predictive distribution --- a double integral (over parameters and outcomes) that is often intractable. Even with MCMC, computing expected utility requires nested Monte Carlo that introduces variance and computational cost. GBC sidesteps this via a quantile identity. For any random variable $X$ with quantile function $Q_X(\tau)$:
$$
E[X] = \int_0^1 Q_X(\tau) \, d\tau.
$$ {#eq-intro-quantile-identity}
For monotone utility $U$, $E[U(X)] = \int_0^1 U(Q_X(\tau)) \, d\tau$. Since the IQN directly produces quantile functions, expected utility is a one-dimensional integral over $\tau$ --- no density estimation, no posterior sampling, no nested Monte Carlo. The integral is computed by simple numerical quadrature (trapezoidal rule or Gauss-Legendre) on a grid of $\tau$ values, costing negligible computation compared to the MCMC-based alternative. This reflects a connection between quantile-based inference and decision theory that traces back to Yaari's dual theory of choice under risk [@yaari1987dual]. Wang's distortion operator [@wang2000class] formalizes the Bayesian updating step in this quantile framework: the posterior quantile function is obtained by composing the prior quantile function with a learned distortion, exactly the operation the IQN performs. See @sec-meu.

*Causal inference.* Estimating treatment effects from observational data requires controlling for confounders --- the fundamental problem being that we never observe both potential outcomes for the same individual [@holland1986statistics]. Under unconfoundedness, the conditional average treatment effect $\tau(x) = E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]$ can be identified from observational data, but estimating it well requires simultaneously learning the propensity score $e(x) = P(Z = 1 \mid X = x)$, the conditional outcome distribution $P(Y \mid X, Z)$, and the treatment effect itself. GBC extends naturally to this setting: a propensity network estimates $e(x)$ via binary cross-entropy, and an IQN learns the conditional quantile function of outcomes using a composite loss (pinball + MAE anchor) with the estimated propensity as an additional covariate. Because the IQN produces the entire conditional quantile function of outcomes, we can compute not just the average treatment effect but quantile treatment effects (QTEs) --- how does the treatment affect the 10th percentile of outcomes? The 90th percentile? These reveal heterogeneity invisible to mean-based estimators. On the LaLonde job-training benchmark, GBC detects that individuals in the top quartile of predicted benefit gain roughly three times more from the program than those in the bottom quartile --- a finding with direct policy implications for targeting interventions. See @sec-causal.



## GBC versus Classical Bayesian Computation {#sec-intro-vs-classical}

How does this book relate to the classical Bayesian computational toolbox --- MCMC, variational inference, expectation propagation, and Gaussian process emulation [see, e.g., @gelman2013bayesian; @robert2004monte; @brooks2011handbook]? The relationship is complementary, not adversarial. Classical methods and GBC share the same inferential goals --- posterior distributions, predictive distributions, and decision analysis --- but differ in their computational strategy. Classical Bayesian computation shines when the model is explicit --- when you can write down $p(y \mid \theta)$ and $\pi(\theta)$ and compute the posterior via MCMC or analytical approximation. The standard workflow is well-established: specify the model, code it in Stan or PyMC, run MCMC, check convergence, interpret the posterior. GBC shines when the model is implicit --- when you have a forward simulator but no closed-form likelihood. The GBC workflow is different: specify the prior, write a simulator, generate training pairs, train an IQN, and query the trained network. In the overlap region where both apply --- models with explicit likelihoods and moderate dimension --- they should give the same answers. We verify this explicitly in @sec-bayesian-inference on the normal-normal conjugate model and a bimodal posterior. The choice between them is then a matter of computational efficiency and workflow: MCMC for single-dataset analyses where convergence is fast, GBC for multi-dataset deployments where amortization pays off.

*Classical Bayesian computation* assumes you can *write down* the likelihood $p(y \mid \theta)$ and *evaluate* it pointwise. This is essential for MCMC, which proposes parameter values and accepts or rejects based on the likelihood ratio.

*GBC* assumes you can *simulate* from the likelihood. You need a forward model that generates synthetic data given parameters, but you never need to evaluate $p(y \mid \theta)$ as a function. Simulation is strictly weaker than evaluation --- and it is often all you have.
:::

When should you use MCMC, and when should you use GBC?

*MCMC is better when* the model is low-dimensional (say $d \leq 5$) with a well-behaved posterior, you need detailed diagnostics such as effective sample size and divergent transitions, and you have a single dataset with a closed-form likelihood. If you have no need for amortization and the cost of running a single chain is modest, MCMC lets you exploit conjugacy or gradient information via HMC.

*GBC is better when* the likelihood has no closed form --- as in simulator-based and agent-based models --- or when you need amortized inference across many datasets. If a lab needs to fit the same model to data from 5,000 sensors, MCMC requires 5,000 separate chains (at 30 seconds each, over 40 hours), while GBC trains once (say, 2 hours) and then runs 5,000 forward passes in about 4 minutes total. GBC is also the right choice when the model involves high-dimensional data demanding linear scaling, or when decision-making requires expected utilities expressed through quantile functions.

There is no universal winner. GBC is not a replacement for careful model building --- you still need to choose the right prior and simulator. It is not a black box that eliminates the need for statistical thinking --- you still need to choose summary statistics, check calibration, and validate results. And it is not a magic bullet for high-dimensional parameter spaces --- the number of training points needed for accurate posterior estimation grows with the dimension of $\theta$, just as it does for any nonparametric method.

What GBC *is* is a computational framework that replaces MCMC's Markov chain with a trained neural network, ABC's local kernel with a global quantile function, and variational inference's parametric family with a nonparametric quantile representation. Variational methods [@blei2017variational] approximate the posterior by optimizing over a tractable family $q(\theta)$, but they require evaluating the likelihood (or its gradient) at every optimization step, and the posterior is constrained to the variational family (typically a factorized Gaussian), which may miss multimodality and complex correlation structure. GBC avoids both limitations: it does not evaluate the likelihood, and the quantile function representation is nonparametric --- it can represent any continuous distribution, not just members of a specified parametric family. Where variational inference approximates $\pi(\theta \mid y)$ with a tractable distribution, GBC approximates the quantile function $Q_{\theta \mid y}(\tau)$ with a neural network, and the approximation error is controlled by the network's capacity, not by the expressiveness of a parametric family. Normalizing flows [@rezende2015variational] address some of variational inference's expressiveness limitations by using invertible neural networks, but they still require likelihood evaluation. GBC requires only simulation.



## Historical Note {#sec-intro-history}

The ideas behind GBC have roots in three distinct intellectual traditions. Tracing these lineages helps explain why the framework arrived when it did and not earlier.

*Parzen's quantile philosophy.* Emanuel Parzen argued throughout his career that quantile functions --- not densities --- are the natural language of statistics. In his influential 1979 paper and later work [@parzen2004quantile], Parzen developed the "quantile approach": every distributional question should be formulated in terms of $Q(\tau) = F^{-1}(\tau)$, and every statistical operation should be expressed as a transformation of quantile functions. The compositional identity $Q_{g(Y)}(\tau) = g(Q_Y(\tau))$ for monotone $g$ connects quantile functions to deep learning, where function composition is the defining principle. Parzen's "comparison distribution" $D(\tau, x)$ that decomposes conditional quantiles as $Q_{Y|X}(\tau \mid x) = Q_Y(D(\tau, x))$ anticipates Wang's distortion operator and the IQN's learned transport map. The comparison distribution tells us how conditioning on $X = x$ distorts the unconditional quantile function --- it is a learned warping of the probability scale, and it is the natural representation of Bayesian updating when you think in quantiles rather than densities. Around the same time, @koenker1978regression developed linear quantile regression, showing that the $\tau$th conditional quantile can be estimated by minimizing the asymmetric absolute loss (the pinball loss). This gave Parzen's quantile philosophy a concrete computational method, one that we extend from linear models to deep networks throughout this book.

*Rubin's simulation principle.* @rubin1984bayesianly showed that Bayesian inference can be understood as a simulation exercise. Consider the joint distribution of $(\theta, y)$ where $\theta \sim \pi(\theta)$ and $y \mid \theta \sim p(y \mid \theta)$. The conditional distribution of $\theta$ given $y = y_{\text{obs}}$ is, by definition, the posterior $\pi(\theta \mid y_{\text{obs}})$. So if we simulate many pairs $(\theta_i, y_i)$ from the joint and keep only those where $y_i \approx y_{\text{obs}}$, we obtain posterior samples. This insight --- that *simulation is sufficient for Bayesian inference* --- is the foundational principle of both ABC and GBC. The difference is in how "matching" is defined: ABC uses a local kernel around the observed summary statistics, while GBC learns a global quantile transport map that works for all possible observed datasets simultaneously. Rubin's paper was primarily about the interpretation of frequentist procedures in a Bayesian framework, and the computational implications were not its main focus. The computational exploitation of Rubin's insight had to wait for the development of ABC in the late 1990s [@tavare1997inferring; @beaumont2002approximate] and for GBC in the 2020s.

*The IQN from distributional RL.* In 2017, @bellemare2017distributional introduced distributional RL, learning the *distribution* of future returns in an RL agent, not just the expected return. This was a departure from classical RL, where the value function $V(s) = E[\sum_t \gamma^t r_t \mid s]$ is a scalar. Distributional RL replaces $V(s)$ with a random variable $Z(s)$ whose distribution captures the full uncertainty about future outcomes. The motivation was practical --- risk-sensitive control requires knowing the full distribution of returns, not just the mean --- but the mathematical framework turned out to be broadly useful. @dabney2017distributional showed that the 1-Wasserstein distance between distributions is minimized by quantile regression, providing the theoretical link between distributional RL and quantile estimation.

@dabney2018implicit then proposed the IQN --- cosine embedding of $\tau$, elementwise combination with state features, training via the pinball loss --- designed for Atari games, but it is precisely the architecture GBC needs for posterior quantile estimation. The connection is not accidental: both problems involve learning a conditional distribution from simulated experience. An Atari agent simulates many game trajectories and learns the distribution of returns conditional on state; a Bayesian analyst simulates many (parameter, data) pairs and learns the posterior conditional on data summaries. The mathematical structure is identical, and the IQN handles both.

The synthesis arrived in @polson2024generative, which recognized that Parzen's quantile philosophy, Rubin's simulation principle, and Dabney's IQN architecture could be woven into a unified framework for Bayesian inference. The noise outsourcing theorem [@kallenberg1997foundations] provides the theoretical guarantee that the conditional quantile function exists and is the right object to learn. The universal approximation theorem for deep ReLU networks [@schmidt-hieber2020nonparametric] guarantees that the IQN can learn it. And the pinball loss provides a proper scoring rule for training.

GBC is thus not a single invention but a convergence of ideas from statistics, probability, and machine learning --- each necessary, none sufficient alone. Parzen gave us the right mathematical objects (quantile functions). Rubin gave us the right computational principle (simulation is sufficient for Bayesian inference). Koenker gave us the right loss function (the pinball loss). Dabney gave us the right architecture (the IQN with cosine embedding). And Kallenberg gave us the right theorem (noise outsourcing). This book assembles these pieces into a coherent framework and develops the theory, algorithms, and applications that make it practical.

## A Note on Style {#sec-intro-style}

This book follows the computational statistics tradition exemplified by @gramacy2020surrogates: we develop theory alongside working code, we are honest about when methods fail, and we believe that understanding comes from running experiments, not just reading proofs. Bobby Gramacy's *Surrogates* set a standard for how a technical monograph can be simultaneously rigorous and readable --- conversational in tone, first-person throughout, with working code integrated into every chapter. Every claim is backed by a reproducible example. Failures are discussed as openly as successes.

We have tried to follow that model, adapted to the GBC framework and Python/PyTorch instead of R. When GBC works well, we give the numbers --- CRPS, RMSE, coverage, wall-clock time. When it fails --- as on high signal-to-noise data where the multiplicative $h \odot \tau_h$ architecture conflates signal and noise, a problem we confront honestly in @sec-calibration --- we say that too, and show what to do about it. We believe that a method paper that reports only successes is less useful than one that also maps the boundaries of applicability.

All code is in Python using PyTorch [@paszke2019pytorch]. The `gbc` package provides modular implementations of every method; see @sec-computing for the full module inventory and setup instructions.

We adopt Parzen's philosophy that *quantiles are the natural language of statistical modeling* [@parzen2004quantile]. Densities, likelihoods, and integrals are replaced wherever possible by quantile functions, pinball losses, and function composition. When you think of a distribution as a density, Bayesian updating is an integral --- and computation requires evaluating that integral (MCMC, variational inference). When you think of a distribution as a quantile function, Bayesian updating is a composition of functions (the Wang distortion) --- and computation requires learning that composition (the IQN). The density perspective privileges analytical tractability: models with conjugate priors, exponential family likelihoods, and closed-form marginals are the easiest to work with. The quantile perspective privileges computational tractability: models where you can simulate (parameter, data) pairs are the easiest to work with, regardless of whether the likelihood has a closed form. Both perspectives are valid. This book is written from the quantile side, and we hope to convince the reader that this perspective is not just viable but often preferable.

**Wang Distortion for Normal Updating.**

The Wang distortion provides a density-free characterization of Bayesian updating in quantile space. This exercise develops the connection between Bayesian updating and quantile function composition.

(d) For a non-conjugate model --- say, a Cauchy likelihood $y \mid \theta \sim \text{Cauchy}(\theta, 1)$ with a normal prior --- the Wang distortion still exists but is no longer available in closed form.
