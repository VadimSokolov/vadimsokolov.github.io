Throughout this book, we have presented the Implicit Quantile Network as a versatile tool. It handles heteroscedastic noise on the motorcycle data (Section 5.7), models jump discontinuities with boundary augmentation (Chapter 8), serves as a competitive surrogate for computer experiments (Chapter 7), and drives active learning via randomized-prior ensembles (Chapter 9). The natural temptation is to reach for IQN on every regression problem with uncertainty quantification.

This chapter is about when that temptation leads you astray.

We document a systematic failure of IQN on a real forecasting task — lake temperature prediction — where the response surface has high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). In this regime, the IQN’s multiplicative \(\tau\)-embedding architecture collapses: all quantile levels converge to the same well-fitted conditional mean, and prediction intervals shrink to near-zero width. Coverage at the 90% nominal level drops to 11–34%. Increasing model capacity makes the problem worse, not better.

The remedy is a two-part strategy. First, we replace IQN with a heteroscedastic MLP ensemble trained with Gaussian negative log-likelihood, which explicitly parameterizes both the conditional mean and variance. Second, we apply conformal calibration via temporal cross-validation to produce distribution-free prediction intervals with honest coverage. The combination — which we call the GBC calibration pipeline — achieves 90.2% coverage on the lake temperature benchmark while reducing RMSE by 7.1% relative to a three-stage Gaussian process baseline.

The lesson is practical: not every problem is best served by quantile regression. When the conditional distribution is well-described by a location-scale family with small residual variance, a parametric variance model with conformal post-processing is the appropriate choice. Knowing when to abandon IQN is as important as knowing how to use it.

We first analyze the IQN failure mode in Section 14.1, including a mathematical explanation of the gradient collapse mechanism. We then develop the het-MLP ensemble (Section 14.2) and its Gaussian NLL training loss (Section 14.3), followed by the conformal calibration pipeline (Section 14.4) with per-horizon adjustments (Section 14.5). The full lake temperature benchmark results appear in Section 14.6, and we close with a diagnostic checklist (Section 14.7) and guidance on when conformal post-processing is warranted (Section 14.8).

14.1 IQN Failure on High Signal-to-Noise Data

To understand the failure, we need to revisit the IQN architecture from Section 5.2. The IQN maps \((x, \tau)\) to a quantile prediction via

\[ \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x) = g_\theta\bigl(\psi_\theta(x) \odot \phi_\theta(\tau)\bigr), \tag{14.1}\]

where \(\psi_\theta(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the covariate encoder, \(\phi_\theta(\tau) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) is the cosine-based quantile encoder, and \(\odot\) denotes elementwise multiplication. The multiplicative merge is the architectural key: the quantile level \(\tau\) modulates the feature representation of \(x\), allowing different parts of the distribution to activate different features.

This works well when the conditional distribution \(Y \mid X = x\) has substantial spread — for example, the motorcycle data where the variance changes by two orders of magnitude, or the Phantom function where bimodal regions produce wide distributional spread. In these settings, the \(\tau\)-modulation has real work to do: \(\hat{Q}(0.05 \mid x)\) and \(\hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x)\) must differ substantially, and the multiplicative architecture can express this difference.

Recall that the cosine embedding from Eq. 5.2 maps \(\tau \in [0, 1]\) to

\[ \phi(\tau) = \text{ReLU}\bigl(W_\tau [\cos(\pi j \tau)]_{j=0}^{d-1} + b_\tau\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^d. \]

When the conditional distribution has large spread, \(\phi(\tau)\) must vary substantially with \(\tau\): the features activated at \(\tau = 0.05\) must differ meaningfully from those activated at \(\tau = 0.95\). The multiplicative merge \(\psi(x) \odot \phi(\tau)\) then produces genuinely different representations for different quantile levels, and the output layer maps these distinct representations to different quantile predictions. The architecture is expressive enough to capture this variation.

But consider what happens when the signal-to-noise ratio is high. If \(Y \mid X = x\) has small variance relative to the range of \(Y\) — say the standard deviation of \(Y \mid X\) is 2% of the total range of \(Y\) — then the quantile function \(F^{-1}_{Y|X}(\tau)\) is nearly flat in \(\tau\). All quantile levels produce nearly the same value: the conditional mean. The optimal \(\phi_\theta(\tau)\) is close to a constant vector, and the \(\tau\)-modulation reduces to a negligible perturbation of the already well-fitted covariate representation \(\psi_\theta(x)\).

During training, the loss landscape strongly rewards fitting the conditional mean — this is where most of the predictive signal lives. Consider the expected pinball loss: \(\mathbb{E}[\rho_\tau(Y - \hat{Q}(\tau \mid X))]\) is minimized when \(\hat{Q}(\tau \mid X) = F^{-1}_{Y|X}(\tau)\). When the conditional distribution is nearly a point mass, the quantile function is nearly flat, and the pinball loss is dominated by the mean-fit component. The gradients that would push \(\phi_\theta(\tau)\) to create distributional spread are tiny compared to the gradients that improve the mean fit. Standard SGD converges to a solution where \(\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)\) is nearly constant in \(\tau\): the network has effectively learned a point prediction, not a distributional one.

14.1.1 Gradient collapse mechanism

We analyze the gradient signal in the multiplicative architecture. Let \(z = \psi_\theta(x) \odot \phi_\theta(\tau) \in \mathbb{R}^d\) be the merged representation, and suppose the output layer is linear: \(\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x) = w^\top z + b\). The pinball loss for quantile level \(\tau\) is \(\rho_\tau(y - \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x))\), and the gradient with respect to the quantile-encoder parameters \(\theta_\tau\) (the parameters of \(\phi_\theta\)) is

\[ \frac{\partial \rho_\tau}{\partial \theta_\tau} = -\bigl(\tau - \mathbf{1}[y < \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)]\bigr) \cdot w^\top \left(\psi_\theta(x) \odot \frac{\partial \phi_\theta(\tau)}{\partial \theta_\tau}\right). \tag{14.2}\]

The relevant factor is the Hadamard product \(\psi_\theta(x) \odot \frac{\partial \phi_\theta(\tau)}{\partial \theta_\tau}\). To produce the correct quantile function in the high-SNR regime, \(\phi_\theta(\tau)\) must be close to a constant vector \(\mathbf{c}\) for all \(\tau\), plus a small perturbation \(\epsilon(\tau)\) that encodes the residual spread:

\[ \phi_\theta(\tau) \approx \mathbf{c} + \epsilon(\tau), \quad \|\epsilon(\tau)\| \ll \|\mathbf{c}\|. \tag{14.3}\]

The perturbation \(\epsilon(\tau)\) does all the distributional work: it creates the difference between \(\hat{Q}(0.05 \mid x)\) and \(\hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x)\). But the gradient of \(\epsilon(\tau)\) must fight against the dominant gradient from the constant part \(\mathbf{c}\), which drives \(\psi_\theta(x) \odot \mathbf{c}\) toward the conditional mean. In the loss landscape, the direction that improves the mean fit (adjusting \(\psi_\theta\) and \(\mathbf{c}\)) has gradient magnitude proportional to the signal strength, while the direction that increases distributional spread (adjusting \(\epsilon(\tau)\)) has gradient magnitude proportional to the noise level \(\sigma\). When the signal-to-noise ratio is large, the distributional gradient is swamped. Throughout this chapter we use the range-based SNR as a practical diagnostic: \[ \mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}} = \left(\frac{\text{range of } y}{\hat\sigma_{\text{residual}}}\right)^2, \tag{14.4}\] which is easy to compute from data. On the lake data, \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}} \approx (26/1.2)^2 \approx 470\), so the mean-fit gradient is roughly \(\sqrt{470} \approx 22\) times larger than the spread gradient, making it nearly impossible for SGD to allocate learning capacity to the distributional component.

Note

The formal variance-based definition \(\mathrm{SNR} = \mathrm{Var}(\mu(X))/\sigma^2\) requires knowledge of the true conditional mean \(\mu(x)\), which is unavailable before fitting the model. The range-based proxy Eq. 14.4 is an upper bound (range \(\geq 2\,\mathrm{sd}\)) and is conservative: if \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}}\) is large, the variance-based SNR is also large.

There is a second, more insidious mechanism that reinforces the first. Suppose the network has learned the conditional mean well, so that \(\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x) \approx \mu(x)\) for all \(\tau\). The residual \(y - \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x) \approx y - \mu(x) = \epsilon\) is independent of \(\tau\). The indicator \(\mathbf{1}[y < \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)]\) then does not vary with \(\tau\) for a given \((x, y)\) pair: it is always 0 or always 1 depending on the sign of \(\epsilon\). The gradient signal \(\tau - \mathbf{1}[\cdot]\) that should push different quantile levels apart becomes effectively constant across \(\tau\), and the quantile encoder receives no informative gradient about how to differentiate the quantile levels. This is the gradient collapse: the very success of the mean fit eliminates the gradient signal that would produce distributional spread.

Consider training with \(\tau = 0.95\). The gradient signal is \(\tau - \mathbf{1}[y < \hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x)] = 0.95 - \mathbf{1}[\epsilon < 0]\). If \(\hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x) \approx \mu(x)\), then the indicator is 0 half the time (when \(\epsilon > 0\)) and 1 half the time (when \(\epsilon < 0\)), giving an average gradient signal of \(0.95 - 0.5 = 0.45\). This is a valid signal — it tells the network to push \(\hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x)\) upward. But through the multiplicative architecture, this signal must modulate \(\phi(0.95)\) relative to the dominant covariate representation \(\psi(x)\), and the required modulation is tiny relative to \(\|\psi(x)\|\) when the SNR is high. The gradient exists but is too small to be effective.

This analysis explains why increasing model capacity makes the problem worse. A larger network fits \(\mu(x)\) more tightly, reducing the residuals further and making the gradient collapse more severe. It also explains why the two-stage approach (MLP for mean, IQN on residuals) provides only marginal improvement: even after subtracting the fitted mean, the residuals have low variance relative to the IQN’s capacity to express distributional spread via the multiplicative architecture, and the same gradient collapse applies at a smaller scale.

An alternative architecture could avoid this failure. If the quantile level \(\tau\) entered additively rather than multiplicatively — for example, \(\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x) = \psi_\theta(x) + \sigma_\theta(x) \cdot \Phi^{-1}(\tau)\), where \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the standard normal quantile function — then the spread would be controlled by \(\sigma_\theta(x)\), which can be trained independently of the mean fit. This is precisely the het-MLP architecture of Section 14.2, which parameterizes the mean and variance separately. The multiplicative architecture of Eq. 14.1 was designed for distributional reinforcement learning (Dabney et al. 2018), where the value distribution is intrinsically spread out and the \(\tau\)-modulation must express complex multimodal shapes. In regression with tight Gaussian-like noise, the architectural flexibility is not needed, and its coupling of mean and spread creates the gradient collapse we observe.

Define the quantile spread ratio (QSR) as the ratio of the learned 90% PI width to the oracle PI width:

\[ \text{QSR} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n [\hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x_i) - \hat{Q}(0.05 \mid x_i)]}{2 \times 1.645 \times \hat{\sigma}_{\text{residual}}}, \tag{14.5}\]

where \(\hat{\sigma}_{\text{residual}}\) is the standard deviation of the IQN’s median residuals. A well-calibrated model has \(\text{QSR} \approx 1\). On the motorcycle data, the IQN achieves \(\text{QSR} \approx 0.95\) (excellent). On the lake data, the standard IQN achieves \(\text{QSR} \approx 0.28\) (catastrophic collapse). The QSR is a useful single-number summary of IQN calibration quality. We recommend computing the QSR as a routine diagnostic whenever training an IQN on a new dataset. If \(\text{QSR} < 0.5\), the IQN is suffering from gradient collapse and should be replaced with a het-MLP. If \(0.5 \leq \text{QSR} \leq 0.8\), the IQN is partially collapsed and conformal post-processing may rescue it. If \(\text{QSR} > 0.8\), the IQN is working well and no intervention is needed.

14.1.2 Empirical evidence

The gradient collapse manifests dramatically on real data. We encountered this failure on the Falling Creek Reservoir lake temperature forecasting task (Holthuijzen et al. 2025). The dataset consists of \(N = 281{,}764\) records with 6 input features (day of year, water depth, forecast horizon, a lagged temperature covariate, and two outputs from a physics-based lake simulator) predicting observed water temperature. The response surface is smooth and highly predictable: the physics-based simulator already captures most of the variance, and the statistical model’s job is to refine the simulator’s output and quantify residual uncertainty.

To quantify the SNR on this dataset: the total range of observed temperatures is approximately 4–30\({}^\circ\)C (a range of 26\({}^\circ\)C), and the standard deviation of the best residuals (after a well-fitted MLP) is approximately \(1.2\,{}^{\circ}\)C. Using Eq. 14.4, \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}} \approx (26/1.2)^2 \approx 470\), or equivalently \(\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}}} \approx 22\) in standard-deviation terms. This is well above the 10:1 threshold (in sd terms) we suggested for IQN failure.

We tested three IQN variants. The standard IQN (256$$3, 3000 epochs) achieved RMSE \(= 1.310\,{}^{\circ}\)C — competitive with the GP baseline — but the 90% prediction interval coverage was only 34%, with an average PI width of just \(1.0\,{}^{\circ}\)C; the network learned an excellent point prediction and negligible distributional spread. The larger IQN (512$$4, 8000 epochs) performed worse on both fronts: RMSE \(= 1.404\,{}^{\circ}\)C and coverage dropped to 11%, because more capacity allowed the covariate encoder to fit the mean more tightly, leaving even less room for \(\tau\)-modulation. The two-stage MLP + IQN (first stage: MLP for mean; second stage: IQN on residuals) reduced stage 1 RMSE to \(1.281\,{}^{\circ}\)C but coverage remained at only 29% — even operating on residuals, the multiplicative architecture could not produce enough spread.

All three variants produce reasonable point predictions but catastrophically miscalibrated prediction intervals. The 90% PI width should be roughly \(\pm 1.645 \sigma\) around the mean; on this data, the required \(\sigma \approx 1.1\,{}^{\circ}\)C, implying a PI width of \(\approx 3.6\,{}^{\circ}\)C. The IQN’s actual width of \(1.0\,{}^{\circ}\)C is a factor of 3.6 too narrow. The IQN’s 90% prediction interval captures only about one-third of the observations it should, making the uncertainty estimate useless for decision-making. A reservoir manager relying on these intervals would be systematically surprised by observations outside the predicted range, which could lead to inappropriate operational decisions — for example, failing to prepare for temperature exceedances that trigger algal blooms because the model predicted narrow intervals suggesting stable conditions.

This failure mode provides a useful diagnostic. Before committing to IQN for a new problem, compute \(\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}}\) from Eq. 14.4 after fitting a quick point predictor. If \(\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}}}\) is large — say greater than 10 — the IQN’s multiplicative \(\tau\)-architecture will likely under-disperse. The symptoms are coverage far below nominal (e.g., 30–50% at a 90% target), PI width that does not grow with the nominal coverage level (so that 80% and 95% PIs have nearly the same width), and a pattern where increasing model capacity makes coverage worse, not better.

When you see these symptoms, switch to a parametric variance model. The remainder of this chapter develops that alternative.

Contrast this failure with the IQN’s success on the motorcycle data (Section 5.7). On the motorcycle, the noise standard deviation varies from near zero (at \(t < 15\) ms) to roughly 40 g’s (at \(t \approx 35\) ms), while the signal range is about 80 g’s. The SNR is at most 4:1 in the high-variance region and effectively infinite in the low-variance region — but the key point is that the distributional structure (heteroscedasticity, possible non-Gaussianity) is exactly what the IQN’s \(\tau\)-modulation is designed to capture. On the lake data, by contrast, the distributional structure is simple (approximately Gaussian with slowly varying variance) and the IQN’s flexibility is wasted. Match the model’s flexibility to the complexity of the conditional distribution, not to the complexity of the mean function.

The following table summarizes the diagnostic across our main benchmarks, ordered by decreasing distributional complexity. As the conditional distribution becomes simpler and the SNR increases, the IQN’s advantage disappears and eventually reverses.

The IQN is the right tool for complex conditional distributions where the quantile function has non-Gaussian structure: heavy tails, multimodality, or rapid changes in variance. When the distribution is approximately Gaussian with high SNR, the IQN’s architectural strength becomes a weakness, and the simpler het-MLP with conformal calibration is the appropriate choice. These methods are complementary tools, not competitors. The diagnostic question is: what is the complexity of the conditional distribution, and does the model architecture match it?

14.2 Het-MLP Ensemble

The remedy is conceptually simple: instead of asking a single network to learn the entire quantile function, we explicitly parameterize the conditional mean and variance in a heteroscedastic MLP and combine multiple such models in an ensemble. This approach makes a stronger distributional assumption (Gaussianity) than the IQN (which is distribution-free), but the assumption buys us a crucial advantage: the variance is parameterized independently of the mean, so it cannot be suppressed by the gradient collapse mechanism.

Each ensemble member is a standard MLP with a shared backbone and two output heads:

\[ \text{backbone:} \quad h = f_\theta(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d, \] \[ \text{mean head:} \quad \hat{\mu}_k(x) = W_\mu^\top h + b_\mu \in \mathbb{R}, \tag{14.6}\] \[ \text{log-variance head:} \quad \log \hat{\sigma}^2_k(x) = W_\sigma^\top h + b_\sigma \in \mathbb{R}. \tag{14.7}\]

The backbone \(f_\theta\) is a standard MLP with ReLU activations. The mean head is a single linear layer. The log-variance head is also a single linear layer, parameterizing \(\log \hat{\sigma}^2\) rather than \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) directly. This log-parameterization ensures positivity of the variance without requiring a softplus or exponential activation in the forward pass (the exponential is applied only when computing the loss and during prediction).

The architectural choice of a shared backbone with separate heads is deliberate. The backbone learns a rich feature representation \(h = f_\theta(x)\) that captures the nonlinear structure in the input-output relationship. The mean and variance heads then compute simple linear functions of this shared representation. This weight-sharing has two advantages: it is parameter-efficient (the bulk of the parameters are in the shared backbone, not duplicated across heads), and it encourages the backbone to learn features that are useful for both mean prediction and variance estimation. The alternative — separate networks for mean and variance — would require twice the parameters and would miss the opportunity to share representational structure.

The two-head architecture also provides a natural form of multi-task learning: the backbone must extract features that serve both the mean and variance heads. This is beneficial because the features that predict the mean (e.g., depth-season interactions, GLM bias patterns) are often correlated with the features that predict the variance (e.g., forecast difficulty varies with the same depth-season interactions). The shared backbone discovers these correlations automatically, producing a more coherent model than two independent networks.

The multi-task perspective also explains a subtle benefit of training with Gaussian NLL rather than MSE. With MSE, the backbone optimizes only for mean prediction accuracy. With Gaussian NLL, the backbone must simultaneously support both accurate mean predictions and accurate variance predictions, which encourages it to learn features that are informative about both the location and the spread of the conditional distribution. Empirically, this produces slightly better mean predictions than MSE alone, because the precision weighting \((y - \mu)^2 / \sigma^2\) focuses the optimization on observations where the model claims to be confident, implicitly downweighting noisy or outlying observations that would distort the MSE-optimal solution.

TipDefinition 14.1: Heteroscedastic MLP

A heteroscedastic MLP with backbone dimension \(d\) and \(L\) hidden layers maps \(x \in \mathbb{R}^p\) to a Gaussian predictive distribution: \[ Y \mid X = x \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\bigl(\hat{\mu}(x),\; \hat{\sigma}^2(x)\bigr), \] where \(\hat{\mu}(x)\) and \(\hat{\sigma}^2(x) = \exp(\text{logvar head output})\) are learned end-to-end.

The following code implements the architecture.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np

torch.manual_seed(42)

class HetMLP(nn.Module):
    """Heteroscedastic MLP with mean and log-variance heads."""
    def __init__(self, xdim, hdim=256, nlayers=3):
        super().__init__()
        layers = [nn.Linear(xdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        for _ in range(nlayers - 1):
            layers += [nn.Linear(hdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        self.backbone = nn.Sequential(*layers)
        self.mean_head = nn.Linear(hdim, 1)
        self.logvar_head = nn.Linear(hdim, 1)

    def forward(self, x):
        h = self.backbone(x)
        return self.mean_head(h), self.logvar_head(h)

# Verify
model = HetMLP(xdim=6, hdim=256, nlayers=3)
x_demo = torch.randn(32, 6)
mu, logvar = model(x_demo)
print(f"Input: x.shape = {x_demo.shape}")
print(f"Output: mu.shape = {mu.shape}, logvar.shape = {logvar.shape}")
n_params = sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters())
print(f"Parameters: {n_params:,}")

We train \(K = 5\) ensemble members with identical architecture but different random seeds for weight initialization and mini-batch ordering. The diversity among members arises purely from the stochasticity of SGD — no bootstrap resampling or explicit diversity mechanism is needed. This follows the deep ensemble approach of Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, and Blundell (2017). The choice of \(K = 5\) balances computational cost against ensemble diversity: Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, and Blundell (2017) found that most of the calibration improvement occurs between \(K = 1\) and \(K = 5\), with diminishing returns beyond \(K = 5\). On the lake data, we confirmed this: \(K = 3\) achieves RMSE of 1.226\({}^\circ\)C, \(K = 5\) achieves 1.219\({}^\circ\)C, and \(K = 10\) achieves 1.215\({}^\circ\)C. The improvement from 5 to 10 members is only 0.3%, not worth the doubled training time.

The ensemble predictive distribution combines aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty via the law of total variance:

\[ \bar{\mu}(x) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{\mu}_k(x), \tag{14.8}\]

\[ \sigma^2_{\text{ens}}(x) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{\sigma}^2_k(x)}_{\text{aleatoric (mean within-member variance)}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{\mu}^2_k(x) - \bar{\mu}^2(x)}_{\text{epistemic (between-member disagreement)}}. \tag{14.9}\]

The first term is the average learned variance across ensemble members — the aleatoric component capturing irreducible noise. The second term is the variance of the member means — the epistemic component capturing model uncertainty. In regions with abundant training data, the epistemic term is small (members agree). In extrapolation regions, the epistemic term grows (members disagree), naturally widening prediction intervals.

This decomposition is particularly useful for active learning, as we discussed in Chapter 9: the epistemic component identifies regions where the model is uncertain due to lack of data, guiding the selection of new observations. For the calibration task in this chapter, both components contribute to the total variance, and we do not need to distinguish between them — the conformal calibration operates on the total prediction error, regardless of its source.

Show code (33 lines) 
def ensemble_predict(models, X):
    """Ensemble predictions with aleatoric + epistemic uncertainty.

    Args:
        models: list of HetMLP models
        X: (n, p) tensor

    Returns:
        ens_mean: (n,) array — ensemble mean prediction
        ens_std:  (n,) array — total standard deviation
    """
    K = len(models)
    all_mu = np.zeros((K, len(X)))
    all_var = np.zeros((K, len(X)))

    for k, m in enumerate(models):
        m.eval()
        with torch.no_grad():
            mu, logvar = m(X)
            all_mu[k] = mu.squeeze(1).numpy()
            all_var[k] = torch.exp(logvar.squeeze(1)).numpy()

    ens_mean = all_mu.mean(axis=0)
    ens_var = (all_var + all_mu**2).mean(axis=0) - ens_mean**2
    ens_std = np.sqrt(np.maximum(ens_var, 1e-6))
    return ens_mean, ens_std

# Demo with 3 untrained members
models_demo = [HetMLP(xdim=6) for _ in range(3)]
x_demo = torch.randn(100, 6)
mu_hat, std_hat = ensemble_predict(models_demo, x_demo)
print(f"Ensemble mean: shape={mu_hat.shape}, range=[{mu_hat.min():.3f}, {mu_hat.max():.3f}]")
print(f"Ensemble std:  shape={std_hat.shape}, range=[{std_hat.min():.3f}, {std_hat.max():.3f}]")

Why not use the Gaussian prediction intervals \(\bar{\mu}(x) \pm z_{0.95} \cdot \sigma_{\text{ens}}(x)\) directly? On the lake temperature data, these intervals achieve only 49% coverage at the 90% nominal level. The learned variance underestimates the true error by a factor of about 2.6. This is a well-known phenomenon: neural network variance estimates are systematically overconfident (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, and Blundell 2017). We address this with conformal calibration in Section 14.4.

Despite this miscalibration, the het-MLP ensemble still provides two valuable properties that the IQN does not. First, it produces point predictions (the ensemble mean) that are as good as or better than the IQN’s, because the mean head is trained to minimize the precision-weighted squared error rather than the pinball loss. The precision weighting means that the network focuses its capacity on regions where it claims high confidence, effectively prioritizing accuracy where it matters most. Second, the learned variance \(\hat{\sigma}^2(x)\), while miscalibrated in absolute magnitude, captures relative differences in uncertainty across inputs. Observations at long horizons produce larger \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) than observations at short horizons; deep-water predictions have larger \(\hat{\sigma}^2\) than surface predictions. This relative ordering is useful even when the absolute scale is wrong, and it provides the foundation for more adaptive conformal methods when the variance estimate becomes reliable enough.

The ensemble diversity also provides a natural measure of model uncertainty that is absent from a single IQN. When the five ensemble members disagree strongly about the prediction at a particular input \(x\), this indicates that the model is uncertain — either because \(x\) is in a region with sparse training data, or because the response surface is difficult to learn. This epistemic uncertainty signal can be used for active learning (as we discussed in Chapter 9) or for flagging predictions that warrant human review in an operational setting.

While the het-MLP ensemble’s Gaussian intervals are too narrow on their own, the architecture provides the right building blocks — good point predictions, relative variance ordering, and epistemic uncertainty from ensemble disagreement — that conformal calibration can transform into honest, well-calibrated prediction intervals.

14.3 Gaussian NLL Loss

Training a heteroscedastic MLP requires a loss function that simultaneously optimizes the mean and variance predictions. The standard choice is the Gaussian negative log-likelihood (NLL).

If we model \(Y \mid X = x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu(x), \sigma^2(x))\), the negative log-likelihood of a single observation \((x_i, y_i)\) is

\[ -\log p(y_i \mid x_i) = \frac{1}{2}\log \sigma^2(x_i) + \frac{(y_i - \mu(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2(x_i)} + \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi). \tag{14.10}\]

Dropping the constant \(\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)\), the Gaussian NLL loss is

\[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}(\mu, \sigma^2; x, y) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\log \sigma^2(x) + \frac{(y - \mu(x))^2}{\sigma^2(x)}\right). \tag{14.11}\]

The two terms interact in a complementary way. The mean term \((y - \mu)^2 / \sigma^2\) is a weighted squared error with weight \(1/\sigma^2\) (the precision): the network is penalized more for errors where it claims high confidence (small \(\sigma^2\)) and less where it claims low confidence (large \(\sigma^2\)). The variance regularizer \(\log \sigma^2\) prevents the trivial solution of setting \(\sigma^2 \to \infty\) (which would drive the mean term to zero) by imposing a log-variance penalty that grows as \(\sigma^2\) increases, forcing the network to keep its variance estimates tight.

The balance between these terms is automatic: at the optimum, \(\hat{\sigma}^2(x)\) equals the true conditional variance \(\text{Var}(Y \mid X = x)\) (up to finite-sample error). This is a maximum likelihood estimator of a location-scale model. The proof is straightforward: take the expected loss \(\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}]\), differentiate with respect to \(\mu(x)\) and \(\sigma^2(x)\) at each \(x\), and solve the first-order conditions. The optimal \(\mu(x)\) minimizes \(\mathbb{E}[(Y - \mu(x))^2 / \sigma^2(x)]\), which gives \(\mu(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]\). Substituting back, the optimal \(\sigma^2(x)\) minimizes \(\log \sigma^2(x) + \text{Var}(Y \mid X = x) / \sigma^2(x)\), which gives \(\sigma^2(x) = \text{Var}(Y \mid X = x)\). Exercise 14.2 asks you to work through this derivation in detail.

Compare Gaussian NLL with the losses we have used elsewhere in this book.

MSE loss \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = (y - \hat{\mu}(x))^2\) learns only the conditional mean. There is no variance output and no mechanism for uncertainty quantification. The MSE-optimal prediction is \(\hat{\mu}(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]\), which tells us nothing about the spread of \(Y \mid X\). This is the default loss in most deep learning applications, and for point prediction it is perfectly adequate. But for uncertainty quantification, we need more.

Pinball loss \(\rho_\tau(y - \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x))\) learns individual quantiles, and the integrated pinball loss (CRPS) learns the full conditional distribution nonparametrically. The IQN uses this loss to great effect when the distributional structure is complex. But as we saw in Section 14.1, the IQN architecture can fail to produce adequate spread on high-SNR data.

Gaussian NLL sits between these extremes. It makes a parametric assumption (Gaussian residuals) but learns both location and scale. When the Gaussian assumption is approximately correct — as it often is for well-specified regression models with moderate residuals — the NLL loss produces well-calibrated variance estimates. When the assumption is wrong, conformal calibration (Section 14.4) corrects the miscalibration.

We do not need the Gaussian assumption to be exactly correct. We need it to be useful enough that the learned \(\hat{\sigma}^2(x)\) captures relative differences in uncertainty across \(x\). Conformal calibration then adjusts the overall scale. This is a recurring theme in applied statistics: use a parametric model not because you believe it is true, but because it provides a useful scaffolding for inference. The Gaussian NLL gives us a mean and a variance; conformal calibration gives us honest intervals. Together, they produce well-calibrated uncertainty without requiring the data to be Gaussian.

There is a connection to scoring rules. A proper scoring rule is one that is minimized (in expectation) when the forecast distribution equals the true data-generating distribution. Gneiting and Raftery (2007) showed that for the Gaussian family, the negative log-likelihood is the unique proper scoring rule (up to affine transformation). This means that training with Gaussian NLL is guaranteed to produce the best Gaussian approximation to the true conditional distribution — it simultaneously optimizes the mean, the variance, and their interaction. Any other loss function for a Gaussian predictive model either ignores the variance (MSE) or is not proper (some ad hoc combinations of mean and variance losses).

The implementation parameterizes \(\log \sigma^2\) rather than \(\sigma^2\) to avoid numerical issues.

def gaussian_nll(mu, logvar, y):
    """Gaussian negative log-likelihood loss.

    Args:
        mu:     (batch, 1) predicted means
        logvar: (batch, 1) predicted log-variances
        y:      (batch, 1) observed values

    Returns:
        scalar loss
    """
    # Clamp variance for numerical stability
    var = torch.exp(logvar).clamp(min=1e-6)
    return 0.5 * (logvar + (y - mu)**2 / var).mean()

14.4 Temporal Cross-Validation Conformal

Even with the Gaussian NLL loss and a \(K = 5\) ensemble, the learned variance underestimates the true prediction error. On the lake temperature data, the Gaussian prediction intervals \(\bar{\mu}(x) \pm z_{0.95} \cdot \sigma_{\text{ens}}(x)\) achieve only 49% coverage at 90% nominal. The learned \(\hat{\sigma}\) is systematically too small by a factor of roughly 2.6.

This is not surprising. Neural networks are known to produce overconfident uncertainty estimates (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, and Blundell 2017), particularly when the test distribution differs from training. In time-series forecasting, the test period (future observations) is inherently different from the training period (past observations), so we should expect calibration to degrade.

The solution is conformal prediction — a distribution-free framework that produces prediction intervals with finite-sample coverage guarantees (Vovk, Gammerman, and Shafer 2005; Lei et al. 2018). Rather than relying on the network’s variance estimate, we use honest out-of-sample residuals to determine the width of prediction intervals.

We introduced conformal prediction in Section 11.2, where we showed that split conformal provides marginal coverage guarantees under exchangeability. Here the challenge is different: we are working with time-series data where exchangeability fails, and we need a conformal procedure that accounts for temporal structure. Temporal cross-validation provides a middle ground: it does not require strict exchangeability, but it generates honest out-of-sample residuals that are approximately representative of the errors the model will make on future data.

In its simplest form, split conformal prediction works as follows:

  1. Split the data into training and calibration sets.
  2. Train the model on the training set.
  3. Compute the absolute residuals \(|y_i - \hat{\mu}(x_i)|\) on the calibration set.
  4. Set \(\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}\) = the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of these residuals.
  5. The prediction interval for a new \(x\) is \(\hat{\mu}(x) \pm \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}\).

Under exchangeability of calibration and test data, this achieves at least \((1-\alpha)\) coverage in finite samples (Vovk, Gammerman, and Shafer 2005). More precisely, if the calibration observations \((x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_m, y_m)\) and the test observation \((x_{m+1}, y_{m+1})\) are exchangeable, then

\[ \mathbb{P}\bigl(y_{m+1} \in \hat{C}_{1-\alpha}(x_{m+1})\bigr) \geq 1 - \alpha. \tag{14.12}\]

The guarantee is distribution-free: it does not depend on the model being well-specified or the errors being Gaussian. It holds for any base model \(\hat{\mu}\), any data distribution, and any sample size \(m\). The only requirement is exchangeability between calibration and test observations.

Standard conformal prediction assumes that calibration and test observations are exchangeable — essentially, that they are drawn from the same distribution. This assumption fails in time-series forecasting: the test period is in the future, and temporal trends, seasonality, and distribution shift mean that future observations are not exchangeable with past ones.

Barber et al. (2023) develop extensions of conformal prediction beyond exchangeability, using weighted quantiles that account for distribution shift. Our approach is simpler: we use temporal cross-validation to generate honest out-of-sample residuals that span different time periods, approximately capturing the error structure across seasonal and inter-annual variability.

14.4.1 Temporal cross-validation in detail

The standard \(k\)-fold cross-validation randomly partitions the data into \(k\) folds. For time series, random partitioning destroys temporal structure: a model trained on data from 2021 and 2023 and validated on 2022 has illegitimate access to the future. Temporal cross-validation respects the arrow of time by using consecutive folds ordered chronologically.

We divide the training dates (all dates before 2022-06-11) into \(J = 5\) consecutive temporal folds. Each fold corresponds to a contiguous block of dates, preserving the seasonal and inter-annual patterns within each fold. The folds are approximately equal in size, with each containing roughly 20% of the training observations.

Importantly, the folds are defined by date, not by observation index, so all depths and horizons associated with a given date fall into the same fold. This prevents data leakage: a model never sees any observation from the same date during training and calibration simultaneously. If folds were defined by observation index, a model could train on the depth-1 observation from June 15 while predicting the depth-5 observation from the same date, creating an optimistic bias in the out-of-fold residuals.

Two common variants of temporal CV exist: rolling and expanding. In rolling CV, each fold uses a fixed-width training window, discarding the oldest data as the validation window advances. In expanding CV, the training set grows with each fold, always starting from the earliest date and extending to just before the validation fold. Rolling CV is appropriate when the data-generating process is non-stationary and old data may be misleading. Expanding CV is appropriate when the process is approximately stationary and more data is always better.

For the lake temperature problem, we use expanding CV for two reasons. First, the lake’s thermal dynamics are governed by stable physical processes (solar radiation, wind mixing, stratification), so older observations remain informative and should not be discarded. Second, the expanding variant produces more training data for later folds, which improves the quality of later cross-validation models and makes the out-of-fold residuals more representative of the final model’s performance.

The number of folds \(J\) trades off between two desiderata. More folds mean more out-of-fold predictions, which improves the precision of the conformal quantile estimates. But more folds also mean less training data per fold, which degrades the quality of each fold’s model and makes the out-of-fold residuals noisier.

We found \(J = 5\) to be a good compromise for this dataset. With fewer folds (\(J = 3\)), the conformal quantiles were noisy due to limited out-of-fold predictions per horizon — roughly 3,800 per horizon, which is adequate but not abundant for quantile estimation at the 90th percentile. With more folds (\(J = 10\)), the fold models had too little training data and produced systematically larger residuals than the final full-data model, biasing the conformal quantiles upward and producing overly conservative intervals. The \(J = 5\) setting strikes a balance: each fold model trains on 80% of the data (enough for a well-fitted model), and the pooled out-of-fold residuals provide about 5,700 observations per horizon (enough for stable 90th-percentile estimation).

Our calibration pipeline has two phases.

Phase 1 (calibration via temporal CV). We divide the training dates into \(J = 5\) consecutive temporal folds. For each fold \(j\):

  1. Train a single heteroscedastic MLP on the remaining 4 folds (3000 epochs — fewer than the final model, since we need speed, not optimality).
  2. Predict on the held-out fold.
  3. Collect the absolute residuals \(r_i = |y_i - \hat{\mu}_{\text{cv}}(x_i)|\) for all observations in the held-out fold.

After all 5 folds, we have out-of-fold residuals for every training observation. These residuals are honest out-of-sample predictions: each observation’s residual was computed by a model that never saw that observation during training.

Phase 2 (final ensemble). We train the \(K = 5\) ensemble on all training data (5000 epochs) for optimal point predictions. The final prediction intervals use the conformal quantiles from Phase 1:

\[ \hat{C}_{1-\alpha}(x) = \bar{\mu}_{\text{ens}}(x) \pm \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}(h), \tag{14.13}\]

where \(h\) is the forecast horizon associated with test point \(x\) and \(\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}(h)\) is the per-horizon conformal quantile (described in Section 14.5).

The separation of calibration (Phase 1) and prediction (Phase 2) is deliberate. The calibration models are disposable — we discard them after extracting residuals. The final ensemble is trained on all available data for the best point predictions. The conformal quantiles bridge the two phases, providing distribution-free uncertainty from the calibration models applied to the final ensemble’s predictions.

14.4.2 Before and after conformal calibration

The impact of conformal calibration on the lake temperature data is dramatic. Before conformal calibration, the ensemble’s Gaussian prediction intervals — \(\bar{\mu}(x) \pm z_{0.95} \cdot \sigma_{\text{ens}}(x)\) — achieve only 49% coverage at the 90% nominal level. The learned variance \(\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ens}}(x)\) systematically underestimates the true error: on average, \(\hat{\sigma}_{\text{ens}}\) is approximately \(0.69\,{}^{\circ}\)C, while the actual RMSE is \(1.22\,{}^{\circ}\)C. The ratio of true error to predicted standard deviation is about 1.8, meaning the network is overconfident by nearly a factor of 2 in standard deviation (and a factor of 2.6 in variance, since \(1.8^2 \approx 3.2\)).

Why does the learned variance underestimate so badly? There are several contributing factors. First, the training data and test data come from different time periods, and the error structure may differ between them — this is the temporal distribution shift inherent in time-series forecasting. Second, the Gaussian NLL loss is averaged over all training observations, and the resulting variance estimate reflects the average error structure during training, not the potentially larger errors that occur during the test period. Third, deep ensembles are known to be underconfident in the variance dimension even when they provide good mean predictions, because the diversity among ensemble members underestimates the true model uncertainty (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, and Blundell 2017).

After applying per-horizon conformal quantiles from the 5-fold temporal CV, coverage rises to 90.2% overall. At individual horizons, coverage ranges from 94.3% at \(h = 1\) (slightly conservative) to 86.7% at \(h = 30\) (slightly liberal). The average PI width is \(3.80\,{}^{\circ}\)C, compared to \(2.28\,{}^{\circ}\)C for the uncalibrated Gaussian intervals. The conformal intervals are wider — necessarily so, since the Gaussian intervals were far too narrow — but they are honest. The width is determined entirely by the empirical error distribution, not by the network’s internal variance estimate.

The slight over-coverage at short horizons (\(h = 1\): 94.3% vs. 90% target) and under-coverage at long horizons (\(h = 30\): 86.7% vs. 90% target) reflect the imperfect exchangeability between cross-validation residuals and test-set errors. The temporal CV residuals span multiple years and seasons, but the test period (starting June 2022) has its own specific characteristics that are not perfectly represented in the training period. In particular, the test period includes a summer-to-spring transition that may have different error characteristics than the winter-to-fall transitions that dominate certain training folds. Nonetheless, the per-horizon conformal intervals are far closer to the 90% target than either the Gaussian intervals (49%) or the IQN intervals (11–34%).

The improvement is not just about achieving the right coverage on average — it is about producing intervals that are useful for decision-making. An interval with 49% coverage is essentially uninformative: it excludes the majority of possible outcomes, giving the decision-maker a false sense of precision. An interval with 90% coverage captures the relevant range of uncertainty, allowing the decision-maker to plan for plausible worst-case scenarios.

The ensemble’s Gaussian intervals have the right shape (wider at long horizons, narrower at short horizons) but the wrong scale. Conformal calibration corrects the scale by replacing the parametric variance with empirical residual quantiles. The cost is a loss of adaptivity: the conformal intervals are constant-width within each horizon group, while the Gaussian intervals vary point-by-point. But the gain in calibration more than compensates for this loss of adaptivity, at least on this benchmark.

Show code (41 lines) 
def compute_conformal_quantiles(residuals, horizons, alpha=0.10,
                                max_horizon=30):
    """Compute per-horizon conformal quantiles from CV residuals.

    Args:
        residuals: (n,) array of absolute residuals |y - mu_hat|
        horizons:  (n,) array of integer horizon values
        alpha:     miscoverage rate (0.10 for 90% PI)
        max_horizon: maximum horizon value

    Returns:
        q_dict: dict mapping horizon -> quantile value
    """
    q_level = 100 * (1 - alpha)
    q_dict = {}
    # For finite-sample correctness, use the (ceil((1-α)(n+1)))-th order statistic
    # instead of the plain (1-α) empirical quantile. The difference is negligible
    # for large calibration sets.
    fallback = np.percentile(residuals, q_level)

    for h in range(1, max_horizon + 1):
        mask = horizons == h
        if mask.sum() > 20:
            q_dict[h] = np.percentile(residuals[mask], q_level)
        else:
            q_dict[h] = fallback  # fallback to pooled quantile

    return q_dict

A natural alternative is conformalized quantile regression (CQR) (Romano, Patterson, and Candès 2019), which uses the nonconformity score \(\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x_i) - y_i,\; y_i - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x_i))\). Taking the empirical \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of this score and adding it symmetrically to the predicted interval produces adaptive prediction intervals: wider where the base quantile predictions are spread apart, narrower where they are tight.

On the lake temperature data, we chose absolute residuals instead. The reason is that the learned \(\hat{\sigma}(x)\) is unreliable in magnitude — it underestimates by a factor of 2.6 on average. While the relative pattern (larger \(\hat{\sigma}\) at long horizons, smaller at short horizons) is roughly correct, the absolute scale is wrong. Standardizing by an unreliable \(\hat{\sigma}\) introduces noise without improving calibration.

The absolute-residual approach produces constant-width prediction intervals per horizon: every test point with the same forecast horizon \(h\) gets the same PI width, regardless of its other features. This is a limitation — ideally, intervals would be wider during rapid temperature changes and narrower during stable periods — but it is a robust default when the variance estimate is untrustworthy.

We note one further design choice: the conformal quantiles in Phase 1 are computed from models trained for 3000 epochs, while the final ensemble in Phase 2 uses 5000 epochs. The shorter training in Phase 1 is deliberate: we need speed (five folds must be trained), and the calibration models need only be good enough to produce representative residuals. The residual distribution from a 3000-epoch model is similar to that from a 5000-epoch model, because most of the RMSE improvement occurs in the first 2000 epochs. The final 3000 epochs of training polish the predictions but do not substantially change the error distribution. This observation also suggests a practical shortcut for large-scale applications: if computational budget is limited, train the calibration models for even fewer epochs (e.g., 1000) and reserve the budget for the final ensemble. The conformal quantiles will be slightly noisier but the coverage guarantee is unaffected, since it depends only on the rank order of the residuals, not on their absolute values.

14.5 Per-Horizon Calibration

A single conformal quantile \(\hat{q}_{0.90}\) for all test points would ignore the fact that forecasting difficulty varies dramatically with the forecast horizon \(h\). A 1-day-ahead forecast (\(h = 1\)) is far more accurate than a 30-day-ahead forecast (\(h = 30\)), and the prediction intervals should reflect this.

Consider the alternative: a single pooled \(\hat{q}_{0.90}\) computed from all out-of-fold residuals regardless of horizon. On the lake data, this pooled quantile is approximately \(1.9\,{}^{\circ}\)C. Applying it uniformly would produce 90% coverage overall, but the coverage would be unevenly distributed:

At \(h = 1\), the errors are small (RMSE \(\approx 0.6\,{}^{\circ}\)C), so the interval \(\pm 1.9\,{}^{\circ}\)C is far too wide and coverage would be near 100%, wasting prediction interval width on a forecast that is already quite accurate. At \(h = 30\), the errors are large (RMSE \(\approx 1.5\,{}^{\circ}\)C), so the same interval is too narrow and coverage would drop below 80%, producing unreliable uncertainty estimates precisely where they are most needed — at long horizons where decisions (such as whether to deploy algal bloom mitigation measures) depend critically on the forecast uncertainty.

The pooled approach achieves the target on average but produces intervals that are simultaneously too wide at short horizons and too narrow at long horizons. This is analogous to the distinction between marginal and conditional coverage in conformal prediction: marginal coverage averages over the input distribution, while conditional coverage holds at each input value. The pooled conformal quantile provides marginal coverage but poor conditional coverage; per-horizon conformal quantiles provide approximate conditional coverage within each horizon group.

The fix is straightforward: compute separate conformal quantiles for each horizon.

\[ \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}(h) = \text{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}\bigl(\{|y_i - \hat{\mu}_{\text{cv}}(x_i)| : h_i = h\}\bigr), \tag{14.14}\]

where the quantile is taken over all out-of-fold residuals at horizon \(h\). The prediction interval for a test point with horizon \(h\) is then

\[ \hat{C}_{1-\alpha}(x, h) = \bar{\mu}_{\text{ens}}(x) \pm \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}(h). \tag{14.15}\]

On the lake temperature data, the per-horizon quantiles range from \(\hat{q}_{0.90}(1) \approx 1.2\,{}^{\circ}\)C (narrow intervals at short horizons) to \(\hat{q}_{0.90}(30) \approx 2.2\,{}^{\circ}\)C (wider intervals at long horizons). This naturally captures the growth of forecast uncertainty with horizon length. The quantiles grow approximately as \(\sqrt{h}\), consistent with the error growth pattern expected from a random-walk-like accumulation of forecast errors. This is not imposed by the method — it emerges from the empirical residual distribution. If the true error growth followed a different pattern (e.g., linear in \(h\), or saturating at long horizons), the per-horizon quantiles would automatically capture that pattern. This adaptivity is a key advantage of the nonparametric conformal approach over parametric variance models that assume a specific functional form for the error growth.

The per-horizon approach is a form of group-conditional conformal prediction. Barber et al. (2023) analyze this setting and show that group-conditional coverage guarantees hold as long as each group has enough calibration observations. On the lake data, each of the 30 horizons has approximately \(172{,}714 / 30 \approx 5{,}700\) out-of-fold residuals — more than enough for stable quantile estimation.

Per-horizon calibration is appropriate when there is a discrete grouping variable (like forecast horizon) that strongly influences the error distribution. It produces constant-width intervals within each group, which is easy to interpret and communicate. In operational settings, this simplicity has real value: a forecaster can tell a decision-maker “our 10-day forecast has an uncertainty of \(\pm 1.8\,{}^{\circ}\)C” without needing to explain that the interval varies point by point.

For continuous adaptation — where you want the PI width to vary smoothly with all input features — conformalized quantile regression (Romano, Patterson, and Candès 2019) is the right tool. This uses the score \(\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x) - y, y - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x))\) as the nonconformity measure, producing intervals that are wider where the model’s predicted quantiles are spread apart and narrower where they are tight. However, as noted in Section 14.4, this requires reliable base quantile estimates, which we do not have from the IQN on the lake data due to the gradient collapse documented in Section 14.1.

Moving from simplest to most flexible, the three available approaches are: pooled conformal (a single \(\hat{q}\) for all test points — easiest but ignores heterogeneity), group conformal (separate \(\hat{q}(g)\) for each group \(g\) such as forecast horizon — captures known heterogeneity), and conformalized quantile regression (Romano, Patterson, and Candès 2019) (using \(\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x) - y,\; y - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x))\) as the nonconformity score — fully adaptive but requires reliable base quantile estimates).

For the lake temperature problem, group conformal (option 2) with horizon as the grouping variable is the sweet spot. The horizon is the single most important determinant of forecast difficulty on this dataset, and stratifying by horizon captures the dominant source of heterogeneity in the error distribution. Additional stratification — by depth or season, for example — would further improve conditional coverage, but at the cost of fewer calibration observations per stratum and noisier quantile estimates. With 30 horizons and 10 depths, there would be 300 strata, each with only about 19 observations per stratum — too few for reliable quantile estimation. The practitioner must balance the granularity of stratification against the sample size available per stratum, and a single grouping variable is often the right tradeoff.

14.6.1 The GPBC baseline

GPBC (Holthuijzen et al. 2025) is a three-stage Gaussian process pipeline, and understanding its structure helps clarify what our single-stage approach replaces.

In Stage 1 (surrogate emulation), a Vecchia-approximated GP (Katzfuss and Guinness 2021) emulates the GLM ensemble mean and standard deviation as functions of the input features. The Vecchia approximation reduces the GP’s \(\mathcal{O}(n^3)\) cost to \(\mathcal{O}(n \, m^2)\) by conditioning on the \(m\) nearest neighbors, making GP inference feasible on the full dataset. This stage produces smoothed versions of the GLM outputs, filtering out the Monte Carlo noise inherent in the ensemble.

In Stage 2 (stochastic kriging), a second GP models the spatially varying noise variance. Standard GP regression assumes homoscedastic noise; stochastic kriging generalizes this to heteroscedastic noise by learning a separate variance function. This is necessary because forecast uncertainty varies strongly with depth and horizon: near-surface temperatures are more predictable than deep-water temperatures, and short-horizon forecasts have smaller errors than long-horizon ones.

In Stage 3 (bias correction), a third GP corrects the systematic discrepancy between GLM predictions and observations, following the Kennedy–O’Hagan framework (Kennedy and O’Hagan 2001). Process-based models like GLM have systematic biases — for example, they may consistently overpredict temperature during fall turnover — and the bias GP learns this correction from training data.

The pipeline is refit daily over the 366-day validation period: for a forecast issued on date \(t\), all three stages have access to observations through date \(t\). This gives GPBC a substantial information advantage over our neural network, which is trained once on pre-validation data and never sees observations from the validation period.

Our GBC calibration pipeline replaces these three stages with a single end-to-end architecture. The heteroscedastic MLP absorbs the surrogate emulation (Stage 1) and bias correction (Stage 3) into one nonlinear mapping from inputs to temperature. The log-variance head replaces the stochastic kriging (Stage 2) with a learned variance function. Conformal calibration replaces the GP’s parametric uncertainty intervals with distribution-free intervals. The result is a pipeline that is conceptually simpler, computationally cheaper, and — as we show below — more accurate.

In the GPBC pipeline, errors from Stage 1 propagate to Stage 2 and Stage 3. If the Vecchia GP in Stage 1 produces a slightly biased emulation of the GLM mean, the bias GP in Stage 3 must correct not only the GLM’s own bias but also the Stage 1 emulation error. This error propagation is difficult to quantify and can lead to unpredictable interactions between stages. The neural network avoids this entirely: the mean head learns the combined mapping from inputs to temperature in a single optimization, and all errors are attributable to a single model rather than distributed across three interacting stages.

Another advantage of the single-stage approach relates to uncertainty propagation. In the GPBC pipeline, the predictive uncertainty is a composite of GP posterior variances from all three stages, combined under assumptions about their independence. These assumptions may be violated when, for example, the bias GP’s uncertainty is correlated with the surrogate GP’s emulation error. The het-MLP ensemble, by contrast, produces a single variance estimate \(\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ens}}(x)\) that captures all sources of uncertainty jointly. Although this estimate is miscalibrated (as we discussed in Section 14.4.2), the conformal calibration corrects the calibration without requiring assumptions about the independence of uncertainty components.

The comparison between GPBC and our approach also highlights a broader theme from Section 7.2: the GP’s cubic scaling in the number of observations makes it difficult to use the full dataset directly. The Vecchia approximation addresses this but introduces its own hyperparameters (the number of neighbors \(m\), the ordering of observations) that affect the quality of the approximation. The choice of \(m\) involves a bias-variance tradeoff: smaller \(m\) reduces computational cost but increases the approximation error, while larger \(m\) provides a better approximation but approaches the cubic cost of exact GP inference. The neural network simply processes all \(n = 172{,}714\) training observations through mini-batch SGD, with no approximation needed. The computational cost is linear in \(n\) per epoch, and the quality of the fit improves monotonically with more data (no approximation error).

The stochastic kriging model assumes a particular functional form for the variance as a function of the inputs, and this functional form may not adequately capture the nonlinear growth of forecast error at long lead times. Our conformal intervals avoid this problem entirely: the per-horizon quantiles are computed from actual out-of-sample errors at each horizon, so they automatically reflect the true error scale, whatever its functional form.

From a Bayesian perspective, the comparison illustrates a tension between prior information and data. GPBC uses a GP prior, which encodes a smoothness assumption and provides a principled mechanism for uncertainty quantification. But the GP’s kernel structure (typically separable, with a limited number of hyperparameters) constrains the class of functions it can represent. The neural network has no explicit prior — its inductive bias comes from the architecture and training procedure — but its functional flexibility allows it to learn complex interactions that the GP cannot represent. On this benchmark, flexibility wins over prior structure, and the conformal post-processing provides the uncertainty quantification that the neural network’s architecture alone cannot deliver.

This is a concrete instance of the broader theme we discussed in Section 7.2: when \(n\) is large and the function is complex, neural networks outperform GPs because they can learn problem-specific representations rather than relying on a fixed kernel. The lake dataset, with \(n > 170{,}000\) training observations and complex nonlinear interactions between depth, season, and horizon, is exactly the regime where we would expect the neural network to dominate. The GP’s advantage — principled Bayesian uncertainty from the posterior distribution — is precisely what conformal calibration replaces. We give up the GP’s coherent posterior in exchange for distribution-free coverage guarantees that do not require any Bayesian machinery.

14.6.4 Computational comparison

The entire GBC pipeline trains in 22 minutes on a single A100 GPU:

Phase 1 (5-fold CV) takes 7 minutes (5 folds \(\times\) 3000 epochs each), and Phase 2 (\(K = 5\) ensemble) takes 15 minutes (5 members \(\times\) 5000 epochs each).

GPBC requires 366 daily refits over the validation period, each involving Vecchia GP, stochastic kriging, and bias GP estimation. The GBC pipeline replaces this entire three-stage model with a single end-to-end architecture trained once. Beyond wall-clock time, the operational simplicity is a major advantage: deploying a static neural network ensemble requires no infrastructure for daily retraining, no monitoring of GP hyperparameter convergence, and no fallback procedures when a daily refit fails.

The computational scaling properties also favor the neural network approach. The GPBC pipeline’s daily refit cost grows with the size of the observation history: as more data accumulates, the Vecchia GP must condition on more neighbors, and the stochastic kriging model has a larger design matrix. The neural network, by contrast, is trained once and inference is a single forward pass — \(\mathcal{O}(1)\) per prediction, regardless of the size of the training set. For operational forecasting systems that must produce predictions in real time, this constant-cost inference is a significant practical advantage.

There is also a software engineering argument. The three-stage GPBC pipeline requires careful orchestration of three separate GP models, each with its own hyperparameter estimation, convergence checks, and failure modes. If the Vecchia GP in Stage 1 fails to converge on a particular day (due to numerical issues or unusual input data), the downstream stages inherit this failure. The neural network pipeline has a single training procedure and a single failure mode (convergence of SGD), making it easier to monitor and debug in production.

From a reproducibility standpoint, the neural network approach is also more straightforward. The training procedure is fully determined by the architecture, the loss function, the optimizer, and the random seed. Given these, the trained model is reproducible (up to floating-point non-determinism). The GPBC pipeline, with its three stages and daily refitting schedule, has a much larger configuration space and more opportunities for implementation choices that affect results but are not fully documented in a paper.

14.6.5 Limitations

Several limitations should be noted.

The most significant limitation is the constant-width prediction intervals within each horizon group. Our conformal PI width depends only on \(h\) and not on other features. This means that a 10-day-ahead forecast issued during a stable summer stratification period receives the same PI width as a 10-day-ahead forecast issued during fall turnover, when temperatures are changing rapidly and unpredictably. GPBC’s intervals adapt to local conditions through the stochastic kriging variance model, which is a genuine advantage when the variance function is well-estimated. The conformalized quantile regression framework of Romano, Patterson, and Candès (2019) could address this limitation by using the score \(\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x) - y, y - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x))\), producing intervals that adapt to the width of the predicted central interval rather than requiring a separate variance estimate. However, as we documented in Section 14.4.2, the learned variance underestimates the true error by a factor of 2.6, so standardized residuals do not improve calibration on this dataset. Future work could explore training the variance head with a different objective — such as a quantile loss on the squared residuals — that might produce more reliable relative variance estimates even if the absolute scale remains wrong. Another promising direction is to combine the het-MLP’s adaptive intervals with conformal calibration: use the CQR score \(\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x) - y, y - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x))\) of Romano, Patterson, and Candès (2019), where the quantiles \(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x)\) and \(\hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x)\) are derived from the het-MLP’s predicted mean and variance as \(\hat{\mu}(x) \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat{\sigma}(x)\), after first recalibrating \(\hat{\sigma}\) by a multiplicative constant estimated from the out-of-fold residuals. This hybrid approach would inherit both the adaptivity of the learned variance and the coverage guarantees of conformal prediction.

We also evaluate on FCR only, so the architecture choices (hidden dimension 256, 3 layers, \(K = 5\) members) may require adjustment for lakes with different thermal dynamics. A deep tropical lake with minimal seasonal variation would present a very different forecasting problem from FCR, and the optimal model configuration might differ substantially. The general methodology, however, is transferable: train a het-MLP ensemble with Gaussian NLL, apply temporal CV conformal calibration, and compute per-horizon (or per-group) conformal quantiles. The specific hyperparameters are secondary to the pipeline structure. The grouping variable for conformal quantiles need not be forecast horizon — for a lake with strong depth dependence, stratifying by depth (or depth \(\times\) horizon) might be more appropriate.

Finally, the neural network does not enforce physical constraints such as monotonic temperature decrease with depth during stratification. During summer, surface waters warm while deep waters remain cold, creating a thermocline. A physics-informed architecture could enforce this monotonicity constraint, potentially improving generalization at depths and horizons with sparse training data. Process-informed neural networks have shown promise in related lake modeling applications, and incorporating such constraints into the het-MLP architecture is a promising direction for future work. One approach would be to add a penalty term to the loss function that penalizes temperature inversions (surface colder than deep water) during stratified periods. Another would be to use a monotone neural network architecture that enforces \(\partial \hat{\mu} / \partial \text{depth} \leq 0\) by construction during stratified periods.

14.7 Diagnostic Checklist

We have now seen that IQN fails silently on high-SNR data and that even the het-MLP ensemble’s Gaussian intervals can be poorly calibrated. Unlike point prediction, where a single metric like RMSE reveals whether the model is working, uncertainty quantification can fail in subtle ways. A model can produce reasonable-looking prediction intervals that have entirely wrong coverage. It can produce correct marginal coverage while being wildly miscalibrated conditionally. It can appear well-calibrated on the training distribution but fail catastrophically under distribution shift. The IQN failure documented in Section 14.1 is a particularly insidious example: the model produces excellent point predictions (RMSE competitive with the GP baseline) while its uncertainty estimates are essentially meaningless (34% coverage at 90% nominal). Without a systematic diagnostic procedure, this failure would be invisible from point-prediction metrics alone.

The checklist follows four sequential diagnostic steps. If the first diagnostic reveals a problem, fix it before proceeding to the second. Each diagnostic has a specific indicator (what to look for), a diagnosis (what the problem is), and a remedy (what to do about it).

The flowchart should be read top-to-bottom: each check either passes (leading to the next check) or fails (leading to a specific remedy). We now describe each diagnostic in detail, including practical guidance on how to implement each check.

Step 1: PIT histogram. The probability integral transform (PIT) maps each observation \(y_i\) through the model’s predicted CDF: \(u_i = \hat{F}(y_i \mid x_i)\). If the model is correctly specified, the PIT values \(u_1, \ldots, u_n\) should be approximately \(\text{Uniform}(0, 1)\). For a het-MLP, the PIT is computed as \(u_i = \Phi((y_i - \hat{\mu}(x_i)) / \hat{\sigma}(x_i))\) where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF. For an IQN, it is computed by inverting the learned quantile function: \(u_i = \hat{F}(y_i \mid x_i)\) where \(\hat{F}\) is the CDF implied by the quantile predictions. In practice, we find \(\tau^*\) by binary search over \(\tau \in (0,1)\) such that \(\hat{Q}(\tau^* \mid x_i) = y_i\), then set \(u_i = \tau^*\). The package provides a utility for this numerical inversion. A U-shaped PIT histogram indicates underdispersion (prediction intervals too narrow). An inverted-U shape indicates overdispersion (intervals too wide). A skewed histogram indicates bias in the location. If the PIT histogram is far from uniform, the model is fundamentally misspecified — neither adding capacity nor applying conformal calibration will fix the underlying problem. Revisit the architecture, the input features, or the loss function.

Step 2: CRPS vs. epoch. The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) is the integral of the pinball loss across all quantile levels, as we defined in Section 5.5.2. Monitoring CRPS on a validation set during training reveals whether the model has converged. If the CRPS plateaus at a high value early in training, the model is underfitting: it lacks the capacity or the training time to learn the conditional distribution. Increase the network width or depth, train for more epochs, or reduce regularization. If the CRPS is still decreasing at the end of training, the model needs more epochs. For het-MLP models, the analogous diagnostic is to monitor the Gaussian NLL on a held-out validation set: if it is still decreasing, the model has not converged. A useful secondary check is to plot the validation NLL alongside the training NLL: if the training NLL continues to decrease while the validation NLL increases, the model is overfitting and needs regularization (dropout, weight decay, or early stopping).

Step 3: Quantile crossings. For any \(\tau_1 < \tau_2\), the true quantile function satisfies \(Q(\tau_1 \mid x) \leq Q(\tau_2 \mid x)\) for all \(x\). The IQN does not enforce this monotonicity constraint, and quantile crossings — where the 0.05 quantile exceeds the 0.95 quantile, for example — indicate that the architecture is struggling to produce a coherent distributional prediction. Occasional crossings (less than 1% of test points) are harmless and can be fixed by post-hoc sorting of the predicted quantiles. Frequent crossings (more than 5%) signal a fundamental architectural problem: the multiplicative \(\tau\)-embedding cannot express the required distributional structure at this SNR level. On the lake data, the standard IQN produces crossings at 12% of test points, confirming the severity of the failure. The remedy is either a monotone output layer that enforces non-crossing by construction (such as the increasing-softmax architecture of Chernozhukov, Fernández-Val, and Galichon (2010)), or a switch to the het-MLP approach where the Gaussian distributional assumption prevents crossings entirely, since \(\hat{\mu}(x) - z \hat{\sigma}(x) < \hat{\mu}(x) + z \hat{\sigma}(x)\) for any \(z > 0\) and \(\hat{\sigma}(x) > 0\).

Step 4: Coverage. Finally, check the empirical coverage of the prediction intervals at the nominal level. If coverage is within 5 percentage points of the target (e.g., 85–95% at a 90% nominal level), the model is reasonably well-calibrated and can be deployed, possibly with minor conformal adjustments. If coverage is off by more than 5 percentage points — as in our IQN experiments where coverage was 11–34% at a 90% target — conformal post-processing is essential. When checking coverage, it is important to check it at multiple nominal levels (e.g., 50%, 80%, 90%, 95%) and across subgroups of the data (e.g., different horizons, different seasons, different depth layers). A model that achieves 90% overall coverage but only 60% coverage at long horizons is miscalibrated in a practically important way that overall metrics miss.

The flowchart in Figure 14.4 is designed to be followed sequentially. A model that fails Step 1 should not be conformally calibrated (Step 4) — conformal calibration can correct the scale of prediction intervals but not their shape, so a fundamentally misspecified model will produce conformal intervals that are correctly sized on average but poorly adapted to the local structure. Fix the model first, then calibrate.

The checklist is intentionally sequential because each diagnostic is most informative when the earlier checks have passed. A model with severe misspecification (Step 1) will have poor CRPS (Step 2), frequent quantile crossings (Step 3), and bad coverage (Step 4) — but fixing the misspecification may resolve all four problems simultaneously. Diagnosing the root cause saves time.

To make the checklist concrete, consider how it applies to our two main examples. On the motorcycle data from Section 5.7, the IQN passes all four steps: the PIT histogram is approximately uniform (the distributional model captures the heteroscedastic, non-Gaussian structure), CRPS converges to a low value, quantile crossings are rare (less than 0.5% of test points), and coverage is within 2 percentage points of nominal. The IQN is the right tool for that problem. On the lake temperature data, the IQN fails at Step 4: coverage is 34% at a 90% target, and the PI width does not vary meaningfully across nominal levels. The checklist directs us to conformal post-processing, and since the underlying model is fundamentally unable to produce spread (an architecture problem, not just a calibration problem), it further suggests switching to the het-MLP. Following the checklist systematically would have saved us the time spent trying larger IQNs and two-stage approaches — all of which failed for the same architectural reason.

14.8 When to Use Conformal Post-Processing

The conformal calibration pipeline developed in Section 14.4 is not always necessary. When should a practitioner invest in conformal post-processing, and when is the model’s native uncertainty sufficient?

Conformal prediction provides a marginal coverage guarantee: the probability that the prediction interval contains a randomly drawn test point is at least \(1 - \alpha\). This guarantee is averaged over both the randomness in the calibration data and the randomness in the test point. It does not guarantee conditional coverage — that is, it does not promise that coverage is \(1 - \alpha\) for every value of \(x\). As Section 11.2 discussed, this distinction matters: marginal coverage is weaker but always achievable, while conditional coverage is stronger but generally impossible without distributional assumptions. Vovk (2012) showed that exact conditional coverage is impossible in a distribution-free setting: for any calibration procedure, there exists a distribution under which the conditional coverage at some \(x\) is arbitrarily far from the nominal level.

We recommend conformal post-processing in three situations.

First, when the model’s native coverage is off by more than 5 percentage points from the target. If a het-MLP ensemble achieves 84% coverage at a 90% nominal level, the miscalibration is modest and may be acceptable for exploratory analysis. But if IQN achieves 34% coverage at a 90% target, conformal calibration is essential — the native intervals are meaningless. The lake temperature benchmark is an extreme case of this scenario: the IQN coverage of 11–34% is so far from the target that conformal calibration on IQN outputs would produce extremely wide intervals. The better approach, as we demonstrated, is to switch to a het-MLP first and then apply conformal calibration to get the scale right. The 5-percentage-point threshold is a pragmatic guideline, not a hard rule: the decision should also consider the downstream consequences of miscalibration and the cost of implementing conformal calibration (which requires a separate calibration phase and slightly more complex deployment infrastructure).

Second, when the deployment context requires guaranteed coverage. In operational forecasting — such as the lake temperature forecasts used for water treatment decisions — the consequences of systematically overconfident intervals can be severe. A water treatment plant that relies on an 85% coverage interval for algal bloom prediction needs that 85% to be real, not an aspirational lower bound. Conformal calibration converts model-dependent coverage claims into distribution-free guarantees (up to the exchangeability assumption), which is a substantial improvement in reliability. The coverage guarantee is marginal — it holds on average over the randomness in both the calibration data and the test point — but marginal coverage with a rigorous guarantee is often more useful in practice than conditional coverage with no guarantee at all.

Third, when the data is non-stationary. In time-series applications, the test distribution systematically differs from the training distribution: new seasons bring new weather patterns, lake conditions evolve, and the physics-based simulator may be updated. The temporal CV approach in Section 14.4.1 generates calibration residuals that span multiple seasons and years, providing some robustness to distributional shift. Without conformal calibration, the model’s native variance estimates are trained on historical data and may be poorly calibrated for future conditions. This is perhaps the strongest argument for conformal post-processing in time-series settings: it provides a principled way to account for the fact that the future is different from the past, without requiring an explicit model of how the distribution shifts.

Conversely, conformal post-processing may be unnecessary or even counterproductive in two situations. When the model is already well-calibrated (coverage within 2–3 percentage points of the target), conformal calibration adds little value and may slightly widen the intervals. And when conditional coverage is paramount, the constant-width conformal intervals may be less useful than adaptive intervals from a well-calibrated model. In such cases, the conformalized quantile regression approach of Romano, Patterson, and Candès (2019) — which we discussed in Section 11.2.1 — provides adaptive intervals with marginal coverage guarantees by using \(\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x) - y, y - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x))\) as the nonconformity score, combining the best of both worlds, provided the base model’s quantile estimates are reasonable.

A rule of thumb from our experience across the benchmarks in this book. On the motorcycle and Phantom datasets, the IQN produces well-calibrated intervals without any conformal correction — the distributional flexibility of the quantile regression approach is both necessary and sufficient. On the lake temperature data, conformal calibration is essential because the base model’s uncertainty estimates are unreliable. The dividing line is, roughly, whether the base model’s coverage is within 5 percentage points of the target. Below that threshold, conformal calibration is the right investment. Above it, the effort is better spent on improving the base model itself.

Conformal calibration should be re-run periodically in non-stationary settings. The conformal quantiles are computed from a specific set of out-of-fold residuals, which reflect the error structure at a particular point in time. As the data-generating process evolves — due to climate change, changes in reservoir management, or updates to the physics-based simulator — the old conformal quantiles may become stale. A practical approach is to update the conformal quantiles (Phase 1 only, without retraining the ensemble) whenever new data accumulates, using an expanding window of recent forecast–observation pairs. This provides a lightweight way to maintain calibration over time without the full cost of retraining. In our lake temperature application, this could be implemented by collecting forecast–observation pairs during each season and updating the per-horizon conformal quantiles at the start of each new season, using all accumulated data. The neural network ensemble would be retrained only when the accumulated performance degradation (measured by RMSE or coverage) exceeds a predetermined threshold.

14.9 Summary: When to Use What

This chapter has presented a diagnostic and a remedy for IQN failure on high-SNR data. The broader message is that the choice of uncertainty quantification method should match the problem characteristics.

The diagnostic is the signal-to-noise ratio. When fitting a good point predictor leaves residuals that are a small fraction of the response range, the IQN architecture will struggle to produce distributional spread. In that regime, the het-MLP ensemble with conformal calibration is the right tool.

The diagnostic checklist in Section 14.7 provides a systematic procedure for evaluating any neural network UQ method on a new dataset. Follow it sequentially: check the PIT histogram for distributional misspecification, monitor CRPS convergence for underfitting, inspect quantile crossings for architectural problems, and validate coverage for calibration failure. If the model passes all four checks, deploy it with confidence. If it fails at any step, the checklist points to the appropriate remedy. The entire diagnostic procedure can be run in minutes, even on large datasets, and should be standard practice before deploying any neural network uncertainty model.

This chapter completes a natural arc. We began in Chapter 5 with the IQN as a universal tool for conditional distributions, demonstrated its power on heteroscedastic (Section 5.7) and multimodal (Chapter 8) problems, and used it to build surrogates (Chapter 7) and drive active learning (Chapter 9). Here we have shown its limits and provided an alternative. The GBC framework is broader than any single architecture: it encompasses IQN when distributional flexibility is needed, het-MLP ensembles when parametric models suffice, and conformal calibration when distribution-free guarantees are required.

Uncertainty quantification is harder than point prediction, and no single method works everywhere. Conformal calibration provides a safety net for either approach, converting model-dependent uncertainty estimates into distribution-free prediction intervals with honest coverage.

In the language of the GBC framework, this chapter demonstrates that “generative Bayesian computation” is not synonymous with “implicit quantile networks.” The GBC philosophy — simulate from the model, train a neural network, produce posterior quantiles — encompasses multiple architectures and calibration strategies. The het-MLP ensemble with conformal calibration is a GBC method: it produces calibrated predictive distributions without evaluating a likelihood, without running MCMC chains, and without specifying a prior. It just happens to use Gaussian NLL rather than pinball loss, and conformal quantiles rather than IQN quantiles. The computational philosophy is the same; only the implementation differs.

# When IQN Fails: Calibration and Remedies {#sec-calibration}

Throughout this book, we have presented the Implicit Quantile Network as a versatile tool. It handles heteroscedastic noise on the motorcycle data (@sec-iqn-motorcycle), models jump discontinuities with boundary augmentation (@sec-jumps), serves as a competitive surrogate for computer experiments (@sec-surrogates), and drives active learning via randomized-prior ensembles (@sec-active-learning). The natural temptation is to reach for IQN on every regression problem with uncertainty quantification.

This chapter is about when that temptation leads you astray.

We document a systematic failure of IQN on a real forecasting task --- lake temperature prediction --- where the response surface has high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). In this regime, the IQN's multiplicative $\tau$-embedding architecture collapses: all quantile levels converge to the same well-fitted conditional mean, and prediction intervals shrink to near-zero width. Coverage at the 90% nominal level drops to 11--34%. Increasing model capacity makes the problem *worse*, not better.

The remedy is a two-part strategy. First, we replace IQN with a *heteroscedastic MLP ensemble* trained with Gaussian negative log-likelihood, which explicitly parameterizes both the conditional mean and variance. Second, we apply *conformal calibration* via temporal cross-validation to produce distribution-free prediction intervals with honest coverage. The combination --- which we call the GBC calibration pipeline --- achieves 90.2% coverage on the lake temperature benchmark while reducing RMSE by 7.1% relative to a three-stage Gaussian process baseline.

The lesson is practical: not every problem is best served by quantile regression. When the conditional distribution is well-described by a location-scale family with small residual variance, a parametric variance model with conformal post-processing is the appropriate choice. Knowing when to abandon IQN is as important as knowing how to use it.

We first analyze the IQN failure mode in @sec-cal-failure, including a mathematical explanation of the gradient collapse mechanism. We then develop the het-MLP ensemble (@sec-cal-het-mlp) and its Gaussian NLL training loss (@sec-cal-nll), followed by the conformal calibration pipeline (@sec-cal-conformal) with per-horizon adjustments (@sec-cal-per-horizon). The full lake temperature benchmark results appear in @sec-cal-lake, and we close with a diagnostic checklist (@sec-cal-diagnostics) and guidance on when conformal post-processing is warranted (@sec-cal-when-conformal).


## IQN Failure on High Signal-to-Noise Data {#sec-cal-failure}

To understand the failure, we need to revisit the IQN architecture from @sec-iqn-architecture. The IQN maps $(x, \tau)$ to a quantile prediction via

$$
\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x) = g_\theta\bigl(\psi_\theta(x) \odot \phi_\theta(\tau)\bigr),
$$ {#eq-cal-iqn-arch}

where $\psi_\theta(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the covariate encoder, $\phi_\theta(\tau) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the cosine-based quantile encoder, and $\odot$ denotes elementwise multiplication. The multiplicative merge is the architectural key: the quantile level $\tau$ *modulates* the feature representation of $x$, allowing different parts of the distribution to activate different features.

This works well when the conditional distribution $Y \mid X = x$ has substantial spread --- for example, the motorcycle data where the variance changes by two orders of magnitude, or the Phantom function where bimodal regions produce wide distributional spread. In these settings, the $\tau$-modulation has real work to do: $\hat{Q}(0.05 \mid x)$ and $\hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x)$ must differ substantially, and the multiplicative architecture can express this difference.

Recall that the cosine embedding from @eq-cosine-embedding maps $\tau \in [0, 1]$ to

$$
\phi(\tau) = \text{ReLU}\bigl(W_\tau [\cos(\pi j \tau)]_{j=0}^{d-1} + b_\tau\bigr) \in \mathbb{R}^d.
$$

When the conditional distribution has large spread, $\phi(\tau)$ must vary substantially with $\tau$: the features activated at $\tau = 0.05$ must differ meaningfully from those activated at $\tau = 0.95$. The multiplicative merge $\psi(x) \odot \phi(\tau)$ then produces genuinely different representations for different quantile levels, and the output layer maps these distinct representations to different quantile predictions. The architecture is expressive enough to capture this variation.

But consider what happens when the signal-to-noise ratio is high. If $Y \mid X = x$ has small variance relative to the range of $Y$ --- say the standard deviation of $Y \mid X$ is 2% of the total range of $Y$ --- then the quantile function $F^{-1}_{Y|X}(\tau)$ is nearly flat in $\tau$. All quantile levels produce nearly the same value: the conditional mean. The optimal $\phi_\theta(\tau)$ is close to a constant vector, and the $\tau$-modulation reduces to a negligible perturbation of the already well-fitted covariate representation $\psi_\theta(x)$.

During training, the loss landscape strongly rewards fitting the conditional mean --- this is where most of the predictive signal lives. Consider the expected pinball loss: $\mathbb{E}[\rho_\tau(Y - \hat{Q}(\tau \mid X))]$ is minimized when $\hat{Q}(\tau \mid X) = F^{-1}_{Y|X}(\tau)$. When the conditional distribution is nearly a point mass, the quantile function is nearly flat, and the pinball loss is dominated by the mean-fit component. The gradients that would push $\phi_\theta(\tau)$ to create distributional spread are tiny compared to the gradients that improve the mean fit. Standard SGD converges to a solution where $\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)$ is nearly constant in $\tau$: the network has effectively learned a point prediction, not a distributional one.

### Gradient collapse mechanism {#sec-cal-gradient-collapse}

We analyze the gradient signal in the multiplicative architecture. Let $z = \psi_\theta(x) \odot \phi_\theta(\tau) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be the merged representation, and suppose the output layer is linear: $\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x) = w^\top z + b$. The pinball loss for quantile level $\tau$ is $\rho_\tau(y - \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x))$, and the gradient with respect to the quantile-encoder parameters $\theta_\tau$ (the parameters of $\phi_\theta$) is

$$
\frac{\partial \rho_\tau}{\partial \theta_\tau} = -\bigl(\tau - \mathbf{1}[y < \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)]\bigr) \cdot w^\top \left(\psi_\theta(x) \odot \frac{\partial \phi_\theta(\tau)}{\partial \theta_\tau}\right).
$$ {#eq-cal-gradient}

The relevant factor is the Hadamard product $\psi_\theta(x) \odot \frac{\partial \phi_\theta(\tau)}{\partial \theta_\tau}$. To produce the correct quantile function in the high-SNR regime, $\phi_\theta(\tau)$ must be close to a constant vector $\mathbf{c}$ for all $\tau$, plus a small perturbation $\epsilon(\tau)$ that encodes the residual spread:

$$
\phi_\theta(\tau) \approx \mathbf{c} + \epsilon(\tau), \quad \|\epsilon(\tau)\| \ll \|\mathbf{c}\|.
$$ {#eq-cal-phi-approx}

The perturbation $\epsilon(\tau)$ does all the distributional work: it creates the difference between $\hat{Q}(0.05 \mid x)$ and $\hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x)$. But the gradient of $\epsilon(\tau)$ must fight against the dominant gradient from the constant part $\mathbf{c}$, which drives $\psi_\theta(x) \odot \mathbf{c}$ toward the conditional mean. In the loss landscape, the direction that improves the mean fit (adjusting $\psi_\theta$ and $\mathbf{c}$) has gradient magnitude proportional to the signal strength, while the direction that increases distributional spread (adjusting $\epsilon(\tau)$) has gradient magnitude proportional to the noise level $\sigma$. When the signal-to-noise ratio is large, the distributional gradient is swamped. Throughout this chapter we use the *range-based* SNR as a practical diagnostic:
$$
\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}} = \left(\frac{\text{range of } y}{\hat\sigma_{\text{residual}}}\right)^2,
$$ {#eq-cal-snr}
which is easy to compute from data. On the lake data, $\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}} \approx (26/1.2)^2 \approx 470$, so the mean-fit gradient is roughly $\sqrt{470} \approx 22$ times larger than the spread gradient, making it nearly impossible for SGD to allocate learning capacity to the distributional component.

::: {.callout-note}
The formal variance-based definition $\mathrm{SNR} = \mathrm{Var}(\mu(X))/\sigma^2$ requires knowledge of the true conditional mean $\mu(x)$, which is unavailable before fitting the model. The range-based proxy @eq-cal-snr is an upper bound (range $\geq 2\,\mathrm{sd}$) and is conservative: if $\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}}$ is large, the variance-based SNR is also large.
:::

There is a second, more insidious mechanism that reinforces the first. Suppose the network has learned the conditional mean well, so that $\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x) \approx \mu(x)$ for all $\tau$. The residual $y - \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x) \approx y - \mu(x) = \epsilon$ is independent of $\tau$. The indicator $\mathbf{1}[y < \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)]$ then does not vary with $\tau$ for a given $(x, y)$ pair: it is always 0 or always 1 depending on the sign of $\epsilon$. The gradient signal $\tau - \mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ that should push different quantile levels apart becomes effectively constant across $\tau$, and the quantile encoder receives no informative gradient about how to differentiate the quantile levels. This is the *gradient collapse*: the very success of the mean fit eliminates the gradient signal that would produce distributional spread.

Consider training with $\tau = 0.95$. The gradient signal is $\tau - \mathbf{1}[y < \hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x)] = 0.95 - \mathbf{1}[\epsilon < 0]$. If $\hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x) \approx \mu(x)$, then the indicator is 0 half the time (when $\epsilon > 0$) and 1 half the time (when $\epsilon < 0$), giving an average gradient signal of $0.95 - 0.5 = 0.45$. This is a valid signal --- it tells the network to push $\hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x)$ upward. But through the multiplicative architecture, this signal must modulate $\phi(0.95)$ relative to the dominant covariate representation $\psi(x)$, and the required modulation is tiny relative to $\|\psi(x)\|$ when the SNR is high. The gradient exists but is too small to be effective.

This analysis explains why increasing model capacity makes the problem worse. A larger network fits $\mu(x)$ more tightly, reducing the residuals further and making the gradient collapse more severe. It also explains why the two-stage approach (MLP for mean, IQN on residuals) provides only marginal improvement: even after subtracting the fitted mean, the residuals have low variance relative to the IQN's capacity to express distributional spread via the multiplicative architecture, and the same gradient collapse applies at a smaller scale.

An alternative architecture could avoid this failure. If the quantile level $\tau$ entered *additively* rather than multiplicatively --- for example, $\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x) = \psi_\theta(x) + \sigma_\theta(x) \cdot \Phi^{-1}(\tau)$, where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the standard normal quantile function --- then the spread would be controlled by $\sigma_\theta(x)$, which can be trained independently of the mean fit. This is precisely the het-MLP architecture of @sec-cal-het-mlp, which parameterizes the mean and variance separately. The multiplicative architecture of @eq-cal-iqn-arch was designed for distributional reinforcement learning [@dabney2018implicit], where the value distribution is intrinsically spread out and the $\tau$-modulation must express complex multimodal shapes. In regression with tight Gaussian-like noise, the architectural flexibility is not needed, and its coupling of mean and spread creates the gradient collapse we observe.

Define the *quantile spread ratio* (QSR) as the ratio of the learned 90% PI width to the oracle PI width:

$$
\text{QSR} = \frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n [\hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x_i) - \hat{Q}(0.05 \mid x_i)]}{2 \times 1.645 \times \hat{\sigma}_{\text{residual}}},
$$ {#eq-cal-qsr}

where $\hat{\sigma}_{\text{residual}}$ is the standard deviation of the IQN's median residuals. A well-calibrated model has $\text{QSR} \approx 1$. On the motorcycle data, the IQN achieves $\text{QSR} \approx 0.95$ (excellent). On the lake data, the standard IQN achieves $\text{QSR} \approx 0.28$ (catastrophic collapse). The QSR is a useful single-number summary of IQN calibration quality. We recommend computing the QSR as a routine diagnostic whenever training an IQN on a new dataset. If $\text{QSR} < 0.5$, the IQN is suffering from gradient collapse and should be replaced with a het-MLP. If $0.5 \leq \text{QSR} \leq 0.8$, the IQN is partially collapsed and conformal post-processing may rescue it. If $\text{QSR} > 0.8$, the IQN is working well and no intervention is needed.

### Empirical evidence {#sec-cal-empirical-failure}

The gradient collapse manifests dramatically on real data. We encountered this failure on the *Falling Creek Reservoir* lake temperature forecasting task [@holthuijzen2025synthesizing]. The dataset consists of $N = 281{,}764$ records with 6 input features (day of year, water depth, forecast horizon, a lagged temperature covariate, and two outputs from a physics-based lake simulator) predicting observed water temperature. The response surface is smooth and highly predictable: the physics-based simulator already captures most of the variance, and the statistical model's job is to refine the simulator's output and quantify residual uncertainty.

To quantify the SNR on this dataset: the total range of observed temperatures is approximately 4--30${}^\circ$C (a range of 26${}^\circ$C), and the standard deviation of the best residuals (after a well-fitted MLP) is approximately $1.2\,{}^{\circ}$C. Using @eq-cal-snr, $\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}} \approx (26/1.2)^2 \approx 470$, or equivalently $\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}}} \approx 22$ in standard-deviation terms. This is well above the 10:1 threshold (in sd terms) we suggested for IQN failure.

We tested three IQN variants. The *standard IQN* (256$\times$3, 3000 epochs) achieved RMSE $= 1.310\,{}^{\circ}$C --- competitive with the GP baseline --- but the 90% prediction interval coverage was only *34%*, with an average PI width of just $1.0\,{}^{\circ}$C; the network learned an excellent point prediction and negligible distributional spread. The *larger IQN* (512$\times$4, 8000 epochs) performed worse on both fronts: RMSE $= 1.404\,{}^{\circ}$C and coverage dropped to *11%*, because more capacity allowed the covariate encoder to fit the mean more tightly, leaving even less room for $\tau$-modulation. The *two-stage MLP + IQN* (first stage: MLP for mean; second stage: IQN on residuals) reduced stage 1 RMSE to $1.281\,{}^{\circ}$C but coverage remained at only *29%* --- even operating on residuals, the multiplicative architecture could not produce enough spread.

All three variants produce reasonable point predictions but catastrophically miscalibrated prediction intervals. The 90% PI width should be roughly $\pm 1.645 \sigma$ around the mean; on this data, the required $\sigma \approx 1.1\,{}^{\circ}$C, implying a PI width of $\approx 3.6\,{}^{\circ}$C. The IQN's actual width of $1.0\,{}^{\circ}$C is a factor of 3.6 too narrow. The IQN's 90% prediction interval captures only about one-third of the observations it should, making the uncertainty estimate useless for decision-making. A reservoir manager relying on these intervals would be systematically surprised by observations outside the predicted range, which could lead to inappropriate operational decisions --- for example, failing to prepare for temperature exceedances that trigger algal blooms because the model predicted narrow intervals suggesting stable conditions.

This failure mode provides a useful diagnostic. Before committing to IQN for a new problem, compute $\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}}$ from @eq-cal-snr after fitting a quick point predictor. If $\sqrt{\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{range}}}$ is large --- say greater than 10 --- the IQN's multiplicative $\tau$-architecture will likely under-disperse. The symptoms are coverage far below nominal (e.g., 30--50% at a 90% target), PI width that does not grow with the nominal coverage level (so that 80% and 95% PIs have nearly the same width), and a pattern where increasing model capacity makes coverage *worse*, not better.

When you see these symptoms, switch to a parametric variance model. The remainder of this chapter develops that alternative.

Contrast this failure with the IQN's success on the motorcycle data (@sec-iqn-motorcycle). On the motorcycle, the noise standard deviation varies from near zero (at $t < 15$ ms) to roughly 40 g's (at $t \approx 35$ ms), while the signal range is about 80 g's. The SNR is at most 4:1 in the high-variance region and effectively infinite in the low-variance region --- but the key point is that the distributional *structure* (heteroscedasticity, possible non-Gaussianity) is exactly what the IQN's $\tau$-modulation is designed to capture. On the lake data, by contrast, the distributional structure is simple (approximately Gaussian with slowly varying variance) and the IQN's flexibility is wasted. Match the model's flexibility to the complexity of the conditional distribution, not to the complexity of the mean function.

The IQN is the right tool for complex conditional distributions where the quantile function has non-Gaussian structure: heavy tails, multimodality, or rapid changes in variance. When the distribution is approximately Gaussian with high SNR, the IQN's architectural strength becomes a weakness, and the simpler het-MLP with conformal calibration is the appropriate choice. These methods are complementary tools, not competitors. The diagnostic question is: what is the complexity of the conditional distribution, and does the model architecture match it?



## Het-MLP Ensemble {#sec-cal-het-mlp}

The remedy is conceptually simple: instead of asking a single network to learn the entire quantile function, we explicitly parameterize the conditional mean and variance in a *heteroscedastic MLP* and combine multiple such models in an ensemble. This approach makes a stronger distributional assumption (Gaussianity) than the IQN (which is distribution-free), but the assumption buys us a crucial advantage: the variance is parameterized independently of the mean, so it cannot be suppressed by the gradient collapse mechanism.

Each ensemble member is a standard MLP with a shared backbone and two output heads:

$$
\text{backbone:} \quad h = f_\theta(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d,
$$
$$
\text{mean head:} \quad \hat{\mu}_k(x) = W_\mu^\top h + b_\mu \in \mathbb{R},
$$ {#eq-cal-mean-head}
$$
\text{log-variance head:} \quad \log \hat{\sigma}^2_k(x) = W_\sigma^\top h + b_\sigma \in \mathbb{R}.
$$ {#eq-cal-logvar-head}

The backbone $f_\theta$ is a standard MLP with ReLU activations. The mean head is a single linear layer. The log-variance head is also a single linear layer, parameterizing $\log \hat{\sigma}^2$ rather than $\hat{\sigma}^2$ directly. This log-parameterization ensures positivity of the variance without requiring a softplus or exponential activation in the forward pass (the exponential is applied only when computing the loss and during prediction).

The architectural choice of a *shared* backbone with *separate* heads is deliberate. The backbone learns a rich feature representation $h = f_\theta(x)$ that captures the nonlinear structure in the input-output relationship. The mean and variance heads then compute simple linear functions of this shared representation. This weight-sharing has two advantages: it is parameter-efficient (the bulk of the parameters are in the shared backbone, not duplicated across heads), and it encourages the backbone to learn features that are useful for both mean prediction and variance estimation. The alternative --- separate networks for mean and variance --- would require twice the parameters and would miss the opportunity to share representational structure.

The two-head architecture also provides a natural form of *multi-task learning*: the backbone must extract features that serve both the mean and variance heads. This is beneficial because the features that predict the mean (e.g., depth-season interactions, GLM bias patterns) are often correlated with the features that predict the variance (e.g., forecast difficulty varies with the same depth-season interactions). The shared backbone discovers these correlations automatically, producing a more coherent model than two independent networks.

The multi-task perspective also explains a subtle benefit of training with Gaussian NLL rather than MSE. With MSE, the backbone optimizes only for mean prediction accuracy. With Gaussian NLL, the backbone must simultaneously support both accurate mean predictions *and* accurate variance predictions, which encourages it to learn features that are informative about both the location and the spread of the conditional distribution. Empirically, this produces slightly better mean predictions than MSE alone, because the precision weighting $(y - \mu)^2 / \sigma^2$ focuses the optimization on observations where the model claims to be confident, implicitly downweighting noisy or outlying observations that would distort the MSE-optimal solution.

::: {.callout-tip appearance="simple" title="Definition 14.1: Heteroscedastic MLP"}
A heteroscedastic MLP with backbone dimension $d$ and $L$ hidden layers maps $x \in \mathbb{R}^p$ to a Gaussian predictive distribution:
$$
Y \mid X = x \;\sim\; \mathcal{N}\bigl(\hat{\mu}(x),\; \hat{\sigma}^2(x)\bigr),
$$
where $\hat{\mu}(x)$ and $\hat{\sigma}^2(x) = \exp(\text{logvar head output})$ are learned end-to-end.
:::

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np

torch.manual_seed(42)

class HetMLP(nn.Module):
    """Heteroscedastic MLP with mean and log-variance heads."""
    def __init__(self, xdim, hdim=256, nlayers=3):
        super().__init__()
        layers = [nn.Linear(xdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        for _ in range(nlayers - 1):
            layers += [nn.Linear(hdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        self.backbone = nn.Sequential(*layers)
        self.mean_head = nn.Linear(hdim, 1)
        self.logvar_head = nn.Linear(hdim, 1)

    def forward(self, x):
        h = self.backbone(x)
        return self.mean_head(h), self.logvar_head(h)
```

We train $K = 5$ ensemble members with identical architecture but different random seeds for weight initialization and mini-batch ordering. The diversity among members arises purely from the stochasticity of SGD --- no bootstrap resampling or explicit diversity mechanism is needed. This follows the deep ensemble approach of @lakshminarayanan2017simple. The choice of $K = 5$ balances computational cost against ensemble diversity: @lakshminarayanan2017simple found that most of the calibration improvement occurs between $K = 1$ and $K = 5$, with diminishing returns beyond $K = 5$. On the lake data, we confirmed this: $K = 3$ achieves RMSE of 1.226${}^\circ$C, $K = 5$ achieves 1.219${}^\circ$C, and $K = 10$ achieves 1.215${}^\circ$C. The improvement from 5 to 10 members is only 0.3%, not worth the doubled training time.

The ensemble predictive distribution combines aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty via the *law of total variance*:

$$
\bar{\mu}(x) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{\mu}_k(x),
$$ {#eq-cal-ens-mean}

$$
\sigma^2_{\text{ens}}(x) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{\sigma}^2_k(x)}_{\text{aleatoric (mean within-member variance)}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{\mu}^2_k(x) - \bar{\mu}^2(x)}_{\text{epistemic (between-member disagreement)}}.
$$ {#eq-cal-total-var}

The first term is the average learned variance across ensemble members --- the *aleatoric* component capturing irreducible noise. The second term is the variance of the member means --- the *epistemic* component capturing model uncertainty. In regions with abundant training data, the epistemic term is small (members agree). In extrapolation regions, the epistemic term grows (members disagree), naturally widening prediction intervals.

This decomposition is particularly useful for active learning, as we discussed in @sec-active-learning: the epistemic component identifies regions where the model is uncertain due to lack of data, guiding the selection of new observations. For the calibration task in this chapter, both components contribute to the total variance, and we do not need to distinguish between them --- the conformal calibration operates on the total prediction error, regardless of its source.

```python
def ensemble_predict(models, X):
    """Ensemble predictions with aleatoric + epistemic uncertainty.

    Args:
        models: list of HetMLP models
        X: (n, p) tensor

    Returns:
        ens_mean: (n,) array — ensemble mean prediction
        ens_std:  (n,) array — total standard deviation
    """
    K = len(models)
    all_mu = np.zeros((K, len(X)))
    all_var = np.zeros((K, len(X)))

    for k, m in enumerate(models):
        m.eval()
        with torch.no_grad():
            mu, logvar = m(X)
            all_mu[k] = mu.squeeze(1).numpy()
            all_var[k] = torch.exp(logvar.squeeze(1)).numpy()

    ens_mean = all_mu.mean(axis=0)
    ens_var = (all_var + all_mu**2).mean(axis=0) - ens_mean**2
    ens_std = np.sqrt(np.maximum(ens_var, 1e-6))
    return ens_mean, ens_std

# Demo with 3 untrained members
models_demo = [HetMLP(xdim=6) for _ in range(3)]
x_demo = torch.randn(100, 6)
mu_hat, std_hat = ensemble_predict(models_demo, x_demo)
print(f"Ensemble mean: shape={mu_hat.shape}, range=[{mu_hat.min():.3f}, {mu_hat.max():.3f}]")
print(f"Ensemble std:  shape={std_hat.shape}, range=[{std_hat.min():.3f}, {std_hat.max():.3f}]")
```

Why not use the Gaussian prediction intervals $\bar{\mu}(x) \pm z_{0.95} \cdot \sigma_{\text{ens}}(x)$ directly? On the lake temperature data, these intervals achieve only 49% coverage at the 90% nominal level. The learned variance underestimates the true error by a factor of about 2.6. This is a well-known phenomenon: neural network variance estimates are systematically overconfident [@lakshminarayanan2017simple]. We address this with conformal calibration in @sec-cal-conformal.

Despite this miscalibration, the het-MLP ensemble still provides two valuable properties that the IQN does not. First, it produces point predictions (the ensemble mean) that are as good as or better than the IQN's, because the mean head is trained to minimize the precision-weighted squared error rather than the pinball loss. The precision weighting means that the network focuses its capacity on regions where it claims high confidence, effectively prioritizing accuracy where it matters most. Second, the learned variance $\hat{\sigma}^2(x)$, while miscalibrated in absolute magnitude, captures relative differences in uncertainty across inputs. Observations at long horizons produce larger $\hat{\sigma}^2$ than observations at short horizons; deep-water predictions have larger $\hat{\sigma}^2$ than surface predictions. This relative ordering is useful even when the absolute scale is wrong, and it provides the foundation for more adaptive conformal methods when the variance estimate becomes reliable enough.

The ensemble diversity also provides a natural measure of model uncertainty that is absent from a single IQN. When the five ensemble members disagree strongly about the prediction at a particular input $x$, this indicates that the model is uncertain --- either because $x$ is in a region with sparse training data, or because the response surface is difficult to learn. This epistemic uncertainty signal can be used for active learning (as we discussed in @sec-active-learning) or for flagging predictions that warrant human review in an operational setting.

While the het-MLP ensemble's Gaussian intervals are too narrow on their own, the architecture provides the right building blocks --- good point predictions, relative variance ordering, and epistemic uncertainty from ensemble disagreement --- that conformal calibration can transform into honest, well-calibrated prediction intervals.


## Gaussian NLL Loss {#sec-cal-nll}

Training a heteroscedastic MLP requires a loss function that simultaneously optimizes the mean and variance predictions. The standard choice is the *Gaussian negative log-likelihood* (NLL).

If we model $Y \mid X = x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu(x), \sigma^2(x))$, the negative log-likelihood of a single observation $(x_i, y_i)$ is

$$
-\log p(y_i \mid x_i) = \frac{1}{2}\log \sigma^2(x_i) + \frac{(y_i - \mu(x_i))^2}{2\sigma^2(x_i)} + \frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi).
$$ {#eq-cal-nll-full}

Dropping the constant $\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi)$, the Gaussian NLL loss is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}(\mu, \sigma^2; x, y) = \frac{1}{2}\left(\log \sigma^2(x) + \frac{(y - \mu(x))^2}{\sigma^2(x)}\right).
$$ {#eq-cal-nll}

The two terms interact in a complementary way. The mean term $(y - \mu)^2 / \sigma^2$ is a weighted squared error with weight $1/\sigma^2$ (the precision): the network is penalized more for errors where it claims high confidence (small $\sigma^2$) and less where it claims low confidence (large $\sigma^2$). The variance regularizer $\log \sigma^2$ prevents the trivial solution of setting $\sigma^2 \to \infty$ (which would drive the mean term to zero) by imposing a log-variance penalty that grows as $\sigma^2$ increases, forcing the network to keep its variance estimates tight.

The balance between these terms is automatic: at the optimum, $\hat{\sigma}^2(x)$ equals the true conditional variance $\text{Var}(Y \mid X = x)$ (up to finite-sample error). This is a maximum likelihood estimator of a location-scale model. The proof is straightforward: take the expected loss $\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}]$, differentiate with respect to $\mu(x)$ and $\sigma^2(x)$ at each $x$, and solve the first-order conditions. The optimal $\mu(x)$ minimizes $\mathbb{E}[(Y - \mu(x))^2 / \sigma^2(x)]$, which gives $\mu(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]$. Substituting back, the optimal $\sigma^2(x)$ minimizes $\log \sigma^2(x) + \text{Var}(Y \mid X = x) / \sigma^2(x)$, which gives $\sigma^2(x) = \text{Var}(Y \mid X = x)$. Exercise 14.2 asks you to work through this derivation in detail.

Compare Gaussian NLL with the losses we have used elsewhere in this book.

*MSE loss* $\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} = (y - \hat{\mu}(x))^2$ learns only the conditional mean. There is no variance output and no mechanism for uncertainty quantification. The MSE-optimal prediction is $\hat{\mu}(x) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x]$, which tells us nothing about the spread of $Y \mid X$. This is the default loss in most deep learning applications, and for point prediction it is perfectly adequate. But for uncertainty quantification, we need more.

*Pinball loss* $\rho_\tau(y - \hat{Q}(\tau \mid x))$ learns individual quantiles, and the integrated pinball loss (CRPS) learns the full conditional distribution nonparametrically. The IQN uses this loss to great effect when the distributional structure is complex. But as we saw in @sec-cal-failure, the IQN architecture can fail to produce adequate spread on high-SNR data.

*Gaussian NLL* sits between these extremes. It makes a parametric assumption (Gaussian residuals) but learns both location and scale. When the Gaussian assumption is approximately correct --- as it often is for well-specified regression models with moderate residuals --- the NLL loss produces well-calibrated variance estimates. When the assumption is wrong, conformal calibration (@sec-cal-conformal) corrects the miscalibration.

We do not need the Gaussian assumption to be exactly correct. We need it to be *useful enough* that the learned $\hat{\sigma}^2(x)$ captures relative differences in uncertainty across $x$. Conformal calibration then adjusts the overall scale. This is a recurring theme in applied statistics: use a parametric model not because you believe it is true, but because it provides a useful scaffolding for inference. The Gaussian NLL gives us a mean and a variance; conformal calibration gives us honest intervals. Together, they produce well-calibrated uncertainty without requiring the data to be Gaussian.

There is a connection to scoring rules. A *proper scoring rule* is one that is minimized (in expectation) when the forecast distribution equals the true data-generating distribution. @gneiting2007strictly showed that for the Gaussian family, the negative log-likelihood is the unique proper scoring rule (up to affine transformation). This means that training with Gaussian NLL is guaranteed to produce the best Gaussian approximation to the true conditional distribution --- it simultaneously optimizes the mean, the variance, and their interaction. Any other loss function for a Gaussian predictive model either ignores the variance (MSE) or is not proper (some ad hoc combinations of mean and variance losses).

The implementation parameterizes $\log \sigma^2$ rather than $\sigma^2$ to avoid numerical issues.

```python
def gaussian_nll(mu, logvar, y):
    """Gaussian negative log-likelihood loss.

    Args:
        mu:     (batch, 1) predicted means
        logvar: (batch, 1) predicted log-variances
        y:      (batch, 1) observed values

    Returns:
        scalar loss
    """
    # Clamp variance for numerical stability
    var = torch.exp(logvar).clamp(min=1e-6)
    return 0.5 * (logvar + (y - mu)**2 / var).mean()

# Demo: compare loss at different variance levels
mu_demo = torch.tensor([[5.0]])
y_demo = torch.tensor([[5.5]])   # error = 0.5

for log_s2 in [-2.0, 0.0, 1.0, 2.0]:
    lv = torch.tensor([[log_s2]])
    loss = gaussian_nll(mu_demo, lv, y_demo)
    s2 = np.exp(log_s2)
    print(f"  sigma^2 = {s2:.3f}, error = 0.5 -> NLL = {loss.item():.3f}")
```

The output shows a key property of the NLL loss: setting $\sigma^2$ too small (overconfident) produces a large penalty from the $(y - \mu)^2 / \sigma^2$ term. Setting $\sigma^2$ too large (underconfident) produces a large penalty from $\log \sigma^2$. The minimum is near $\sigma^2 = (y - \mu)^2 = 0.25$, which is the maximum-likelihood estimate for a single observation. This self-correcting behavior is what makes the Gaussian NLL an attractive training objective: the network automatically learns to set its confidence level to match its actual prediction accuracy, at least on the training distribution.



## Temporal Cross-Validation Conformal {#sec-cal-conformal}

Even with the Gaussian NLL loss and a $K = 5$ ensemble, the learned variance underestimates the true prediction error. On the lake temperature data, the Gaussian prediction intervals $\bar{\mu}(x) \pm z_{0.95} \cdot \sigma_{\text{ens}}(x)$ achieve only 49% coverage at 90% nominal. The learned $\hat{\sigma}$ is systematically too small by a factor of roughly 2.6.

This is not surprising. Neural networks are known to produce overconfident uncertainty estimates [@lakshminarayanan2017simple], particularly when the test distribution differs from training. In time-series forecasting, the test period (future observations) is inherently different from the training period (past observations), so we should expect calibration to degrade.

The solution is **conformal prediction** --- a distribution-free framework that produces prediction intervals with finite-sample coverage guarantees [@vovk2005algorithmic; @lei2018distribution]. Rather than relying on the network's variance estimate, we use honest out-of-sample residuals to determine the width of prediction intervals.

We introduced conformal prediction in @sec-pred-conformal, where we showed that split conformal provides marginal coverage guarantees under exchangeability. Here the challenge is different: we are working with time-series data where exchangeability fails, and we need a conformal procedure that accounts for temporal structure. Temporal cross-validation provides a middle ground: it does not require strict exchangeability, but it generates honest out-of-sample residuals that are approximately representative of the errors the model will make on future data.

In its simplest form, split conformal prediction works as follows:

1. Split the data into training and calibration sets.
2. Train the model on the training set.
3. Compute the absolute residuals $|y_i - \hat{\mu}(x_i)|$ on the calibration set.
4. Set $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}$ = the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of these residuals.
5. The prediction interval for a new $x$ is $\hat{\mu}(x) \pm \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}$.

Under exchangeability of calibration and test data, this achieves at least $(1-\alpha)$ coverage in finite samples [@vovk2005algorithmic]. More precisely, if the calibration observations $(x_1, y_1), \ldots, (x_m, y_m)$ and the test observation $(x_{m+1}, y_{m+1})$ are exchangeable, then

$$
\mathbb{P}\bigl(y_{m+1} \in \hat{C}_{1-\alpha}(x_{m+1})\bigr) \geq 1 - \alpha.
$$ {#eq-cal-conformal-guarantee}

The guarantee is distribution-free: it does not depend on the model being well-specified or the errors being Gaussian. It holds for any base model $\hat{\mu}$, any data distribution, and any sample size $m$. The only requirement is exchangeability between calibration and test observations.

Standard conformal prediction assumes that calibration and test observations are exchangeable --- essentially, that they are drawn from the same distribution. This assumption fails in time-series forecasting: the test period is in the future, and temporal trends, seasonality, and distribution shift mean that future observations are *not* exchangeable with past ones.

@barber2023conformal develop extensions of conformal prediction beyond exchangeability, using weighted quantiles that account for distribution shift. Our approach is simpler: we use *temporal cross-validation* to generate honest out-of-sample residuals that span different time periods, approximately capturing the error structure across seasonal and inter-annual variability.

### Temporal cross-validation in detail {#sec-cal-temporal-cv}

The standard $k$-fold cross-validation randomly partitions the data into $k$ folds. For time series, random partitioning destroys temporal structure: a model trained on data from 2021 and 2023 and validated on 2022 has illegitimate access to the future. Temporal cross-validation respects the arrow of time by using *consecutive* folds ordered chronologically.

We divide the training dates (all dates before 2022-06-11) into $J = 5$ consecutive temporal folds. Each fold corresponds to a contiguous block of dates, preserving the seasonal and inter-annual patterns within each fold. The folds are approximately equal in size, with each containing roughly 20% of the training observations.

Importantly, the folds are defined by *date*, not by observation index, so all depths and horizons associated with a given date fall into the same fold. This prevents data leakage: a model never sees any observation from the same date during training and calibration simultaneously. If folds were defined by observation index, a model could train on the depth-1 observation from June 15 while predicting the depth-5 observation from the same date, creating an optimistic bias in the out-of-fold residuals.

Two common variants of temporal CV exist: *rolling* and *expanding*. In rolling CV, each fold uses a fixed-width training window, discarding the oldest data as the validation window advances. In expanding CV, the training set grows with each fold, always starting from the earliest date and extending to just before the validation fold. Rolling CV is appropriate when the data-generating process is non-stationary and old data may be misleading. Expanding CV is appropriate when the process is approximately stationary and more data is always better.

For the lake temperature problem, we use expanding CV for two reasons. First, the lake's thermal dynamics are governed by stable physical processes (solar radiation, wind mixing, stratification), so older observations remain informative and should not be discarded. Second, the expanding variant produces more training data for later folds, which improves the quality of later cross-validation models and makes the out-of-fold residuals more representative of the final model's performance.

The number of folds $J$ trades off between two desiderata. More folds mean more out-of-fold predictions, which improves the precision of the conformal quantile estimates. But more folds also mean less training data per fold, which degrades the quality of each fold's model and makes the out-of-fold residuals noisier.

We found $J = 5$ to be a good compromise for this dataset. With fewer folds ($J = 3$), the conformal quantiles were noisy due to limited out-of-fold predictions per horizon --- roughly 3,800 per horizon, which is adequate but not abundant for quantile estimation at the 90th percentile. With more folds ($J = 10$), the fold models had too little training data and produced systematically larger residuals than the final full-data model, biasing the conformal quantiles upward and producing overly conservative intervals. The $J = 5$ setting strikes a balance: each fold model trains on 80% of the data (enough for a well-fitted model), and the pooled out-of-fold residuals provide about 5,700 observations per horizon (enough for stable 90th-percentile estimation).

Our calibration pipeline has two phases.

*Phase 1 (calibration via temporal CV).* We divide the training dates into $J = 5$ consecutive temporal folds. For each fold $j$:

1. Train a single heteroscedastic MLP on the remaining 4 folds (3000 epochs --- fewer than the final model, since we need speed, not optimality).
2. Predict on the held-out fold.
3. Collect the absolute residuals $r_i = |y_i - \hat{\mu}_{\text{cv}}(x_i)|$ for all observations in the held-out fold.

After all 5 folds, we have out-of-fold residuals for every training observation. These residuals are honest out-of-sample predictions: each observation's residual was computed by a model that never saw that observation during training.

*Phase 2 (final ensemble).* We train the $K = 5$ ensemble on all training data (5000 epochs) for optimal point predictions. The final prediction intervals use the conformal quantiles from Phase 1:

$$
\hat{C}_{1-\alpha}(x) = \bar{\mu}_{\text{ens}}(x) \pm \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}(h),
$$ {#eq-cal-conformal-pi}

where $h$ is the forecast horizon associated with test point $x$ and $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}(h)$ is the per-horizon conformal quantile (described in @sec-cal-per-horizon).

The separation of calibration (Phase 1) and prediction (Phase 2) is deliberate. The calibration models are disposable --- we discard them after extracting residuals. The final ensemble is trained on all available data for the best point predictions. The conformal quantiles bridge the two phases, providing distribution-free uncertainty from the calibration models applied to the final ensemble's predictions.

### Before and after conformal calibration {#sec-cal-before-after}

The impact of conformal calibration on the lake temperature data is dramatic. Before conformal calibration, the ensemble's Gaussian prediction intervals --- $\bar{\mu}(x) \pm z_{0.95} \cdot \sigma_{\text{ens}}(x)$ --- achieve only 49% coverage at the 90% nominal level. The learned variance $\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ens}}(x)$ systematically underestimates the true error: on average, $\hat{\sigma}_{\text{ens}}$ is approximately $0.69\,{}^{\circ}$C, while the actual RMSE is $1.22\,{}^{\circ}$C. The ratio of true error to predicted standard deviation is about 1.8, meaning the network is overconfident by nearly a factor of 2 in standard deviation (and a factor of 2.6 in variance, since $1.8^2 \approx 3.2$).

Why does the learned variance underestimate so badly? There are several contributing factors. First, the training data and test data come from different time periods, and the error structure may differ between them --- this is the temporal distribution shift inherent in time-series forecasting. Second, the Gaussian NLL loss is averaged over all training observations, and the resulting variance estimate reflects the *average* error structure during training, not the potentially larger errors that occur during the test period. Third, deep ensembles are known to be underconfident in the variance dimension even when they provide good mean predictions, because the diversity among ensemble members underestimates the true model uncertainty [@lakshminarayanan2017simple].

After applying per-horizon conformal quantiles from the 5-fold temporal CV, coverage rises to 90.2% overall. At individual horizons, coverage ranges from 94.3% at $h = 1$ (slightly conservative) to 86.7% at $h = 30$ (slightly liberal). The average PI width is $3.80\,{}^{\circ}$C, compared to $2.28\,{}^{\circ}$C for the uncalibrated Gaussian intervals. The conformal intervals are wider --- necessarily so, since the Gaussian intervals were far too narrow --- but they are honest. The width is determined entirely by the empirical error distribution, not by the network's internal variance estimate.

The slight over-coverage at short horizons ($h = 1$: 94.3% vs. 90% target) and under-coverage at long horizons ($h = 30$: 86.7% vs. 90% target) reflect the imperfect exchangeability between cross-validation residuals and test-set errors. The temporal CV residuals span multiple years and seasons, but the test period (starting June 2022) has its own specific characteristics that are not perfectly represented in the training period. In particular, the test period includes a summer-to-spring transition that may have different error characteristics than the winter-to-fall transitions that dominate certain training folds. Nonetheless, the per-horizon conformal intervals are far closer to the 90% target than either the Gaussian intervals (49%) or the IQN intervals (11--34%).

The improvement is not just about achieving the right coverage on average --- it is about producing intervals that are *useful* for decision-making. An interval with 49% coverage is essentially uninformative: it excludes the majority of possible outcomes, giving the decision-maker a false sense of precision. An interval with 90% coverage captures the relevant range of uncertainty, allowing the decision-maker to plan for plausible worst-case scenarios.

The ensemble's Gaussian intervals have the right *shape* (wider at long horizons, narrower at short horizons) but the wrong *scale*. Conformal calibration corrects the scale by replacing the parametric variance with empirical residual quantiles. The cost is a loss of adaptivity: the conformal intervals are constant-width within each horizon group, while the Gaussian intervals vary point-by-point. But the gain in calibration more than compensates for this loss of adaptivity, at least on this benchmark.

```python
def compute_conformal_quantiles(residuals, horizons, alpha=0.10,
                                max_horizon=30):
    """Compute per-horizon conformal quantiles from CV residuals.

    Args:
        residuals: (n,) array of absolute residuals |y - mu_hat|
        horizons:  (n,) array of integer horizon values
        alpha:     miscoverage rate (0.10 for 90% PI)
        max_horizon: maximum horizon value

    Returns:
        q_dict: dict mapping horizon -> quantile value
    """
    q_level = 100 * (1 - alpha)
    q_dict = {}
    # For finite-sample correctness, use the (ceil((1-α)(n+1)))-th order statistic
    # instead of the plain (1-α) empirical quantile. The difference is negligible
    # for large calibration sets.
    fallback = np.percentile(residuals, q_level)

    for h in range(1, max_horizon + 1):
        mask = horizons == h
        if mask.sum() > 20:
            q_dict[h] = np.percentile(residuals[mask], q_level)
        else:
            q_dict[h] = fallback  # fallback to pooled quantile

    return q_dict
```

A natural alternative is conformalized quantile regression (CQR) [@romano2019conformalized], which uses the nonconformity score $\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x_i) - y_i,\; y_i - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x_i))$. Taking the empirical $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of this score and adding it symmetrically to the predicted interval produces adaptive prediction intervals: wider where the base quantile predictions are spread apart, narrower where they are tight.

On the lake temperature data, we chose absolute residuals instead. The reason is that the learned $\hat{\sigma}(x)$ is unreliable in magnitude --- it underestimates by a factor of 2.6 on average. While the *relative* pattern (larger $\hat{\sigma}$ at long horizons, smaller at short horizons) is roughly correct, the absolute scale is wrong. Standardizing by an unreliable $\hat{\sigma}$ introduces noise without improving calibration.

The absolute-residual approach produces *constant-width prediction intervals per horizon*: every test point with the same forecast horizon $h$ gets the same PI width, regardless of its other features. This is a limitation --- ideally, intervals would be wider during rapid temperature changes and narrower during stable periods --- but it is a robust default when the variance estimate is untrustworthy.

We note one further design choice: the conformal quantiles in Phase 1 are computed from models trained for 3000 epochs, while the final ensemble in Phase 2 uses 5000 epochs. The shorter training in Phase 1 is deliberate: we need speed (five folds must be trained), and the calibration models need only be *good enough* to produce representative residuals. The residual distribution from a 3000-epoch model is similar to that from a 5000-epoch model, because most of the RMSE improvement occurs in the first 2000 epochs. The final 3000 epochs of training polish the predictions but do not substantially change the error distribution. This observation also suggests a practical shortcut for large-scale applications: if computational budget is limited, train the calibration models for even fewer epochs (e.g., 1000) and reserve the budget for the final ensemble. The conformal quantiles will be slightly noisier but the coverage guarantee is unaffected, since it depends only on the rank order of the residuals, not on their absolute values.


## Per-Horizon Calibration {#sec-cal-per-horizon}

A single conformal quantile $\hat{q}_{0.90}$ for all test points would ignore the fact that forecasting difficulty varies dramatically with the forecast horizon $h$. A 1-day-ahead forecast ($h = 1$) is far more accurate than a 30-day-ahead forecast ($h = 30$), and the prediction intervals should reflect this.

Consider the alternative: a single pooled $\hat{q}_{0.90}$ computed from all out-of-fold residuals regardless of horizon. On the lake data, this pooled quantile is approximately $1.9\,{}^{\circ}$C. Applying it uniformly would produce 90% coverage overall, but the coverage would be unevenly distributed:

At $h = 1$, the errors are small (RMSE $\approx 0.6\,{}^{\circ}$C), so the interval $\pm 1.9\,{}^{\circ}$C is far too wide and coverage would be near 100%, wasting prediction interval width on a forecast that is already quite accurate. At $h = 30$, the errors are large (RMSE $\approx 1.5\,{}^{\circ}$C), so the same interval is too narrow and coverage would drop below 80%, producing unreliable uncertainty estimates precisely where they are most needed --- at long horizons where decisions (such as whether to deploy algal bloom mitigation measures) depend critically on the forecast uncertainty.

The pooled approach achieves the target *on average* but produces intervals that are simultaneously too wide at short horizons and too narrow at long horizons. This is analogous to the distinction between marginal and conditional coverage in conformal prediction: marginal coverage averages over the input distribution, while conditional coverage holds at each input value. The pooled conformal quantile provides marginal coverage but poor conditional coverage; per-horizon conformal quantiles provide approximate conditional coverage within each horizon group.

The fix is straightforward: compute separate conformal quantiles for each horizon.

$$
\hat{q}_{1-\alpha}(h) = \text{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}\bigl(\{|y_i - \hat{\mu}_{\text{cv}}(x_i)| : h_i = h\}\bigr),
$$ {#eq-cal-per-horizon-q}

where the quantile is taken over all out-of-fold residuals at horizon $h$. The prediction interval for a test point with horizon $h$ is then

$$
\hat{C}_{1-\alpha}(x, h) = \bar{\mu}_{\text{ens}}(x) \pm \hat{q}_{1-\alpha}(h).
$$ {#eq-cal-per-horizon-pi}

On the lake temperature data, the per-horizon quantiles range from $\hat{q}_{0.90}(1) \approx 1.2\,{}^{\circ}$C (narrow intervals at short horizons) to $\hat{q}_{0.90}(30) \approx 2.2\,{}^{\circ}$C (wider intervals at long horizons). This naturally captures the growth of forecast uncertainty with horizon length. The quantiles grow approximately as $\sqrt{h}$, consistent with the error growth pattern expected from a random-walk-like accumulation of forecast errors. This is not imposed by the method --- it emerges from the empirical residual distribution. If the true error growth followed a different pattern (e.g., linear in $h$, or saturating at long horizons), the per-horizon quantiles would automatically capture that pattern. This adaptivity is a key advantage of the nonparametric conformal approach over parametric variance models that assume a specific functional form for the error growth.

The per-horizon approach is a form of *group-conditional conformal prediction*. @barber2023conformal analyze this setting and show that group-conditional coverage guarantees hold as long as each group has enough calibration observations. On the lake data, each of the 30 horizons has approximately $172{,}714 / 30 \approx 5{,}700$ out-of-fold residuals --- more than enough for stable quantile estimation.

Per-horizon calibration is appropriate when there is a discrete grouping variable (like forecast horizon) that strongly influences the error distribution. It produces constant-width intervals within each group, which is easy to interpret and communicate. In operational settings, this simplicity has real value: a forecaster can tell a decision-maker "our 10-day forecast has an uncertainty of $\pm 1.8\,{}^{\circ}$C" without needing to explain that the interval varies point by point.

For continuous adaptation --- where you want the PI width to vary smoothly with all input features --- conformalized quantile regression [@romano2019conformalized] is the right tool. This uses the score $\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x) - y, y - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x))$ as the nonconformity measure, producing intervals that are wider where the model's predicted quantiles are spread apart and narrower where they are tight. However, as noted in @sec-cal-conformal, this requires reliable base quantile estimates, which we do not have from the IQN on the lake data due to the gradient collapse documented in @sec-cal-failure.

Moving from simplest to most flexible, the three available approaches are: pooled conformal (a single $\hat{q}$ for all test points --- easiest but ignores heterogeneity), group conformal (separate $\hat{q}(g)$ for each group $g$ such as forecast horizon --- captures known heterogeneity), and conformalized quantile regression [@romano2019conformalized] (using $\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x) - y,\; y - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x))$ as the nonconformity score --- fully adaptive but requires reliable base quantile estimates).

For the lake temperature problem, group conformal (option 2) with horizon as the grouping variable is the sweet spot. The horizon is the single most important determinant of forecast difficulty on this dataset, and stratifying by horizon captures the dominant source of heterogeneity in the error distribution. Additional stratification --- by depth or season, for example --- would further improve conditional coverage, but at the cost of fewer calibration observations per stratum and noisier quantile estimates. With 30 horizons and 10 depths, there would be 300 strata, each with only about 19 observations per stratum --- too few for reliable quantile estimation. The practitioner must balance the granularity of stratification against the sample size available per stratum, and a single grouping variable is often the right tradeoff.

### The GPBC baseline {#sec-cal-gpbc}

GPBC [@holthuijzen2025synthesizing] is a three-stage Gaussian process pipeline, and understanding its structure helps clarify what our single-stage approach replaces.

In Stage 1 (surrogate emulation), a Vecchia-approximated GP [@katzfuss2021general] emulates the GLM ensemble mean and standard deviation as functions of the input features. The Vecchia approximation reduces the GP's $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$ cost to $\mathcal{O}(n \, m^2)$ by conditioning on the $m$ nearest neighbors, making GP inference feasible on the full dataset. This stage produces smoothed versions of the GLM outputs, filtering out the Monte Carlo noise inherent in the ensemble.

In Stage 2 (stochastic kriging), a second GP models the spatially varying noise variance. Standard GP regression assumes homoscedastic noise; stochastic kriging generalizes this to heteroscedastic noise by learning a separate variance function. This is necessary because forecast uncertainty varies strongly with depth and horizon: near-surface temperatures are more predictable than deep-water temperatures, and short-horizon forecasts have smaller errors than long-horizon ones.

In Stage 3 (bias correction), a third GP corrects the systematic discrepancy between GLM predictions and observations, following the Kennedy--O'Hagan framework [@kennedy2001bayesian]. Process-based models like GLM have systematic biases --- for example, they may consistently overpredict temperature during fall turnover --- and the bias GP learns this correction from training data.

The pipeline is *refit daily* over the 366-day validation period: for a forecast issued on date $t$, all three stages have access to observations through date $t$. This gives GPBC a substantial information advantage over our neural network, which is trained once on pre-validation data and never sees observations from the validation period.

Our GBC calibration pipeline replaces these three stages with a single end-to-end architecture. The heteroscedastic MLP absorbs the surrogate emulation (Stage 1) and bias correction (Stage 3) into one nonlinear mapping from inputs to temperature. The log-variance head replaces the stochastic kriging (Stage 2) with a learned variance function. Conformal calibration replaces the GP's parametric uncertainty intervals with distribution-free intervals. The result is a pipeline that is conceptually simpler, computationally cheaper, and --- as we show below --- more accurate.

In the GPBC pipeline, errors from Stage 1 propagate to Stage 2 and Stage 3. If the Vecchia GP in Stage 1 produces a slightly biased emulation of the GLM mean, the bias GP in Stage 3 must correct not only the GLM's own bias but also the Stage 1 emulation error. This error propagation is difficult to quantify and can lead to unpredictable interactions between stages. The neural network avoids this entirely: the mean head learns the combined mapping from inputs to temperature in a single optimization, and all errors are attributable to a single model rather than distributed across three interacting stages.

Another advantage of the single-stage approach relates to uncertainty propagation. In the GPBC pipeline, the predictive uncertainty is a composite of GP posterior variances from all three stages, combined under assumptions about their independence. These assumptions may be violated when, for example, the bias GP's uncertainty is correlated with the surrogate GP's emulation error. The het-MLP ensemble, by contrast, produces a single variance estimate $\hat{\sigma}^2_{\text{ens}}(x)$ that captures all sources of uncertainty jointly. Although this estimate is miscalibrated (as we discussed in @sec-cal-before-after), the conformal calibration corrects the calibration without requiring assumptions about the independence of uncertainty components.

The comparison between GPBC and our approach also highlights a broader theme from @sec-surr-gbc: the GP's cubic scaling in the number of observations makes it difficult to use the full dataset directly. The Vecchia approximation addresses this but introduces its own hyperparameters (the number of neighbors $m$, the ordering of observations) that affect the quality of the approximation. The choice of $m$ involves a bias-variance tradeoff: smaller $m$ reduces computational cost but increases the approximation error, while larger $m$ provides a better approximation but approaches the cubic cost of exact GP inference. The neural network simply processes all $n = 172{,}714$ training observations through mini-batch SGD, with no approximation needed. The computational cost is linear in $n$ per epoch, and the quality of the fit improves monotonically with more data (no approximation error).

The following table summarizes the structural correspondence.

| GPBC component | GBC equivalent | Advantage |
|:---------------|:---------------|:----------|
| Vecchia GP surrogate (Stage 1) | MLP backbone + mean head | No kernel choice; learns interactions automatically |
| Stochastic kriging (Stage 2) | MLP backbone + log-variance head | Shared backbone; no separate heteroscedastic model |
| Bias GP (Stage 3) | Absorbed into mean head | Single optimization, no error propagation |
| GP posterior intervals | Conformal calibration | Distribution-free; no parametric assumptions |

### Computational comparison {#sec-cal-lake-computation}

The entire GBC pipeline trains in 22 minutes on a single A100 GPU:

Phase 1 (5-fold CV) takes 7 minutes (5 folds $\times$ 3000 epochs each), and Phase 2 ($K = 5$ ensemble) takes 15 minutes (5 members $\times$ 5000 epochs each).

GPBC requires 366 daily refits over the validation period, each involving Vecchia GP, stochastic kriging, and bias GP estimation. The GBC pipeline replaces this entire three-stage model with a single end-to-end architecture trained once. Beyond wall-clock time, the operational simplicity is a major advantage: deploying a static neural network ensemble requires no infrastructure for daily retraining, no monitoring of GP hyperparameter convergence, and no fallback procedures when a daily refit fails.

The computational scaling properties also favor the neural network approach. The GPBC pipeline's daily refit cost grows with the size of the observation history: as more data accumulates, the Vecchia GP must condition on more neighbors, and the stochastic kriging model has a larger design matrix. The neural network, by contrast, is trained once and inference is a single forward pass --- $\mathcal{O}(1)$ per prediction, regardless of the size of the training set. For operational forecasting systems that must produce predictions in real time, this constant-cost inference is a significant practical advantage.

There is also a software engineering argument. The three-stage GPBC pipeline requires careful orchestration of three separate GP models, each with its own hyperparameter estimation, convergence checks, and failure modes. If the Vecchia GP in Stage 1 fails to converge on a particular day (due to numerical issues or unusual input data), the downstream stages inherit this failure. The neural network pipeline has a single training procedure and a single failure mode (convergence of SGD), making it easier to monitor and debug in production.

From a reproducibility standpoint, the neural network approach is also more straightforward. The training procedure is fully determined by the architecture, the loss function, the optimizer, and the random seed. Given these, the trained model is reproducible (up to floating-point non-determinism). The GPBC pipeline, with its three stages and daily refitting schedule, has a much larger configuration space and more opportunities for implementation choices that affect results but are not fully documented in a paper.

### Limitations {#sec-cal-lake-limitations}

Several limitations should be noted.

The most significant limitation is the constant-width prediction intervals within each horizon group. Our conformal PI width depends only on $h$ and not on other features. This means that a 10-day-ahead forecast issued during a stable summer stratification period receives the same PI width as a 10-day-ahead forecast issued during fall turnover, when temperatures are changing rapidly and unpredictably. GPBC's intervals adapt to local conditions through the stochastic kriging variance model, which is a genuine advantage when the variance function is well-estimated. The conformalized quantile regression framework of @romano2019conformalized could address this limitation by using the score $\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x) - y, y - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x))$, producing intervals that adapt to the width of the predicted central interval rather than requiring a separate variance estimate. However, as we documented in @sec-cal-before-after, the learned variance underestimates the true error by a factor of 2.6, so standardized residuals do not improve calibration on this dataset. Future work could explore training the variance head with a different objective --- such as a quantile loss on the squared residuals --- that might produce more reliable relative variance estimates even if the absolute scale remains wrong. Another promising direction is to combine the het-MLP's adaptive intervals with conformal calibration: use the CQR score $\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x) - y, y - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x))$ of @romano2019conformalized, where the quantiles $\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x)$ and $\hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x)$ are derived from the het-MLP's predicted mean and variance as $\hat{\mu}(x) \pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\hat{\sigma}(x)$, after first recalibrating $\hat{\sigma}$ by a multiplicative constant estimated from the out-of-fold residuals. This hybrid approach would inherit both the adaptivity of the learned variance and the coverage guarantees of conformal prediction.

We also evaluate on FCR only, so the architecture choices (hidden dimension 256, 3 layers, $K = 5$ members) may require adjustment for lakes with different thermal dynamics. A deep tropical lake with minimal seasonal variation would present a very different forecasting problem from FCR, and the optimal model configuration might differ substantially. The general methodology, however, is transferable: train a het-MLP ensemble with Gaussian NLL, apply temporal CV conformal calibration, and compute per-horizon (or per-group) conformal quantiles. The specific hyperparameters are secondary to the pipeline structure. The grouping variable for conformal quantiles need not be forecast horizon --- for a lake with strong depth dependence, stratifying by depth (or depth $\times$ horizon) might be more appropriate.

Finally, the neural network does not enforce physical constraints such as monotonic temperature decrease with depth during stratification. During summer, surface waters warm while deep waters remain cold, creating a thermocline. A physics-informed architecture could enforce this monotonicity constraint, potentially improving generalization at depths and horizons with sparse training data. Process-informed neural networks have shown promise in related lake modeling applications, and incorporating such constraints into the het-MLP architecture is a promising direction for future work. One approach would be to add a penalty term to the loss function that penalizes temperature inversions (surface colder than deep water) during stratified periods. Another would be to use a monotone neural network architecture that enforces $\partial \hat{\mu} / \partial \text{depth} \leq 0$ by construction during stratified periods.


## Diagnostic Checklist {#sec-cal-diagnostics}

We have now seen that IQN fails silently on high-SNR data and that even the het-MLP ensemble's Gaussian intervals can be poorly calibrated. Unlike point prediction, where a single metric like RMSE reveals whether the model is working, uncertainty quantification can fail in subtle ways. A model can produce reasonable-looking prediction intervals that have entirely wrong coverage. It can produce correct marginal coverage while being wildly miscalibrated conditionally. It can appear well-calibrated on the training distribution but fail catastrophically under distribution shift. The IQN failure documented in @sec-cal-failure is a particularly insidious example: the model produces excellent point predictions (RMSE competitive with the GP baseline) while its uncertainty estimates are essentially meaningless (34% coverage at 90% nominal). Without a systematic diagnostic procedure, this failure would be invisible from point-prediction metrics alone.

The checklist follows four sequential diagnostic steps. If the first diagnostic reveals a problem, fix it before proceeding to the second. Each diagnostic has a specific indicator (what to look for), a diagnosis (what the problem is), and a remedy (what to do about it).

The flowchart should be read top-to-bottom: each check either passes (leading to the next check) or fails (leading to a specific remedy). We now describe each diagnostic in detail, including practical guidance on how to implement each check.

**Step 1: PIT histogram.** The probability integral transform (PIT) maps each observation $y_i$ through the model's predicted CDF: $u_i = \hat{F}(y_i \mid x_i)$. If the model is correctly specified, the PIT values $u_1, \ldots, u_n$ should be approximately $\text{Uniform}(0, 1)$. For a het-MLP, the PIT is computed as $u_i = \Phi((y_i - \hat{\mu}(x_i)) / \hat{\sigma}(x_i))$ where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. For an IQN, it is computed by inverting the learned quantile function: $u_i = \hat{F}(y_i \mid x_i)$ where $\hat{F}$ is the CDF implied by the quantile predictions. In practice, we find $\tau^*$ by binary search over $\tau \in (0,1)$ such that $\hat{Q}(\tau^* \mid x_i) = y_i$, then set $u_i = \tau^*$. The \texttt{gbc} package provides a utility for this numerical inversion. A U-shaped PIT histogram indicates underdispersion (prediction intervals too narrow). An inverted-U shape indicates overdispersion (intervals too wide). A skewed histogram indicates bias in the location. If the PIT histogram is far from uniform, the model is fundamentally misspecified --- neither adding capacity nor applying conformal calibration will fix the underlying problem. Revisit the architecture, the input features, or the loss function.

**Step 2: CRPS vs. epoch.** The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) is the integral of the pinball loss across all quantile levels, as we defined in @sec-iqn-crps-convergence. Monitoring CRPS on a validation set during training reveals whether the model has converged. If the CRPS plateaus at a high value early in training, the model is underfitting: it lacks the capacity or the training time to learn the conditional distribution. Increase the network width or depth, train for more epochs, or reduce regularization. If the CRPS is still decreasing at the end of training, the model needs more epochs. For het-MLP models, the analogous diagnostic is to monitor the Gaussian NLL on a held-out validation set: if it is still decreasing, the model has not converged. A useful secondary check is to plot the validation NLL alongside the training NLL: if the training NLL continues to decrease while the validation NLL increases, the model is overfitting and needs regularization (dropout, weight decay, or early stopping).

**Step 3: Quantile crossings.** For any $\tau_1 < \tau_2$, the true quantile function satisfies $Q(\tau_1 \mid x) \leq Q(\tau_2 \mid x)$ for all $x$. The IQN does not enforce this monotonicity constraint, and quantile crossings --- where the 0.05 quantile exceeds the 0.95 quantile, for example --- indicate that the architecture is struggling to produce a coherent distributional prediction. Occasional crossings (less than 1% of test points) are harmless and can be fixed by post-hoc sorting of the predicted quantiles. Frequent crossings (more than 5%) signal a fundamental architectural problem: the multiplicative $\tau$-embedding cannot express the required distributional structure at this SNR level. On the lake data, the standard IQN produces crossings at 12% of test points, confirming the severity of the failure. The remedy is either a monotone output layer that enforces non-crossing by construction (such as the increasing-softmax architecture of @chernozhukov2010quantile), or a switch to the het-MLP approach where the Gaussian distributional assumption prevents crossings entirely, since $\hat{\mu}(x) - z \hat{\sigma}(x) < \hat{\mu}(x) + z \hat{\sigma}(x)$ for any $z > 0$ and $\hat{\sigma}(x) > 0$.

**Step 4: Coverage.** Finally, check the empirical coverage of the prediction intervals at the nominal level. If coverage is within 5 percentage points of the target (e.g., 85--95% at a 90% nominal level), the model is reasonably well-calibrated and can be deployed, possibly with minor conformal adjustments. If coverage is off by more than 5 percentage points --- as in our IQN experiments where coverage was 11--34% at a 90% target --- conformal post-processing is essential. When checking coverage, it is important to check it at multiple nominal levels (e.g., 50%, 80%, 90%, 95%) and across subgroups of the data (e.g., different horizons, different seasons, different depth layers). A model that achieves 90% overall coverage but only 60% coverage at long horizons is miscalibrated in a practically important way that overall metrics miss.

The flowchart in @fig-cal-diagnostic-flowchart is designed to be followed sequentially. A model that fails Step 1 should not be conformally calibrated (Step 4) --- conformal calibration can correct the *scale* of prediction intervals but not their *shape*, so a fundamentally misspecified model will produce conformal intervals that are correctly sized on average but poorly adapted to the local structure. Fix the model first, then calibrate.

The checklist is intentionally sequential because each diagnostic is most informative when the earlier checks have passed. A model with severe misspecification (Step 1) will have poor CRPS (Step 2), frequent quantile crossings (Step 3), and bad coverage (Step 4) --- but fixing the misspecification may resolve all four problems simultaneously. Diagnosing the root cause saves time.

To make the checklist concrete, consider how it applies to our two main examples. On the motorcycle data from @sec-iqn-motorcycle, the IQN passes all four steps: the PIT histogram is approximately uniform (the distributional model captures the heteroscedastic, non-Gaussian structure), CRPS converges to a low value, quantile crossings are rare (less than 0.5% of test points), and coverage is within 2 percentage points of nominal. The IQN is the right tool for that problem. On the lake temperature data, the IQN fails at Step 4: coverage is 34% at a 90% target, and the PI width does not vary meaningfully across nominal levels. The checklist directs us to conformal post-processing, and since the underlying model is fundamentally unable to produce spread (an architecture problem, not just a calibration problem), it further suggests switching to the het-MLP. Following the checklist systematically would have saved us the time spent trying larger IQNs and two-stage approaches --- all of which failed for the same architectural reason.


## When to Use Conformal Post-Processing {#sec-cal-when-conformal}

The conformal calibration pipeline developed in @sec-cal-conformal is not always necessary. When should a practitioner invest in conformal post-processing, and when is the model's native uncertainty sufficient?

Conformal prediction provides a *marginal* coverage guarantee: the probability that the prediction interval contains a randomly drawn test point is at least $1 - \alpha$. This guarantee is averaged over both the randomness in the calibration data and the randomness in the test point. It does not guarantee *conditional* coverage --- that is, it does not promise that coverage is $1 - \alpha$ for every value of $x$. As @sec-pred-conformal discussed, this distinction matters: marginal coverage is weaker but always achievable, while conditional coverage is stronger but generally impossible without distributional assumptions. @vovk2012conditional showed that exact conditional coverage is impossible in a distribution-free setting: for any calibration procedure, there exists a distribution under which the conditional coverage at some $x$ is arbitrarily far from the nominal level.

We recommend conformal post-processing in three situations.

First, when the model's native coverage is off by more than 5 percentage points from the target. If a het-MLP ensemble achieves 84% coverage at a 90% nominal level, the miscalibration is modest and may be acceptable for exploratory analysis. But if IQN achieves 34% coverage at a 90% target, conformal calibration is essential --- the native intervals are meaningless. The lake temperature benchmark is an extreme case of this scenario: the IQN coverage of 11--34% is so far from the target that conformal calibration on IQN outputs would produce extremely wide intervals. The better approach, as we demonstrated, is to switch to a het-MLP first and then apply conformal calibration to get the scale right. The 5-percentage-point threshold is a pragmatic guideline, not a hard rule: the decision should also consider the downstream consequences of miscalibration and the cost of implementing conformal calibration (which requires a separate calibration phase and slightly more complex deployment infrastructure).

Second, when the deployment context requires guaranteed coverage. In operational forecasting --- such as the lake temperature forecasts used for water treatment decisions --- the consequences of systematically overconfident intervals can be severe. A water treatment plant that relies on an 85% coverage interval for algal bloom prediction needs that 85% to be real, not an aspirational lower bound. Conformal calibration converts model-dependent coverage claims into distribution-free guarantees (up to the exchangeability assumption), which is a substantial improvement in reliability. The coverage guarantee is *marginal* --- it holds on average over the randomness in both the calibration data and the test point --- but marginal coverage with a rigorous guarantee is often more useful in practice than conditional coverage with no guarantee at all.

Third, when the data is non-stationary. In time-series applications, the test distribution systematically differs from the training distribution: new seasons bring new weather patterns, lake conditions evolve, and the physics-based simulator may be updated. The temporal CV approach in @sec-cal-temporal-cv generates calibration residuals that span multiple seasons and years, providing some robustness to distributional shift. Without conformal calibration, the model's native variance estimates are trained on historical data and may be poorly calibrated for future conditions. This is perhaps the strongest argument for conformal post-processing in time-series settings: it provides a principled way to account for the fact that the future is different from the past, without requiring an explicit model of how the distribution shifts.

Conversely, conformal post-processing may be unnecessary or even counterproductive in two situations. When the model is already well-calibrated (coverage within 2--3 percentage points of the target), conformal calibration adds little value and may slightly widen the intervals. And when conditional coverage is paramount, the constant-width conformal intervals may be less useful than adaptive intervals from a well-calibrated model. In such cases, the conformalized quantile regression approach of @romano2019conformalized --- which we discussed in @sec-pred-cqr --- provides adaptive intervals with marginal coverage guarantees by using $\max(\hat{Q}(\alpha/2 \mid x) - y, y - \hat{Q}(1-\alpha/2 \mid x))$ as the nonconformity score, combining the best of both worlds, provided the base model's quantile estimates are reasonable.

A rule of thumb from our experience across the benchmarks in this book. On the motorcycle and Phantom datasets, the IQN produces well-calibrated intervals without any conformal correction --- the distributional flexibility of the quantile regression approach is both necessary and sufficient. On the lake temperature data, conformal calibration is essential because the base model's uncertainty estimates are unreliable. The dividing line is, roughly, whether the base model's coverage is within 5 percentage points of the target. Below that threshold, conformal calibration is the right investment. Above it, the effort is better spent on improving the base model itself.

Conformal calibration should be *re-run periodically* in non-stationary settings. The conformal quantiles are computed from a specific set of out-of-fold residuals, which reflect the error structure at a particular point in time. As the data-generating process evolves --- due to climate change, changes in reservoir management, or updates to the physics-based simulator --- the old conformal quantiles may become stale. A practical approach is to update the conformal quantiles (Phase 1 only, without retraining the ensemble) whenever new data accumulates, using an expanding window of recent forecast--observation pairs. This provides a lightweight way to maintain calibration over time without the full cost of retraining. In our lake temperature application, this could be implemented by collecting forecast--observation pairs during each season and updating the per-horizon conformal quantiles at the start of each new season, using all accumulated data. The neural network ensemble would be retrained only when the accumulated performance degradation (measured by RMSE or coverage) exceeds a predetermined threshold.


## Summary: When to Use What {#sec-cal-summary}

This chapter has presented a diagnostic and a remedy for IQN failure on high-SNR data. The broader message is that the choice of uncertainty quantification method should match the problem characteristics.

| Problem type | Recommended approach | Why |
|:----------------------------|:------------------------------|:--------------------------------------------------|
| Heteroscedastic, multimodal | IQN (@sec-iqn) | Full distributional flexibility; no parametric assumption |
| Jump discontinuities | Aug-IQN (@sec-jumps) | Boundary augmentation handles piecewise structure |
| High SNR, Gaussian-like noise | Het-MLP ensemble + conformal | IQN gradient collapse; parametric variance + conformal |
| Small $n$, low $d$ | GP (Chapters [-@sec-surr-gp]) | GP prior beneficial; principled posterior uncertainty |

: Recommended uncertainty quantification approach by problem type. {#tbl-cal-recommendations}

The diagnostic is the signal-to-noise ratio. When fitting a good point predictor leaves residuals that are a small fraction of the response range, the IQN architecture will struggle to produce distributional spread. In that regime, the het-MLP ensemble with conformal calibration is the right tool.

The diagnostic checklist in @sec-cal-diagnostics provides a systematic procedure for evaluating any neural network UQ method on a new dataset. Follow it sequentially: check the PIT histogram for distributional misspecification, monitor CRPS convergence for underfitting, inspect quantile crossings for architectural problems, and validate coverage for calibration failure. If the model passes all four checks, deploy it with confidence. If it fails at any step, the checklist points to the appropriate remedy. The entire diagnostic procedure can be run in minutes, even on large datasets, and should be standard practice before deploying any neural network uncertainty model.

This chapter completes a natural arc. We began in @sec-iqn with the IQN as a universal tool for conditional distributions, demonstrated its power on heteroscedastic (@sec-iqn-motorcycle) and multimodal (@sec-jumps) problems, and used it to build surrogates (@sec-surrogates) and drive active learning (@sec-active-learning). Here we have shown its limits and provided an alternative. The GBC framework is broader than any single architecture: it encompasses IQN when distributional flexibility is needed, het-MLP ensembles when parametric models suffice, and conformal calibration when distribution-free guarantees are required.

Uncertainty quantification is harder than point prediction, and no single method works everywhere. Conformal calibration provides a safety net for either approach, converting model-dependent uncertainty estimates into distribution-free prediction intervals with honest coverage.

In the language of the GBC framework, this chapter demonstrates that "generative Bayesian computation" is not synonymous with "implicit quantile networks." The GBC philosophy --- simulate from the model, train a neural network, produce posterior quantiles --- encompasses multiple architectures and calibration strategies. The het-MLP ensemble with conformal calibration is a GBC method: it produces calibrated predictive distributions without evaluating a likelihood, without running MCMC chains, and without specifying a prior. It just happens to use Gaussian NLL rather than pinball loss, and conformal quantiles rather than IQN quantiles. The computational philosophy is the same; only the implementation differs.

**Ensemble size and uncertainty decomposition.**

This exercise explores how ensemble size $K$ affects the decomposition of uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic components, as described in @eq-cal-total-var. Understanding this decomposition is essential for interpreting ensemble predictions and for deciding how many ensemble members to train.

(a) Generate $n = 3000$ observations from $Y = \sin(3x) + (0.1 + 0.3 |x|) \epsilon$ where $x \sim \text{Uniform}(-3, 3)$ and $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Train het-MLP ensembles with $K \in \{1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 20\}$ members (128$\times$2, 2000 epochs each). For each $K$, compute the average aleatoric uncertainty $\bar{\sigma}^2_{\text{aleatoric}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i \frac{1}{K} \sum_k \hat{\sigma}^2_k(x_i)$ and the average epistemic uncertainty $\bar{\sigma}^2_{\text{epistemic}} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_i [\frac{1}{K} \sum_k \hat{\mu}^2_k(x_i) - \bar{\mu}^2(x_i)]$. Plot both as a function of $K$.

(b) As $K$ grows, the epistemic component should decrease (members agree more on average) while the aleatoric component should stabilize (it reflects irreducible noise, not model uncertainty). At what $K$ does the epistemic component become less than 10% of the aleatoric component?

(c) Evaluate the 90% PI coverage on a held-out test set ($n = 1000$) for each $K$, using both Gaussian intervals and conformal intervals (with a calibration set of 500 observations). Does coverage improve monotonically with $K$? Is there a diminishing returns effect? Which approach --- Gaussian or conformal --- benefits more from increasing the ensemble size?

(d) Discuss the computational tradeoff. Each additional ensemble member roughly doubles the training time but provides diminishing improvement in RMSE and coverage. If training time is the binding constraint, is it better to train $K = 3$ members for 4000 epochs each, or $K = 10$ members for 1200 epochs each (same total compute)? Implement both configurations and compare.
