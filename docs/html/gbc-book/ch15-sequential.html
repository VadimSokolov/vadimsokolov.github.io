“The art of progress is to preserve order amid change and to preserve change amid order.” Alfred North Whitehead

Every example in Parts I–III is either i.i.d. or fixed-design: we observe a dataset all at once and infer static parameters. Real systems do not cooperate. Financial returns arrive tick by tick. Epidemic surveillance data accumulate day by day. Sensor networks stream measurements in real time. In all these settings, we want the posterior to update as data arrive, not after a long batch has accumulated.

This chapter extends GBC to state-space models, where a latent state evolves in time and observations arrive sequentially. The key idea is amortization: instead of running a new inference algorithm every time a new observation arrives, we train a single filtering IQN once and apply it by forward pass at every time step. The random-variable Bellman recursion of Chapter 6, which in Part I served mainly as theoretical background, becomes the working tool here.

15.1 The Sequential Inference Problem

A state-space model specifies the joint distribution of latent states \(x_{1:T}\) and observations \(y_{1:T}\): \[ x_t \mid x_{t-1} \sim p(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, \theta), \qquad y_t \mid x_t \sim p(y_t \mid x_t, \theta). \] The first equation is the transition (dynamics); the second is the observation model. The parameter \(\theta\) governs both; we treat it as fixed and known for now (Section 15.12 asks the reader to extend to unknown \(\theta\)).

Two inference targets:

  • Filtering. \(\pi(x_t \mid y_{1:t})\): the posterior over the latent state given all data observed so far. This is the online target, relevant for real-time decision-making.
  • Smoothing. \(\pi(x_t \mid y_{1:T})\): the posterior given all data, including future observations. This is the retrospective target, relevant for batch analysis after data collection ends.

The defining structural feature, which distinguishes this from Parts I–III, is that the conditioning set grows with \(t\). We want the entire sequence of posteriors \(\pi(x_t \mid y_{1:t})\), not a single posterior.

15.2 Classical Approaches and Their Limits

The Kalman filter is exact for linear-Gaussian models: if the transition and observation maps are linear and the noise is Gaussian, the filtering distribution is Gaussian at every step and its mean and covariance admit a closed-form recursion. The Kalman filter is elegant and computationally cheap, but its Gaussian-linear restriction excludes most realistic models.

Particle filters (Sequential Monte Carlo) are general and asymptotically exact, but suffer two practical problems. First, weight degeneracy: after a few steps, one particle carries nearly all the weight, and the effective sample size collapses. Resampling mitigates weight degeneracy but introduces path degeneracy: distinct particles trace identical ancestry. Second, particle filters are per-series: running the filter on a new observed sequence requires a complete re-execution of the algorithm, with cost \(O(T \cdot n_\text{particles})\) per series.

Particle MCMC combines SMC with MCMC to produce correct joint inference over states and parameters, but at substantial computational cost and with no amortization across series.

The GBC pitch for the sequential setting is identical to the pitch in Chapter 1 for the static setting: classical methods re-run per series; GBC trains once and filters any series by forward pass.

15.3 Noise Outsourcing for Filtering

Apply Corollary 4.1 with \(\theta \leftarrow x_t\) and \(y \leftarrow y_{1:t}\): there exists \(U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\) and a map \(G_t^*\) such that \[ x_t \mid y_{1:t} \overset{d}{=} G_t^*(U, y_{1:t}). \] The quantile function of the filtering distribution exists and carries the full conditional distribution. The obstacle: \(y_{1:t}\) is a growing-dimension object. For the static IQN of Chapter 5, the input \(z\) has fixed dimension. We resolve the growing-dimension problem through a recurrent sufficient summary, developed in Section 15.4.

15.4 The Recurrent Summary Statistic

Replace the growing input \(y_{1:t}\) with a fixed-dimension state summary updated recurrently: \[ s_t = h_\psi(s_{t-1}, y_t), \qquad s_0 = \mathbf{0}. \] Here \(h_\psi\) is a learned encoder (we use a GRU in the worked examples, though S4 or a lightweight linear SSM are viable alternatives for long sequences). The approximation claim is that \(s_t\) is an approximately sufficient statistic for \(x_t\) given \(y_{1:t}\): \[ \pi(x_t \mid y_{1:t}) \approx \pi(x_t \mid s_t). \] This connects to Section 10.4: just as the IQN in the static setting uses a learned summary of \(y\) as its input, the sequential IQN uses a recursively learned summary that folds in one new observation per step. The encoder \(h_\psi\) and the quantile head \(G_\phi\) are trained jointly; see Section 15.5.

15.5 The Filtering IQN

The filtering IQN has two components:

  1. Recurrent encoder. A GRU that maps the observation sequence to a fixed-dimension summary: \(s_t = \text{GRU}_\psi(s_{t-1}, y_t)\).
  2. Quantile head. The standard IQN architecture of Section 5.2, with \(s_t\) in place of the static covariate \(z\): \(\hat{G}_\phi(\tau, s_t) \approx Q_{x_t \mid y_{1:t}}(\tau)\).

The two components are illustrated in the following diagram.

Training. Simulate the model forward: draw \(\theta \sim \pi(\theta)\) (or fix \(\theta\) if known), roll out \(x_{1:T}, y_{1:T}\), and form supervised pairs \((x_t, s_t)\) at every \(t\). The pinball loss is summed over \(t\) and averaged over simulated trajectories: \[ L(\phi, \psi) = \frac{1}{NT} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^T \rho_{\tau_{i,t}}(x_t^{(i)} - \hat{G}_\phi(\tau_{i,t}, s_t^{(i)})), \] where \(\tau_{i,t} \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\) independently for each \((i, t)\). Crucially this is one network, shared across all time steps and all simulated series, trained once.

Multivariate state. Use the autoregressive decomposition of Chapter 4 for the components of \(x_t\), with separate quantile heads per component conditioned on previous components. The guidance from Section 10.9 on independent versus autoregressive factorizations applies here without modification.

15.6 The Bayes Filter as a Distributional Contraction

This section makes Chapter 6 pay off. The filtering recursion decomposes into two steps: \[ \pi(x_t \mid y_{1:t}) = \underbrace{[\text{update}]}_{\times\; p(y_t|x_t)} \circ \underbrace{[\text{predict}]}_{\text{marginalize over } x_{t-1}} \;\pi(x_{t-1} \mid y_{1:t-1}). \] Each step is a map on distributions: predict convolves the previous filtering distribution with the transition kernel; update reweights by the likelihood \(p(y_t \mid x_t)\). Composing these two maps gives the Bayes filter operator \(\mathcal{B}_t\).

NoteFiltering Contraction (informal)

Under mixing conditions on the transition kernel \(p(x_t \mid x_{t-1})\), the Bayes filter operator \(\mathcal{B}_t\) is a contraction in Wasserstein-1 distance: two filtering distributions initialized differently converge at a geometric rate. Specifically, if \(\pi\) and \(\tilde{\pi}\) are two initial distributions, \[ W_1(\mathcal{B}_t^n \pi, \mathcal{B}_t^n \tilde{\pi}) \leq C \rho^n W_1(\pi, \tilde{\pi}), \] for some \(C < \infty\) and \(\rho < 1\) depending on the mixing rate of the transition.

This contraction plays the same structural role as the Bellman contraction in Chapter 6: it justifies training a single shared \(\hat{G}_\phi\) across all time steps, because the filtering distribution does not depend sensitively on distant initial conditions. Errors in the learned quantile function do not accumulate without bound.

15.7 Smoothing via Forward-Backward Summaries

Filtering uses a forward summary \(s_t\) that processes \(y_1, \ldots, y_t\) left to right. Smoothing needs future observations too. Add a backward recurrent summary: \[ b_t = \tilde{h}_\xi(b_{t+1}, y_t), \qquad b_T = \mathbf{0}, \] processed right to left, and learn the smoothing quantile \(\hat{G}(\tau, s_t, b_{t+1})\). This is the GBC analogue of the forward-backward smoother, with no backward simulation of importance weights and no path degeneracy. Training cost increases by roughly a factor of two (two passes per trajectory), but inference remains \(O(T)\) per series.

15.8 Worked Example: Stochastic Volatility

The canonical continuous-time financial state-space model couples log-volatility (latent) to returns (observed): \[ x_t = \mu + \rho(x_{t-1} - \mu) + \sigma_\eta \eta_t, \qquad y_t = \exp(x_t / 2) \cdot \varepsilon_t, \] where \(\eta_t, \varepsilon_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(0,1)\). We use parameters \(\mu = -0.8\), \(\rho = 0.97\), \(\sigma_\eta = 0.15\). The non-Gaussian observation distribution (returns have heavier tails than \(N(0, e^{x_t})\) conditional on \(x_t\)) makes classical filtering hard; standard practice relies on particle filters with \(n_\text{particles} \approx 500\)–\(2000\).

Code 
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np

torch.manual_seed(42)
np.random.seed(42)

# ── Model parameters ──
mu_sv   = -0.8
rho_sv  = 0.97
sig_eta = 0.15
T_sv    = 200    # trajectory length
N_sv    = 5000   # number of training trajectories
d_s     = 32     # summary dimension

# ── Simulate training data ──
X_all, Y_all = [], []
for _ in range(N_sv):
    x = np.zeros(T_sv)
    y = np.zeros(T_sv)
    x[0] = np.random.normal(mu_sv, sig_eta / np.sqrt(1 - rho_sv**2))
    y[0] = np.exp(x[0] / 2) * np.random.randn()
    for t in range(1, T_sv):
        x[t] = mu_sv + rho_sv * (x[t-1] - mu_sv) + sig_eta * np.random.randn()
        y[t] = np.exp(x[t] / 2) * np.random.randn()
    X_all.append(x)
    Y_all.append(y)

X_all = np.array(X_all)  # (N_sv, T_sv)
Y_all = np.array(Y_all)

# ── Filtering IQN ──
class FilterIQN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, y_dim=1, s_dim=32, hdim=128, M=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.gru    = nn.GRUCell(y_dim, s_dim)
        self.fc_tau = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(M, hdim), nn.ReLU())
        self.fc_x   = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(s_dim, hdim), nn.ReLU())
        self.head   = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(hdim, hdim), nn.ReLU(),
                                    nn.Linear(hdim, 1))
        self.M      = M

    def forward(self, s, tau):
        # s: (batch, s_dim), tau: (batch, 1)
        i = torch.arange(1, self.M + 1, dtype=torch.float32).unsqueeze(0)
        phi_tau = torch.cos(i * torch.pi * tau) # (batch, M)
        h_tau = self.fc_tau(phi_tau)
        h_x   = self.fc_x(s)
        h     = h_x * h_tau
        return self.head(h)


model_sv = FilterIQN(y_dim=1, s_dim=d_s, hdim=128, M=64)
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model_sv.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

X_t = torch.tensor(X_all, dtype=torch.float32)  # (N_sv, T_sv)
Y_t = torch.tensor(Y_all, dtype=torch.float32).unsqueeze(-1)  # (N_sv, T_sv, 1)

n_epochs = 300
batch_traj = 256

for epoch in range(n_epochs):
    idx = torch.randint(0, N_sv, (batch_traj,))
    x_b = X_t[idx]     # (batch_traj, T_sv)
    y_b = Y_t[idx]     # (batch_traj, T_sv, 1)

    s = torch.zeros(batch_traj, d_s)
    total_loss = torch.tensor(0.0)
    for t in range(T_sv):
        s = model_sv.gru(y_b[:, t, :], s)  # update summary
        tau = torch.rand(batch_traj, 1)
        q_hat = model_sv(s, tau)
        residual = x_b[:, t:t+1] - q_hat
        loss_t = torch.mean(torch.maximum(tau * residual, (tau - 1) * residual))
        total_loss = total_loss + loss_t

    optimizer.zero_grad()
    (total_loss / T_sv).backward()
    optimizer.step()
    if epoch % 50 == 0:
        print(f"Epoch {epoch:3d}  loss={total_loss.item()/T_sv:.4f}")

torch.save(model_sv.state_dict(), "cache/sv_filter_iqn.pt")
print("Saved to cache/sv_filter_iqn.pt")

Amortized inference. Once trained, applying the filter to a new observed return series costs \(T\) GRU steps and \(T\) IQN forward passes. No per-series fitting, no particle weights.

15.9 Worked Example: Time-Varying Epidemic Transmission

A time-varying-\(R_t\) epidemic model: the effective reproduction number follows a random walk, observed case counts are noisy. This is operationally the most compelling application of sequential GBC: outbreak response needs online posterior updates as surveillance data arrive daily.

Code 
# SIR-like model with time-varying Rt
# State: log(Rt), observed: log(cases + 1)
# Transition: log(Rt) = log(R_{t-1}) + sigma_R * eta_t, eta ~ N(0,1)
# Observation: cases_t ~ NegBin(mu_t, phi), mu_t = S_t * (Rt - 1) * I_t / N
# Simplified version for the book: treat log(Rt) as AR(1), cases as Poisson(exp(log_Rt * 50))

sigma_R = 0.1
T_epi   = 100
N_epi   = 3000

log_Rt_all, cases_all = [], []
for _ in range(N_epi):
    log_Rt = np.zeros(T_epi)
    log_Rt[0] = np.log(1.2) + np.random.normal(0, sigma_R)
    for t in range(1, T_epi):
        log_Rt[t] = log_Rt[t-1] + np.random.normal(0, sigma_R)
    # Poisson observations proportional to Rt
    cases = np.random.poisson(np.exp(log_Rt) * 50)
    log_Rt_all.append(log_Rt)
    cases_all.append(np.log1p(cases))

log_Rt_all = np.array(log_Rt_all)
cases_all  = np.array(cases_all)

The filtering IQN (same architecture as Section 15.8 with \(y = \log(1 + \text{cases})\) and \(x = \log R_t\)) produces filtered \(R_t\) trajectories with credible bands. The practical output of interest is the crossing of \(R_t = 1\): as long as the posterior’s 95th percentile remains above 1, the epidemic is growing; once the posterior’s 5th percentile drops below 1, suppression is confirmed. Sequential GBC provides this diagnostic in real time with a single forward pass per day.

15.10 Amortization Across Series

The payoff of training once and filtering many times is identical in structure to the amortization story in Section 10.4. State it precisely, in parallel with ?tbl-seq-timing above:

  • Training cost. \(N_\text{sim}\) simulated trajectories of length \(T\), one network, paid once.
  • Inference cost. For a new observed series of length \(T\): \(T\) GRU updates and \(T\) IQN forward passes. Total cost \(O(T)\), independent of \(N_\text{sim}\).
  • Contrast. A particle filter requires \(O(T \cdot n_\text{particles})\) per series. A fresh MCMC run has no amortization at all.

In outbreak monitoring, where the same model is applied to hundreds of geographic units simultaneously, the amortization gains are decisive: training takes hours, but filtering all units takes seconds.

15.11 When Sequential GBC Struggles

In the spirit of Chapter 14:

Summary insufficiency. The recurrent summary \(s_t\) is only approximately sufficient. If the model has long-range dependence that a finite-state GRU cannot capture (e.g., fractional Brownian motion, or a hidden Markov model with many states), the filtering posterior is biased. Diagnostic: compare \(\hat{G}(\tau, s_t)\) against a long-\(T\) particle filter on simulated data with known ground truth; if the two diverge as \(T\) grows, \(s_t\) is lossy and a larger hidden state or a deeper encoder is needed.

Distribution shift. The encoder is trained on rollouts of a fixed length \(T_\text{train}\). If deployed on series much longer than \(T_\text{train}\), the marginal distribution of \(s_t\) drifts out of the training regime. Mitigate with longer training rollouts, random-length rollouts, or a stationary initialization scheme.

Tail quantiles of the filter. The moderate-deviation problem of Appendix C reappears here at every time step: uniform-\(\tau\) training under-resolves the filtering distribution’s tails. Tilted-\(\tau\) sampling (Section C.4) carries over directly.

Model misspecification. Amortization bakes in the simulator. If the real series is generated by a different process than the one used for training, the filter is confidently wrong. The price of amortization is that misspecification is silent: the network produces quantiles with no warning that the training distribution has been violated. Posterior-predictive checks using the observation model provide a practical misspecification diagnostic.

15.12 Exercises

  2. Implement the recurrent summary \(s_t\) for the stochastic-volatility model using a GRU with hidden dimension 16. Verify approximate sufficiency by comparing filtering quantiles against a 2000-particle bootstrap filter on 100 simulated test series.

  3. Show that the shared-across-\(t\) filtering IQN is justified by the Wasserstein contraction of Section 15.6: a filtering error at time \(t\) does not compound without bound at \(t + k\) as \(k \to \infty\).

  4. Train the filtering IQN on the stochastic-volatility model with uniform \(\tau\) and with logit-normal tilted \(\tau\) (\(s = 2\)). Compare the empirical coverage of the filtered 95% intervals on a held-out test set of 200 series.

  5. Apply the trained filter to a real daily return series (e.g., S&P 500 log-returns from Yahoo Finance). Plot the filtered log-volatility with 90% credible bands. Identify at least two periods where the bands widen noticeably; check whether these correspond to known high-volatility episodes (COVID crash, financial crisis, etc.). Run a posterior-predictive check: under the stochastic-volatility model, are the observed returns consistent with the filtered volatility?

## The Sequential Inference Problem {#sec-seq-problem}

$$
x_t \mid x_{t-1} \sim p(x_t \mid x_{t-1}, \theta), \qquad y_t \mid x_t \sim p(y_t \mid x_t, \theta).
$$
The first equation is the **transition** (dynamics); the second is the **observation** model. The parameter $\theta$ governs both; we treat it as fixed and known for now (@sec-seq-exercises asks the reader to extend to unknown $\theta$).

The defining structural feature, which distinguishes this from Parts I--III, is that the conditioning set *grows with $t$*. We want the entire sequence of posteriors $\pi(x_t \mid y_{1:t})$, not a single posterior.

```mermaid
    y2["$y_2$"] --> GRU
    yT["$y_t$"] --> GRU
    GRU --> st["$s_t$"]
    st --> IQN["Quantile head $G_\\phi(\\tau, s_t)$"]
    tau["$\\tau \\sim U(0,1)$"] --> IQN
    IQN --> Qhat["$\\hat{Q}_{x_t|y_{1:t}}(\\tau)$"]
```

**Training.** Simulate the model forward: draw $\theta \sim \pi(\theta)$ (or fix $\theta$ if known), roll out $x_{1:T}, y_{1:T}$, and form supervised pairs $(x_t, s_t)$ at every $t$. The pinball loss is summed over $t$ and averaged over simulated trajectories:
$$
L(\phi, \psi) = \frac{1}{NT} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{t=1}^T \rho_{\tau_{i,t}}(x_t^{(i)} - \hat{G}_\phi(\tau_{i,t}, s_t^{(i)})),
$$
where $\tau_{i,t} \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ independently for each $(i, t)$. Crucially this is one network, shared across all time steps and all simulated series, trained once.

**Multivariate state.** Use the autoregressive decomposition of @sec-noise-outsourcing for the components of $x_t$, with separate quantile heads per component conditioned on previous components. The guidance from @sec-bi-multivariate on independent versus autoregressive factorizations applies here without modification.

## The Bayes Filter as a Distributional Contraction {#sec-seq-contraction}

This section makes Chapter 6 pay off. The filtering recursion decomposes into two steps:
$$
\pi(x_t \mid y_{1:t}) = \underbrace{[\text{update}]}_{\times\; p(y_t|x_t)} \circ \underbrace{[\text{predict}]}_{\text{marginalize over } x_{t-1}} \;\pi(x_{t-1} \mid y_{1:t-1}).
$$
Each step is a map on distributions: *predict* convolves the previous filtering distribution with the transition kernel; *update* reweights by the likelihood $p(y_t \mid x_t)$. Composing these two maps gives the Bayes filter operator $\mathcal{B}_t$.

::: {.callout-note}
## Filtering Contraction (informal)
Under mixing conditions on the transition kernel $p(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$, the Bayes filter operator $\mathcal{B}_t$ is a contraction in Wasserstein-1 distance: two filtering distributions initialized differently converge at a geometric rate. Specifically, if $\pi$ and $\tilde{\pi}$ are two initial distributions,
$$
W_1(\mathcal{B}_t^n \pi, \mathcal{B}_t^n \tilde{\pi}) \leq C \rho^n W_1(\pi, \tilde{\pi}),
$$
for some $C < \infty$ and $\rho < 1$ depending on the mixing rate of the transition.
:::

This contraction plays the same structural role as the Bellman contraction in @sec-dist-rl: it justifies training a single shared $\hat{G}_\phi$ across all time steps, because the filtering distribution does not depend sensitively on distant initial conditions. Errors in the learned quantile function do not accumulate without bound.

## Smoothing via Forward-Backward Summaries {#sec-seq-smoothing}

Filtering uses a *forward* summary $s_t$ that processes $y_1, \ldots, y_t$ left to right. Smoothing needs future observations too. Add a *backward* recurrent summary:
$$
b_t = \tilde{h}_\xi(b_{t+1}, y_t), \qquad b_T = \mathbf{0},
$$
processed right to left, and learn the smoothing quantile $\hat{G}(\tau, s_t, b_{t+1})$. This is the GBC analogue of the forward-backward smoother, with no backward simulation of importance weights and no path degeneracy. Training cost increases by roughly a factor of two (two passes per trajectory), but inference remains $O(T)$ per series.

## Worked Example: Stochastic Volatility {#sec-seq-volat}

The canonical continuous-time financial state-space model couples log-volatility (latent) to returns (observed):
$$
x_t = \mu + \rho(x_{t-1} - \mu) + \sigma_\eta \eta_t, \qquad y_t = \exp(x_t / 2) \cdot \varepsilon_t,
$$
where $\eta_t, \varepsilon_t \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(0,1)$. We use parameters $\mu = -0.8$, $\rho = 0.97$, $\sigma_\eta = 0.15$. The non-Gaussian observation distribution (returns have heavier tails than $N(0, e^{x_t})$ conditional on $x_t$) makes classical filtering hard; standard practice relies on particle filters with $n_\text{particles} \approx 500$--$2000$.

```python
#| eval: false
#| code-fold: true
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np

torch.manual_seed(42)
np.random.seed(42)

# ── Model parameters ──
mu_sv   = -0.8
rho_sv  = 0.97
sig_eta = 0.15
T_sv    = 200    # trajectory length
N_sv    = 5000   # number of training trajectories
d_s     = 32     # summary dimension

# ── Simulate training data ──
X_all, Y_all = [], []
for _ in range(N_sv):
    x = np.zeros(T_sv)
    y = np.zeros(T_sv)
    x[0] = np.random.normal(mu_sv, sig_eta / np.sqrt(1 - rho_sv**2))
    y[0] = np.exp(x[0] / 2) * np.random.randn()
    for t in range(1, T_sv):
        x[t] = mu_sv + rho_sv * (x[t-1] - mu_sv) + sig_eta * np.random.randn()
        y[t] = np.exp(x[t] / 2) * np.random.randn()
    X_all.append(x)
    Y_all.append(y)

X_all = np.array(X_all)  # (N_sv, T_sv)
Y_all = np.array(Y_all)

# ── Filtering IQN ──
class FilterIQN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, y_dim=1, s_dim=32, hdim=128, M=64):
        super().__init__()
        self.gru    = nn.GRUCell(y_dim, s_dim)
        self.fc_tau = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(M, hdim), nn.ReLU())
        self.fc_x   = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(s_dim, hdim), nn.ReLU())
        self.head   = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(hdim, hdim), nn.ReLU(),
                                    nn.Linear(hdim, 1))
        self.M      = M

    def forward(self, s, tau):
        # s: (batch, s_dim), tau: (batch, 1)
        i = torch.arange(1, self.M + 1, dtype=torch.float32).unsqueeze(0)
        phi_tau = torch.cos(i * torch.pi * tau) # (batch, M)
        h_tau = self.fc_tau(phi_tau)
        h_x   = self.fc_x(s)
        h     = h_x * h_tau
        return self.head(h)


model_sv = FilterIQN(y_dim=1, s_dim=d_s, hdim=128, M=64)
optimizer = torch.optim.Adam(model_sv.parameters(), lr=1e-3)

X_t = torch.tensor(X_all, dtype=torch.float32)  # (N_sv, T_sv)
Y_t = torch.tensor(Y_all, dtype=torch.float32).unsqueeze(-1)  # (N_sv, T_sv, 1)

n_epochs = 300
batch_traj = 256

for epoch in range(n_epochs):
    idx = torch.randint(0, N_sv, (batch_traj,))
    x_b = X_t[idx]     # (batch_traj, T_sv)
    y_b = Y_t[idx]     # (batch_traj, T_sv, 1)

    s = torch.zeros(batch_traj, d_s)
    total_loss = torch.tensor(0.0)
    for t in range(T_sv):
        s = model_sv.gru(y_b[:, t, :], s)  # update summary
        tau = torch.rand(batch_traj, 1)
        q_hat = model_sv(s, tau)
        residual = x_b[:, t:t+1] - q_hat
        loss_t = torch.mean(torch.maximum(tau * residual, (tau - 1) * residual))
        total_loss = total_loss + loss_t

    optimizer.zero_grad()
    (total_loss / T_sv).backward()
    optimizer.step()
    if epoch % 50 == 0:
        print(f"Epoch {epoch:3d}  loss={total_loss.item()/T_sv:.4f}")

torch.save(model_sv.state_dict(), "cache/sv_filter_iqn.pt")
print("Saved to cache/sv_filter_iqn.pt")
```

**Amortized inference.** Once trained, applying the filter to a new observed return series costs $T$ GRU steps and $T$ IQN forward passes. No per-series fitting, no particle weights.

## Worked Example: Time-Varying Epidemic Transmission {#sec-seq-epidemic}

A time-varying-$R_t$ epidemic model: the effective reproduction number follows a random walk, observed case counts are noisy. This is operationally the most compelling application of sequential GBC: outbreak response needs *online* posterior updates as surveillance data arrive daily.

```python
#| eval: false
#| code-fold: true
# SIR-like model with time-varying Rt
# State: log(Rt), observed: log(cases + 1)
# Transition: log(Rt) = log(R_{t-1}) + sigma_R * eta_t, eta ~ N(0,1)
# Observation: cases_t ~ NegBin(mu_t, phi), mu_t = S_t * (Rt - 1) * I_t / N
# Simplified version for the book: treat log(Rt) as AR(1), cases as Poisson(exp(log_Rt * 50))

sigma_R = 0.1
T_epi   = 100
N_epi   = 3000

log_Rt_all, cases_all = [], []
for _ in range(N_epi):
    log_Rt = np.zeros(T_epi)
    log_Rt[0] = np.log(1.2) + np.random.normal(0, sigma_R)
    for t in range(1, T_epi):
        log_Rt[t] = log_Rt[t-1] + np.random.normal(0, sigma_R)
    # Poisson observations proportional to Rt
    cases = np.random.poisson(np.exp(log_Rt) * 50)
    log_Rt_all.append(log_Rt)
    cases_all.append(np.log1p(cases))

log_Rt_all = np.array(log_Rt_all)
cases_all  = np.array(cases_all)
```

The filtering IQN (same architecture as @sec-seq-volat with $y = \log(1 + \text{cases})$ and $x = \log R_t$) produces filtered $R_t$ trajectories with credible bands. The practical output of interest is the crossing of $R_t = 1$: as long as the posterior's 95th percentile remains above 1, the epidemic is growing; once the posterior's 5th percentile drops below 1, suppression is confirmed. Sequential GBC provides this diagnostic in real time with a single forward pass per day.

## Amortization Across Series {#sec-seq-amortization}

The payoff of training once and filtering many times is identical in structure to the amortization story in @sec-bi-algorithm. State it precisely, in parallel with @tbl-seq-timing above:

- **Training cost.** $N_\text{sim}$ simulated trajectories of length $T$, one network, paid once.
- **Inference cost.** For a new observed series of length $T$: $T$ GRU updates and $T$ IQN forward passes. Total cost $O(T)$, independent of $N_\text{sim}$.
- **Contrast.** A particle filter requires $O(T \cdot n_\text{particles})$ per series. A fresh MCMC run has no amortization at all.

In outbreak monitoring, where the same model is applied to hundreds of geographic units simultaneously, the amortization gains are decisive: training takes hours, but filtering all units takes seconds.

## When Sequential GBC Struggles {#sec-seq-limitations}

In the spirit of @sec-calibration:

**Summary insufficiency.** The recurrent summary $s_t$ is only *approximately* sufficient. If the model has long-range dependence that a finite-state GRU cannot capture (e.g., fractional Brownian motion, or a hidden Markov model with many states), the filtering posterior is biased. Diagnostic: compare $\hat{G}(\tau, s_t)$ against a long-$T$ particle filter on simulated data with known ground truth; if the two diverge as $T$ grows, $s_t$ is lossy and a larger hidden state or a deeper encoder is needed.

**Distribution shift.** The encoder is trained on rollouts of a fixed length $T_\text{train}$. If deployed on series much longer than $T_\text{train}$, the marginal distribution of $s_t$ drifts out of the training regime. Mitigate with longer training rollouts, random-length rollouts, or a stationary initialization scheme.

**Tail quantiles of the filter.** The moderate-deviation problem of @sec-moddev reappears here at every time step: uniform-$\tau$ training under-resolves the filtering distribution's tails. Tilted-$\tau$ sampling (@sec-moddev-remedy) carries over directly.

**Model misspecification.** Amortization bakes in the simulator. If the real series is generated by a different process than the one used for training, the filter is confidently wrong. The price of amortization is that misspecification is silent: the network produces quantiles with no warning that the training distribution has been violated. Posterior-predictive checks using the observation model provide a practical misspecification diagnostic.
