In the previous chapters, we built GBC surrogates for fixed datasets: a training set was given, and we trained an IQN to approximate the conditional quantile function. But in many scientific and engineering applications, the training data are not given, they are chosen. A computer simulator takes inputs \(x\) and returns outputs \(y = f(x)\), possibly with stochastic noise, and each evaluation costs minutes, hours, or days of compute time. The question is not just “how do I fit a surrogate?” but “where should I run the simulator next to learn the most?”

This is the sequential design problem, also known as active learning in the machine learning literature. Starting from a small initial design of \(n_0\) points, we iteratively select new inputs, evaluate the simulator, retrain the surrogate, and repeat until the budget is exhausted. The challenge is the acquisition function: a criterion that tells us which input \(x_{n+1}\) to evaluate next. A good acquisition function directs simulator evaluations toward regions of high uncertainty, reducing global prediction error faster than random sampling.

Gaussian processes have dominated sequential design for decades, largely because the GP posterior variance provides a natural, closed-form uncertainty estimate that feeds directly into acquisition criteria like Active Learning Cohn [ALC; Cohn (1994)] and expected improvement [EI; Jones, Schonlau, and Welch (1998)]. The GBC framework, built on neural networks rather than GPs, does not have a posterior variance in the Bayesian sense. So how do we do active learning with GBC?

The answer is randomized-prior ensembles (Osband, Aslanides, and Cassirer 2018). We train \(K\) IQN models, each augmented with a frozen random prior network whose weights are drawn once and never updated. In data-sparse regions, the ensemble members disagree because their prior networks pull them in different directions; in data-rich regions, the trained components dominate and the members converge. This disagreement is the acquisition signal: we sample where the ensemble disagrees most.

This chapter develops GBC-based active learning from first principles. We introduce the sequential design framework (Section 9.1), describe the randomized-prior ensemble and why it outperforms alternatives (Section 9.2), define the acquisition function (Section 9.3), and then evaluate on three benchmarks of increasing complexity: a smooth 2D function where GBC loses to GP-based methods (Section 9.4), a rocket aerodynamics problem where GBC wins by a factor of three (Section 9.5), and a satellite drag application that reveals a nuanced crossover between the two approaches (Section 9.6). We then address two practical considerations that arise in real deployments: acquiring multiple points per iteration (Section 9.7) and the computational cost of retraining at each step (Section 9.8). We close with practical guidance on choosing between GBC-AL and GP-AL (Section 9.9).

The chapter connects to the rest of the book as follows. The IQN surrogate from Chapter 5 provides the predictive engine; the ensemble technique extends the single-model IQN to produce epistemic uncertainty estimates. The boundary augmentation from Chapter 8 can be combined with active learning when the response surface has jump discontinuities, though we do not demonstrate this combination here. The conformal calibration from Chapter 14 can be applied post-hoc to the GBC-AL surrogate to provide formal coverage guarantees on the actively learned model.

The active learning problem also connects to broader themes in the GBC framework. The noise outsourcing theorem (Chapter 4) guarantees that any conditional distribution can be represented as a deterministic function of the input and a uniform random variable. In the active learning context, this means that the IQN surrogate can, in principle, learn the full conditional distribution \(Y \mid X = x\), not just the mean, as more data are acquired. The ensemble disagreement criterion directs acquisitions toward regions where this conditional distribution is poorly determined, and the pinball loss ensures that the acquired data are used to improve the quantile function across the entire distribution, not just the conditional mean.

In Chapter 10, we will use the IQN to approximate posterior distributions \(\theta \mid y\) by training on simulated prior-predictive pairs \((\theta, y)\). The active learning question in that context becomes: which prior-predictive pairs should we simulate to learn the posterior most efficiently? The ensemble disagreement criterion provides a natural answer: simulate parameters in regions where the posterior quantile function is most uncertain, as measured by ensemble disagreement. This is active Bayesian computation, using sequential design principles to make Bayesian inference more sample-efficient. The connection is concrete: the same RandomPriorNet code, the same ensemble_disagreement function, and the same select_next function can be applied to the Bayesian inference problem by replacing the simulator output \(y = f(x)\) with the prior-predictive pair \((\theta, y) \sim p(\theta) p(y \mid \theta)\).

9.1 The Sequential Design Problem

Consider a simulator \(f : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}\) that maps a \(d\)-dimensional input \(x \in \mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d\) to a scalar output \(y = f(x) + \varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon\) represents either stochastic noise (for Monte Carlo simulators) or is zero (for deterministic codes). Each evaluation of \(f\) is expensive, perhaps a finite-element solve, a particle simulation, or a climate model run. The goal is to build a surrogate \(\hat{f}\) that approximates \(f\) over \(\mathcal{X}\) using as few evaluations as possible.

The sequential design procedure works as follows:

  1. Initialize. Select \(n_0\) initial design points \(\{x_1, \ldots, x_{n_0}\}\), typically via Latin hypercube sampling (LHS), and evaluate the simulator to obtain \(\mathcal{D}_0 = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_0}\).

  2. Fit surrogate. Train a surrogate model on \(\mathcal{D}_n\).

  3. Acquire. Evaluate an acquisition function \(a(x)\) over a candidate set \(\mathcal{C} \subset \mathcal{X}\) and select \[ x_{n+1}^* = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{C}} \; a(x). \tag{9.1}\]

  4. Evaluate. Run the simulator at \(x_{n+1}^*\) to obtain \(y_{n+1}\), and update \(\mathcal{D}_{n+1} = \mathcal{D}_n \cup \{(x_{n+1}^*, y_{n+1})\}\).

  5. Repeat steps 2–4 until the budget is exhausted.

The entire procedure hinges on the acquisition function \(a(x)\). Random sampling, choosing \(x_{n+1}\) uniformly from \(\mathcal{C}\), ignores the surrogate’s predictions entirely and is the baseline. A good acquisition function directs evaluations toward regions where the surrogate is uncertain, achieving lower prediction error for the same budget.

Several design choices in this procedure deserve comment. The initial design \(\mathcal{D}_0\) is typically a Latin hypercube sample (LHS), which provides better space-filling coverage than a simple random sample of the same size (McKay, Beckman, and Conover 1979). The size \(n_0\) should be large enough to provide at least minimal coverage of the input space, a common rule of thumb is \(n_0 = 10d\) where \(d\) is the input dimension, but small enough to leave most of the budget for sequential acquisition, where the active learning criterion can direct evaluations more efficiently than the initial space-filling design. For the benchmarks in this chapter, we use \(n_0 = 10\) for \(d = 2\), \(n_0 = 50\) for \(d = 3\), and \(n_0 = 110\) for \(d = 7\), following the protocols of Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon (2023).

The candidate set \(\mathcal{C}\) is typically a large random or quasi-random sample over \(\mathcal{X}\) (e.g., 10,000 Sobol points), and the acquisition function is evaluated exhaustively over \(\mathcal{C}\) rather than optimized continuously. This discretization introduces a small approximation error but avoids the difficulty of optimizing the acquisition function over a continuous domain, which can be non-convex and multimodal. The size of \(\mathcal{C}\) matters: too small and the best candidate may miss important regions; too large and the evaluation cost becomes prohibitive. In our experiments, \(|\mathcal{C}| = 10{,}000\) provides a good balance for \(d \leq 7\). For higher dimensions, larger candidate sets may be needed, though the evaluation cost remains modest because the ensemble forward pass is fast on GPUs.

The stopping criterion is typically a fixed budget: “evaluate the simulator \(n_{\text{total}}\) times.” More sophisticated stopping criteria are possible, for example, stop when the ensemble disagreement drops below a threshold, or when the RMSE on a held-out validation set stabilizes, but fixed budgets are the standard in the computer experiments literature and are what we use here.

The candidate pool \(\mathcal{C}\) can also be refreshed during the active learning loop. Starting with a fixed candidate pool and removing selected points (as in the active_learning_loop code above) gradually depletes the pool, potentially leaving gaps in regions where the remaining candidates are sparse. An alternative is to regenerate the candidate pool at each iteration by sampling a fresh set of quasi-random points. This ensures that the pool always covers the input space uniformly, at the cost of slightly increased computational overhead for generating new Sobol sequences at each iteration. For the benchmarks in this chapter, the fixed pool is sufficient because the initial pool (10,000 points) is much larger than the number of acquisitions (at most 390), so depletion is not a concern. For problems with very large acquisition budgets (\(n > 5{,}000\)), refreshing the candidate pool is recommended to avoid gaps in coverage.

A more sophisticated approach is gradient-based acquisition optimization: instead of evaluating the disagreement on a discrete candidate pool, compute the gradient of the disagreement with respect to the input \(x\) and use gradient ascent to find the continuous maximizer. This requires differentiating through the ensemble’s forward passes, which is straightforward in PyTorch using torch.autograd. The advantage is that gradient-based optimization can find sharp peaks in the disagreement surface that might fall between candidate pool points, potentially identifying features that a coarse candidate pool would miss. The disadvantage is that the optimization landscape is typically non-convex, requiring multiple random restarts to avoid local maxima. In our experiments, we found that 50 random restarts with L-BFGS optimization produced essentially the same acquisition points as a 10,000-point candidate pool, but took longer to compute. In practice, the discrete candidate pool approach is simpler, faster, and produces nearly identical results because the disagreement surface is smooth enough that a 10,000-point candidate pool captures its features adequately. We recommend the gradient-based approach only for very high-dimensional problems (\(d > 20\)) where a candidate pool of adequate density would be prohibitively large.

The Gaussian process provides the canonical acquisition function for surrogate-based sequential design. Because the GP posterior is a Gaussian distribution at every point, the posterior variance \(\sigma^2_n(x) = \text{Var}[f(x) \mid \mathcal{D}_n]\) is available in closed form. The Active Learning Cohn (ALC) criterion (Cohn 1994) selects the point that maximally reduces the integrated posterior variance: \[ x_{n+1}^{\text{ALC}} = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{C}} \int_{\mathcal{X}} \bigl[\sigma^2_n(x') - \sigma^2_{n+1}(x')\bigr] \, dx', \tag{9.2}\] where \(\sigma^2_{n+1}(x')\) is the posterior variance that would result from adding \(x\) to the training set (regardless of the observed \(y\), since GP variance depends only on input locations). The integral is typically approximated over a reference grid.

ALC is model-based (uses the GP posterior), look-ahead (considers the effect of adding a point), and global (integrates over the entire input space). Its effectiveness has been demonstrated across many computer experiments benchmarks (Gramacy 2020; Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon 2023).

Other GP-based acquisition functions share these properties. Expected improvement (EI) (Jones, Schonlau, and Welch 1998), originally developed for optimization rather than global approximation, selects the point where the expected improvement over the current best observation is maximized; it is the standard criterion for Bayesian optimization. Integrated mean squared prediction error (IMSPE) is closely related to ALC but integrates the posterior mean squared error rather than the variance reduction. For global surrogate building (as opposed to optimization), ALC and IMSPE are the most common choices.

Active learning for surrogate building (our focus here) differs from active learning for optimization (Bayesian optimization). Surrogate building aims to learn the function everywhere: a global approximation with low RMSE over the entire input domain. Optimization aims to find the input that maximizes (or minimizes) the function, without caring about the function’s value at other points. EI is designed for the optimization setting: it concentrates evaluations near the current best observation and in high-uncertainty regions that might contain a better optimum. ALC is designed for the surrogate setting: it distributes evaluations to reduce global prediction uncertainty. The ensemble disagreement criterion is closer in spirit to ALC than to EI, because it measures global uncertainty rather than local improvement potential. We use DGP+ALC as the GP comparator throughout this chapter because it represents the state of the art for GP-based sequential design for computer experiments (Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon 2023).

The challenge for GBC is that neural networks do not have a posterior variance. An IQN trained on \(\mathcal{D}_n\) produces a point prediction and predictive quantiles, but these reflect aleatoric uncertainty (noise in \(y \mid x\)), not epistemic uncertainty (uncertainty about the function \(f\) due to limited data). A well-trained IQN in a data-sparse region may produce narrow, confident intervals, not because the function is well-determined, but because the network has no mechanism to signal “I have not seen data here.”

This distinction is the central obstacle for neural-network-based active learning, and it is what the randomized-prior ensemble is designed to overcome.

To make the distinction concrete, consider an IQN trained on 20 data points in \([0,1]^2\), with all points concentrated in \([0, 0.5]^2\). The IQN’s predicted 90% interval at \(x = (0.3, 0.3)\) (a data-rich region) might be \([1.2, 1.8]\), and at \(x = (0.9, 0.9)\) (a data-free region) it might also be \([1.2, 1.8]\). Both intervals have the same width, yet they mean entirely different things. The first interval reflects genuine noise in the data; the second reflects the network’s extrapolation, which has no basis in observation. A GP would assign a wide posterior variance at \((0.9, 0.9)\) because it is far from training points, and a narrow variance at \((0.3, 0.3)\) where data constrain the posterior. The IQN makes no such distinction because its intervals are driven by the loss function, which only sees the training data.

This is not a bug in the IQN, it is a fundamental consequence of the neural network’s parametric structure. The IQN is trained to minimize the pinball loss over the training distribution, which does not cover \((0.9, 0.9)\). The network’s behavior at \((0.9, 0.9)\) is determined by its inductive bias (the architecture, activation functions, and implicit regularization of SGD) rather than by any observed data. Different random seeds will produce different IQN models that agree near \((0.3, 0.3)\) (where the data constrain the predictions) but disagree near \((0.9, 0.9)\) (where the predictions are unconstrained). This observation is the foundation for the ensemble-based approach to epistemic uncertainty that we develop in the next section.

The aleatoric/epistemic distinction has practical consequences beyond acquisition function design. When reporting the uncertainty of a GBC surrogate’s predictions, it is important to distinguish between the two types. The IQN’s quantile estimates (e.g., the 90% prediction interval) reflect aleatoric uncertainty and should be reported as such. The ensemble disagreement reflects epistemic uncertainty and should be reported separately. Conflating the two, for example, by treating the ensemble spread as a prediction interval, can lead to miscalibrated uncertainty estimates.

9.2 Randomized-Prior Ensembles

The idea of Osband, Aslanides, and Cassirer (2018), originally developed for exploration in reinforcement learning, is to augment each ensemble member with a frozen random prior network that injects structural diversity. Each member \(k\) computes \[ G_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) = g_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) + \alpha \, g_{0,k}(\tau, x), \tag{9.3}\] where \(g_{\phi_k}\) is a standard trainable IQN, \(g_{0,k}\) is a frozen network whose weights are drawn from an initialization distribution (e.g., Xavier uniform) at the start of training and never updated, and \(\alpha > 0\) is a prior scale parameter. In our experiments, we use \(\alpha = 0.5\) and \(K = 3\) ensemble members.

TipDefinition 9.1: Randomized-Prior IQN Ensemble

A randomized-prior IQN ensemble of size \(K\) consists of \(K\) IQN models, each with output \[ G_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) = g_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) + \alpha \, g_{0,k}(\tau, x), \quad k = 1, \ldots, K, \] where \(g_{\phi_k}\) is trainable, \(g_{0,k}\) has frozen random weights, and \(\alpha > 0\) controls the prior strength. All \(K\) members are trained independently on the same data \(\mathcal{D}_n\) with different random seeds.

The prior scale \(\alpha\) controls the strength of the perturbation. When \(\alpha\) is too small, the frozen network’s contribution is negligible and the ensemble collapses to a standard ensemble, all members converge to the same predictions everywhere, regardless of data density, and the disagreement signal vanishes. When \(\alpha\) is too large, the frozen network dominates even in data-rich regions, preventing the trainable component from fitting the data and producing high disagreement everywhere, the acquisition signal becomes uninformative because it no longer distinguishes data-rich from data-sparse regions. The value \(\alpha = 0.5\) is a compromise that works well across the benchmarks in this chapter.

The choice of \(\alpha\) is analogous to the choice of kernel variance \(\sigma_f^2\) in a GP: it sets the scale of the prior’s contribution to the prediction. In a GP, \(\sigma_f^2\) is typically estimated from the data via maximum marginal likelihood. For the randomized-prior ensemble, there is no analogous marginal likelihood to optimize, so \(\alpha\) must be set by the user or tuned via cross-validation. In practice, values in the range \([0.1, 1.0]\) work well for most problems; the sensitivity to \(\alpha\) within this range is modest, as we will see in Exercise 9.3.

The choice of \(K = 3\) ensemble members is also a compromise: more members would provide a more stable estimate of disagreement, but each additional member multiplies the retraining cost proportionally. In our experiments, the improvement from \(K = 3\) to \(K = 5\) was typically less than 5%, while the retraining cost increased by 67%. With \(K = 2\) members, the disagreement reduces to the absolute difference between two predictions, which is a noisy estimator of the standard deviation. We found \(K = 2\) to be adequate for exploratory runs but too noisy for definitive benchmarks. The choice \(K = 3\) provides sufficient stability for the acquisition function while keeping the computational cost manageable. We recommend starting with \(K = 3\) for exploratory experiments and increasing to \(K = 5\) only if the acquisition function appears noisy (i.e., if the selected points change substantially across independent runs with different random seeds).

Why does the randomized-prior mechanism work? The intuition is rooted in the contrast between GP posteriors and neural network ensembles.

GP posterior samples are draws from a stochastic process whose diversity is guaranteed by the posterior covariance. Even as \(n \to \infty\), the posterior samples remain distinct wherever posterior uncertainty is nonzero. The GP prior encodes a belief about function smoothness (via the kernel), and the posterior shrinks this belief toward the data. In regions far from training points, the posterior is wide, and samples are diverse.

Standard NN ensembles, multiple networks trained with different random seeds, behave very differently. As \(n\) grows, SGD drives all members toward the same empirical risk minimizer. With enough data, the members converge to nearly identical predictions everywhere, including regions far from the training data. The ensemble “collapses” and disagreement vanishes, even where epistemic uncertainty should be high. This collapse has been documented extensively in the deep learning literature (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel, and Blundell 2017; Fort, Hu, and Lakshminarayanan 2019): while deep ensembles provide useful uncertainty estimates on classification tasks where the function is complex enough that different random initializations explore different modes of the loss landscape, they are unreliable for regression tasks with smooth functions and moderate \(n\), which is precisely the setting of computer experiments.

Bootstrap ensembles partially restore diversity by training each member on a resampled version of the data. The idea is sound in principle: each member sees a different version of the data, so they learn slightly different functions, and their disagreement reflects the sensitivity of the predictions to the specific training points. This is the basis of bagging (Breiman 1996), which is highly effective for decision trees and random forests. But at small \(n\), exactly the regime where active learning operates, bootstrap resampling produces degenerate training sets (some points duplicated, others missing entirely), leading to poorly trained individual members. Neural networks are much more sensitive to training set perturbations than decision trees, because the loss landscape is highly non-convex and a degenerate training set can cause the optimizer to converge to a qualitatively different local minimum. In our experiments, bootstrap ensembles were consistently the worst acquisition strategy: 17% worse than random selection on a 7D benchmark and 30% worse on a satellite drag problem.

Randomized priors solve the collapse problem by injecting structural diversity that persists regardless of \(n\). In data-sparse regions, the trainable component \(g_{\phi_k}\) has not seen enough data to learn the function and therefore cannot cancel the random contribution \(\alpha \, g_{0,k}\); different members make different predictions because their prior networks differ, so disagreement is high, signaling epistemic uncertainty. In data-rich regions, the trainable component has converged to the true function and its magnitude dominates the prior perturbation; all members agree and disagreement is low, correctly indicating that the function is well-determined.

There is a useful analogy with regularization in classical statistics. Ridge regression adds a penalty \(\lambda \|\beta\|_2^2\) to the least-squares loss, shrinking the coefficient vector toward zero. The penalty acts as a prior: it encodes the belief that the coefficients should be small unless the data provide strong evidence otherwise. The randomized prior plays a similar role: it encodes the belief that the function should look like the frozen random network unless the data provide strong evidence otherwise. The key difference is that the prior is different for each ensemble member, so the “default” prediction varies across members. In regions where the data are informative, all members are pulled toward the same fit (just as ridge regression with strong data approaches OLS). In regions where the data are uninformative, each member stays near its own random prior, creating disagreement.

This analogy can be pushed further. In Bayesian linear regression, the posterior variance is \(\Sigma_{\text{post}} = (\Sigma_{\text{prior}}^{-1} + X^\top X / \sigma^2)^{-1}\). Where \(X^\top X\) is large (data-rich), the posterior variance is small regardless of the prior. Where \(X^\top X\) is zero (data-free), the posterior variance equals the prior variance. The randomized-prior ensemble exhibits exactly this behavior: where data are abundant, the trainable component dominates and the prior’s contribution is negligible; where data are absent, the prior network determines the prediction and different members disagree. The ensemble disagreement is thus a nonparametric, distribution-free analog of the Bayesian posterior variance, obtained without specifying a likelihood, a prior distribution, or a posterior inference algorithm.

To see this mathematically, consider two ensemble members \(k\) and \(k'\) at a point \(x\) where no training data are available. Their predictions are \[ G_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) = g_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) + \alpha \, g_{0,k}(\tau, x), \quad G_{\phi_{k'}}(\tau, x) = g_{\phi_{k'}}(\tau, x) + \alpha \, g_{0,k'}(\tau, x). \] Without data near \(x\), the trainable components \(g_{\phi_k}\) and \(g_{\phi_{k'}}\) are determined by the loss at distant training points and are roughly equal (both extrapolate similarly). The disagreement is therefore driven by the frozen prior difference: \(G_{\phi_k} - G_{\phi_{k'}} \approx \alpha(g_{0,k} - g_{0,k'})\), which is nonzero because \(g_{0,k}\) and \(g_{0,k'}\) have different random weights. At a point \(x'\) where training data are abundant, the trainable components have each converged to approximately \(f(x') - \alpha \, g_{0,k}(\tau, x')\), so \(G_{\phi_k}(\tau, x') \approx f(x')\) regardless of \(k\), and the disagreement vanishes.

This is exactly the behavior we want from an acquisition signal: high where we need more data, low where we have enough. The randomized-prior ensemble achieves this without any of the machinery required by GP posteriors: no kernel function, no Cholesky factorization, no posterior sampling. The implementation is simple: define a frozen random network, add it to the trainable network, and train with the standard pinball loss. The result is a practical epistemic uncertainty estimate that works for any neural network architecture, including the IQN.

The RandomPriorNet class implements this idea for a simple MLP. The same principle applies to IQN members, where we add a frozen random network to the IQN’s output.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np

class RandomPriorNet(nn.Module):
    """Trainable MLP + frozen random prior (Osband et al. 2018).

    The output is: train_net(x) + prior_scale * prior_net(x),
    where prior_net has frozen random weights.
    """
    def __init__(self, xdim, hdim=128, nlayers=3, prior_scale=0.5):
        super().__init__()
        # Trainable network
        layers = [nn.Linear(xdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        for _ in range(nlayers - 1):
            layers += [nn.Linear(hdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        layers.append(nn.Linear(hdim, 1))
        self.train_net = nn.Sequential(*layers)

        # Frozen prior network (same architecture, random weights)
        prior_layers = [nn.Linear(xdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        for _ in range(nlayers - 1):
            prior_layers += [nn.Linear(hdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        prior_layers.append(nn.Linear(hdim, 1))
        self.prior_net = nn.Sequential(*prior_layers)
        for p in self.prior_net.parameters():
            p.requires_grad = False  # freeze!

        self.prior_scale = prior_scale

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.train_net(x) + self.prior_scale * self.prior_net(x)

# Verify: three ensemble members give different predictions at the same point
torch.manual_seed(0)
ensemble = [RandomPriorNet(xdim=2, hdim=64, nlayers=2, prior_scale=0.5)
            for _ in range(3)]

x_test = torch.tensor([[0.5, 0.5]])  # a single test point
for k, model in enumerate(ensemble):
    with torch.no_grad():
        pred = model(x_test).item()
    print(f"  Member {k}: prediction = {pred:.4f}")

Even before training, the three members produce different predictions at the same input because their frozen prior networks have different random weights. After training on data, members will agree in data-rich regions and continue to disagree in data-sparse regions. The training data “wash out” the prior perturbation where they are abundant, but in data-sparse regions the perturbation persists, creating a measurable signal of epistemic uncertainty.

The connection to Bayesian reasoning is explicit. In a GP, the posterior variance at a point \(x\) measures how much the posterior distribution of \(f(x)\) would change if we obtained different training data. The randomized-prior ensemble achieves something analogous: the ensemble disagreement at \(x\) measures how much the prediction at \(x\) would change if we used a different prior network. In data-rich regions, the prediction is determined by the data regardless of the prior, so disagreement is low. In data-sparse regions, the prediction depends on the prior, so disagreement is high. This is the neural-network analog of the GP’s prior-to-posterior contraction.

There is an important subtlety regarding the frozen prior network’s architecture. In our implementation, the prior network has the same architecture (depth, width, activation function) as the trainable network. This is a design choice, not a requirement. Using a simpler prior network (e.g., a single-layer MLP with fewer hidden units) would produce smoother prior perturbations, biasing the disagreement signal toward large-scale spatial variation rather than fine-grained variation. Using a more complex prior network would produce more rapidly varying perturbations, potentially creating spurious disagreement peaks at small spatial scales. We found that matching the architectures provides a good balance: the prior perturbation varies at roughly the same spatial scale as the trainable network’s predictions, so the disagreement signal is sensitive to the same features that the surrogate is trying to learn.

The weight initialization distribution for the frozen network also matters. We use the default PyTorch initialization (Kaiming uniform for linear layers), which produces weights scaled to maintain unit variance through the network. Other initializations would produce different prior perturbation scales. For instance, initializing the frozen network with larger weights would effectively increase \(\alpha\) without changing the prior scale parameter, while initializing with smaller weights would decrease the effective prior contribution. In practice, the combination of default initialization and \(\alpha = 0.5\) produces perturbations of appropriate magnitude relative to the trainable network’s predictions, and we have not found it necessary to tune the initialization distribution separately.

One might also consider using structured prior networks rather than random ones. For example, if the practitioner has prior knowledge about the response surface’s structure, such as periodicity, monotonicity, or known symmetries, this knowledge could be encoded in the frozen prior network’s architecture or initialization. A periodic prior network (using sinusoidal activation functions) would produce periodic perturbations, making the ensemble more sensitive to periodic features in the response surface. We have not explored structured priors in this chapter, but they represent a natural extension that would bridge the gap between the GP’s explicit prior (encoded in the kernel function) and the randomized prior’s implicit prior (encoded in the random network architecture).

We tested four alternative uncertainty quantification strategies for active learning. All were inferior to randomized priors on our benchmarks.

Each of these failure modes deserves closer examination, because they illuminate why epistemic uncertainty is fundamentally difficult for neural networks.

Bootstrap ensembles fail because active learning operates in the small-\(n\) regime where bootstrap resampling is most destructive. With \(n = 50\) training points and sampling with replacement, approximately \(1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \approx 63\%\) of the original points appear in each resample, meaning roughly a third of the data is missing from each member’s training set. When \(n\) is small, those missing points may constitute the only observations in an entire region of input space, so the ensemble member trained without them produces wildly inaccurate predictions there, not because epistemic uncertainty is high, but because the training set is deficient. The resulting disagreement is driven by data poverty rather than genuine model uncertainty, creating an acquisition signal that is essentially noise.

The problem is compounded by the sequential nature of active learning. After the first acquisition, the training set grows to \(n + 1\) points. The bootstrap resample of this slightly larger set will still be degenerate, and the new point (which was selected based on the noisy acquisition signal) may not improve the situation. Over multiple iterations, the bootstrap ensemble can enter a pathological cycle: it selects uninformative points because its disagreement signal is noisy, and the uninformative points do not improve the disagreement signal, so the next selection is equally uninformative. In our 7D proxy benchmark, bootstrap ensemble acquisition performed 17% worse than uniform random sampling, meaning the acquisition signal was actively harmful, it directed acquisitions to worse locations than random chance would have produced. On the SatMini benchmark, the degradation was 30%, confirming that bootstrap ensembles are unsuitable for active learning with neural networks at small sample sizes.

SNGP (Spectral Normalized GP) replaces the neural network’s last layer with a GP, using spectral normalization of the preceding layers to preserve distance information. The idea is appealing: get the GP’s calibrated uncertainty from the last layer while retaining the neural network’s feature extraction in the preceding layers. The spectral normalization ensures that the feature map does not collapse distances, if two inputs \(x\) and \(x'\) are far apart in the input space, they remain far apart in the feature space, so the GP last layer can assign high uncertainty between them. In practice, however, the spectral normalization constraint limits the network’s representational capacity, and the GP last layer operates in a learned feature space whose geometry may not support meaningful distance-based uncertainty for sequential design. The feature space changes each time the network is retrained (because spectral normalization does not fix the feature map, only constrain it), so the GP’s uncertainty estimates are not comparable across iterations. On our low-dimensional benchmarks (\(d = 2, 3\)), SNGP performed comparably to randomized priors, but on \(d = 7\) it produced acquisition signals that were no better than random selection. We suspect this is because the spectral normalization becomes increasingly conservative in high dimensions, constraining the network’s ability to learn a useful feature representation while providing diminishing uncertainty benefits.

Anchored ensembles (Pearce et al. 2020) add an L2 penalty that pulls each member’s weights toward their random initialization, rather than freezing the prior network entirely. The loss for member \(k\) becomes \(\mathcal{L}_k = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda \|\phi_k - \phi_{0,k}\|_2^2\), where \(\phi_{0,k}\) are the random initial weights and \(\lambda\) controls the penalty strength. This is mathematically similar to the randomized-prior approach, in both cases, member predictions in data-sparse regions are influenced by their random initialization. However, the anchored approach requires tuning the regularization strength \(\lambda\), and in sequential settings where the training set changes at every iteration, the optimal regularization strength changes too. When \(\lambda\) is too large, the trainable weights cannot move far from initialization and the ensemble underfits the data. When \(\lambda\) is too small, the weights converge to the empirical risk minimizer and the ensemble collapses, just like a standard ensemble. We found that anchored ensembles were less stable across iterations than randomized priors, with occasional pathological rounds where the acquisition signal collapsed. The frozen-prior approach avoids this tuning problem entirely by separating the trainable and prior components architecturally rather than through regularization.

Single-model quantile spread is the most natural GBC approach and therefore the most instructive failure. The IQN produces quantile estimates \(\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)\) for any \(\tau\), so a natural acquisition function is the predicted interval width: \[ a_{\text{spread}}(x) = \hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x) - \hat{Q}(0.05 \mid x). \tag{9.4}\] Note: this uses a 90% predictive interval (\(\tau \in \{0.05, 0.95\}\)), chosen here because it is more sensitive to local variation in data-sparse regions than the 95% interval (\(\tau \in \{0.025, 0.975\}\)) used elsewhere in the book. The rest of the book standardizes on \(\hat{Q}(0.975) - \hat{Q}(0.025)\); either convention is valid, but this chapter uses the 90% interval throughout.

This measures the IQN’s predictive uncertainty. The problem is that this spread captures aleatoric uncertainty (noise in \(y \mid x\)) rather than epistemic uncertainty (ignorance about \(f\)). Worse, as the IQN overfits the growing training set in sequential settings, the predictive spread can actually shrink in data-sparse regions, directing acquisition away from where it is needed most. The mechanism is subtle: in regions with no data, the IQN’s quantile function is determined entirely by the random initialization and the implicit regularization of SGD. An overparameterized network trained to convergence on the available data can produce any shape of quantile function in data-free regions, including narrow intervals that appear confident. As the training set grows in sequential settings, the IQN fits the data more tightly, and the quantile spread in data-free regions can shrink even further, the network becomes more confident in its extrapolation even though no new data have appeared in those regions. This is the opposite of what we want: the acquisition function should increase in data-free regions as the model becomes better calibrated in data-rich regions, directing future acquisitions toward the remaining gaps.

The quantile spread failure has a formal analog in the GP literature: a GP with a misspecified noise variance will produce posterior intervals that are too narrow, causing the ALC criterion to underweight uncertain regions. The difference is that the GP’s miscalibration can be diagnosed and corrected by estimating the noise variance from residuals, while the IQN’s quantile spread miscalibration has no simple fix within the single-model framework. The ensemble disagreement criterion, described next, avoids this failure mode by measuring inter-model variation rather than intra-model variation.

9.3 Acquisition Functions

We now have a mechanism (randomized-prior ensembles) for generating diverse predictions that reflect epistemic uncertainty. The next step is to convert this diversity into a scalar acquisition function, a number at each candidate point that tells us how valuable it would be to evaluate the simulator there. The acquisition function must satisfy two properties: it should be high in regions where the surrogate is uncertain (encouraging exploration) and it should decrease after a point is acquired in that region (preventing the algorithm from repeatedly selecting the same location). The ensemble disagreement satisfies both properties naturally.

Before defining the acquisition function, we note that other ways of converting ensemble diversity into a scalar score are possible. One alternative is the range of predictions: \(a_{\text{range}}(x) = \max_k \tilde{y}_k(x) - \min_k \tilde{y}_k(x)\). The range is more sensitive to outlier ensemble members than the standard deviation, which may be desirable if one member has detected a feature that the others have missed. Another alternative is the interquartile range of predictions, which is more robust to outliers but requires \(K \geq 4\) ensemble members. We use the standard deviation (equivalently, the variance) throughout this chapter because it is the most common choice in the ensemble learning literature and performs well with \(K = 3\) members.

With a trained randomized-prior ensemble of \(K\) IQN members, we define the acquisition function as the ensemble disagreement: the standard deviation of median predictions across members.

If multiple models trained on the same data make different predictions at a point \(x\), then the data have not determined the function value at \(x\). The standard deviation across predictions quantifies this indeterminacy. What makes randomized priors special is that they guarantee this disagreement persists in data-sparse regions, whereas a standard ensemble would eventually collapse as all members converge to the same fit.

TipDefinition 9.2: Ensemble Disagreement Acquisition

Given \(K\) ensemble members with median predictions \(\tilde{y}_k(x) = \text{median}_b \, G_{\phi_k}(\tau^{(b)}, x)\) for \(b = 1, \ldots, B\) samples, the ensemble disagreement at \(x\) is \[ a(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K-1} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \bigl(\tilde{y}_k(x) - \bar{\tilde{y}}(x)\bigr)^2}, \quad \bar{\tilde{y}}(x) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{y}_k(x). \tag{9.5}\] The next acquisition point is \(x_{n+1}^* = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{C}} \, a(x)\).

In practice, we compute the median prediction of each member by averaging \(B = 100\) forward passes with \(\tau^{(b)} \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\) and taking the empirical median. The disagreement is then the standard deviation across the \(K\) medians.

Note that we use the median rather than the mean of each member’s predictions. The median is more robust to the long tails that can arise in IQN predictions at extreme quantile levels, especially in the early rounds of active learning when the ensemble members are not yet well-trained. This robustness matters: if one ensemble member produces an outlier prediction at \(\tau = 0.99\), it will inflate the mean but not the median, and the disagreement computed from medians will better reflect the genuine spread in the members’ central estimates.

An alternative formulation computes the disagreement not at the median but at a specific quantile level \(\tau_0\). Setting \(\tau_0 = 0.5\) (the median) is the most natural choice for symmetric distributions, but for skewed distributions one might want to acquire points where the ensemble disagrees most at the tails. For instance, setting \(\tau_0 = 0.95\) would direct acquisitions toward regions where the upper tail of the conditional distribution is most uncertain. This could be useful for reliability engineering applications where the tail behavior matters more than the central tendency. We have not explored this direction systematically, but it is a natural extension of the framework.

One could also combine disagreement across multiple quantile levels into a single acquisition function. For example, one could compute the disagreement at \(\tau \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9\}\) and take the maximum across these levels. This would direct acquisitions toward regions where the ensemble disagrees about any aspect of the conditional distribution, not just the center. On heteroscedastic problems where the noise level varies across the input space, this multi-quantile acquisition function might identify regions where the ensemble members disagree about the width of the conditional distribution even if they agree about its center.

def ensemble_disagreement(models, X):
    """Compute ensemble disagreement as acquisition function.

    Args:
        models: list of K trained ensemble members
        X: (n_candidates, d) tensor of candidate points

    Returns:
        (n_candidates,) array of disagreement scores
    """
    preds = []
    for m in models:
        m.eval()
        with torch.no_grad():
            preds.append(m(X).squeeze(-1).cpu().numpy())
    preds = np.stack(preds)  # (K, n_candidates)
    return preds.std(axis=0, ddof=1)  # sample std across ensemble members (matches Definition 9.2)

def select_next(models, X_candidates, batch_size=1):
    """Select next points by maximum disagreement.

    Args:
        models: list of ensemble members
        X_candidates: (n_cand, d) candidate pool
        batch_size: number of points to select

    Returns:
        Indices into X_candidates of selected points.
    """
    scores = ensemble_disagreement(models, X_candidates)
    return np.argsort(scores)[-batch_size:][::-1].copy()

The full active learning loop retrains the ensemble from scratch on the augmented dataset at each iteration (rather than fine-tuning, which can lead to catastrophic forgetting of the prior network’s contribution), computes the disagreement on the candidate pool, selects the maximizer, and repeats. The implementation choices are: (1) retraining from scratch at each iteration (as opposed to warm-starting), (2) removing selected points from the candidate pool (to avoid reselecting the same point), and (3) using a fixed number of training epochs rather than an adaptive convergence criterion. We will discuss the rationale for choice (1) in Section 9.8; choices (2) and (3) are standard practice in the active learning literature.

def active_learning_loop(
    f_simulator,        # simulator function: x -> y
    X_init, y_init,     # initial design
    X_candidates,       # candidate pool
    n_acquire,          # number of points to acquire
    K=3,                # ensemble size
    epochs=3000,        # training epochs per round
):
    """GBC active learning with randomized-prior ensemble.

    train_model: trains a RandomPriorNet on (X, y) for the given
    number of epochs using Adam and pinball loss. In practice, use
    gbc.ensemble.train_het_mlp or a custom training loop.
    """
    X_train = X_init.clone()
    y_train = y_init.clone()

    for step in range(n_acquire):
        # Train K ensemble members from scratch
        ensemble = []
        for k in range(K):
            model = RandomPriorNet(
                xdim=X_train.shape[1], hdim=128,
                nlayers=3, prior_scale=0.5
            )
            train_model(model, X_train, y_train, epochs=epochs)
            ensemble.append(model)

        # Select next point by maximum disagreement
        idx = select_next(ensemble, X_candidates, batch_size=1).item()
        x_new = X_candidates[idx].unsqueeze(0)   # (1, d)
        y_new = f_simulator(x_new).reshape(-1)

        # Update training set
        X_train = torch.cat([X_train, x_new])
        y_train = torch.cat([y_train, y_new])

        # Remove selected point from candidate pool
        mask = torch.ones(len(X_candidates), dtype=torch.bool)
        mask[idx] = False
        X_candidates = X_candidates[mask]

    return X_train, y_train

The GP’s ALC criterion (Eq. 9.2) and the ensemble disagreement criterion (Eq. 9.5) target the same goal, identifying regions of high epistemic uncertainty, but through fundamentally different mechanisms.

The table reveals a fundamental architectural difference: the GP computes uncertainty analytically from a probabilistic model, while GBC computes uncertainty empirically from an ensemble of point estimators. This difference has consequences that go beyond computational cost. The GP’s uncertainty is calibrated by construction (assuming the kernel is correctly specified): the posterior variance at a test point has a precise Bayesian interpretation as the expected squared error of the posterior mean. The ensemble disagreement has no such calibration guarantee, it is a heuristic measure of inter-model variation that correlates with true epistemic uncertainty but is not equal to it. On our benchmarks, the correlation between ensemble disagreement and true squared error ranges from 0.6 to 0.8 depending on the problem, which is sufficient for acquisition but not for downstream uncertainty quantification.

The GP’s look-ahead property is a genuine advantage: ALC does not just find the point of highest uncertainty, it finds the point whose addition would most reduce total uncertainty. The ensemble disagreement criterion is greedy, it picks the point of highest current disagreement without considering the downstream effect. In practice, the greedy strategy works well because ensemble disagreement tends to be highest in the same regions where adding a point would most reduce global uncertainty, but the correspondence is not exact.

The GP’s smoothness prior (discussed in Section 7.1.3) is both a strength and a weakness: powerful regularization on smooth functions with small \(n\), but a poor fit for discontinuous or heterogeneous response surfaces.

There is a deeper connection between ALC and ensemble disagreement. Both criteria are attempting to estimate the same quantity: the reduction in prediction error that would result from evaluating the simulator at a candidate point. ALC estimates this directly, using the GP’s posterior to compute the expected variance reduction. Ensemble disagreement estimates it indirectly: points of high disagreement are points where the current surrogate is unreliable, and evaluating the simulator there will reduce the disagreement (and hence the prediction error) by providing data that resolves the inter-model ambiguity. The two criteria would be identical if the ensemble disagreement were perfectly calibrated, that is, if the standard deviation of ensemble predictions equaled the true standard deviation of the prediction error. In practice, the calibration is imperfect, but the correlation between disagreement and true error is strong enough (typically 0.6–0.8 in our experiments) that the greedy selection of maximum-disagreement points produces near-optimal acquisition sequences.

The computational architecture of the two approaches also differs in a way that matters for implementation. The per-candidate cost is \(O(n^2)\) for GP-ALC versus \(O(Kw^2L)\) for the ensemble, comparable at small \(n\), but the GP cost grows quadratically while the network cost is independent of \(n\). See Section 7.6 for the full flop-count comparison.

There is another operational difference that matters in practice: the GP-based ALC criterion is typically implemented in R (via packages like deepgp or hetGP), while the IQN ensemble is implemented in Python/PyTorch. The GP implementation runs on CPU, while the IQN runs on GPU. This means that the wall-clock times for the two methods are not directly comparable, the GPU provides a substantial constant-factor speedup for the matrix operations involved in the network’s forward and backward passes. A fair comparison would require either running both methods on the same hardware or reporting asymptotic operation counts rather than wall-clock times. We have tried to do the latter in Table 9.2, but the reader should be aware that the wall-clock advantage of GBC-AL is partly architectural (GPU vs. CPU) rather than purely algorithmic.

9.4 2D Exponential Benchmark

We now evaluate GBC-AL on three benchmarks from the computer experiments literature, following the experimental protocol of Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon (2023) exactly. Each benchmark reveals a different aspect of the GBC-vs-GP tradeoff. The 2D Exponential (Section 9.4) is smooth, low-dimensional, and favors the GP. The Rocket LGBB (Section 9.5) has a sharp nonlinear ridge and favors GBC. The Satellite GRACE (Section 9.6) is smooth and high-dimensional, producing a crossover where GBC leads early and the GP leads late. Together, these three benchmarks map out the frontier of GBC-AL’s advantages and limitations.

The benchmarks are ordered by increasing complexity and increasing favorability to GBC-AL. This ordering is deliberate: we begin with a benchmark where GBC loses (2D Exponential) to establish that the comparison is fair and to identify the conditions under which GPs have a genuine advantage. We then move to a benchmark where GBC wins decisively (Rocket LGBB) to demonstrate the potential of the method. Finally, we examine a benchmark with a nuanced crossover (Satellite GRACE) that captures the full complexity of the GBC-vs-GP tradeoff. Three supplementary benchmarks (SatMini, 7D Proxy, and the full Satellite at \(n = 500\)) provide additional evidence and confirm the patterns observed in the primary benchmarks.

The 2D Exponential function is the simplest benchmark from Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon (2023): \[ f(x_1, x_2) = 10 \, x_1 \exp(-x_1^2 - x_2^2), \quad (x_1, x_2) \in [-2, 2]^2, \tag{9.6}\] with additive Gaussian noise \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, 0.1^2)\). The function is smooth, unimodal, and well-suited to a squared-exponential kernel. It is a setting where GPs should excel, and they do.

Following Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon (2023), we start with \(n_0 = 10\) initial LHS points and acquire 90 additional points one at a time (\(n_{\text{total}} = 100\)), retraining after each acquisition. We use \(K = 3\) randomized-prior IQN members with 3,000 training epochs per round. The comparator is DGP+ALC, a two-layer deep GP with elliptical slice sampling, using the ALC acquisition criterion (Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon 2023). We run 30 independent replicates.

The DGP achieves 21% lower RMSE than GBC-AL. This gap is structural, not a tuning failure. We investigated several modifications (more ensemble members, longer training, different prior scales) and none closed the gap substantially.

Why does GBC lose here? Three factors combine to favor the GP on this benchmark, and understanding each one clarifies when GBC-AL should and should not be used.

The first factor is exploration–exploitation balance. ALC is a look-ahead criterion: it evaluates how much adding a candidate point would reduce the integrated posterior variance over the entire domain. This means ALC inherently balances exploration (sampling in regions of high uncertainty) with exploitation (refining predictions where the function varies most). The ensemble disagreement criterion, by contrast, is greedy, it simply picks the point of maximum current disagreement. On a smooth 2D surface, ALC’s ability to plan ahead matters: it recognizes that adding a point at one location will reduce uncertainty not just locally but over a neighborhood determined by the kernel length-scale. The greedy GBC criterion does not account for this spatial spillover, and occasionally selects points that are informative locally but redundant given nearby observations.

The second factor is boundary detection. The 2D Exponential function has its steepest gradients near \(x_1 \approx \pm 0.7\) where the exponential decay begins. The GP kernel, with its learned length-scale, provides an implicit model of the function’s smoothness that concentrates posterior variance exactly where the gradient is large. The IQN ensemble, lacking an explicit smoothness model, distributes its disagreement more uniformly, sometimes wasting acquisitions in the flat tails of the function where additional data does little to improve the fit.

The third factor is variance estimation from limited data. With only 100 total points (starting from 10), there is very little data for the neural network to learn from. The GP’s prior encodes smoothness and length-scale, providing strong regularization that effectively fills in the function between observed points. The IQN must learn its implicit smoothness from data alone. In two dimensions the GP kernel has only a few hyperparameters (length-scale, signal variance, noise variance) to estimate, and maximum likelihood estimation of these is reliable even from \(n = 10\) points. As \(d\) grows, the number of kernel hyperparameters increases and MLE becomes less reliable, a setting that increasingly favors the neural network. The 2D Exponential has no discontinuities, ridges, or heteroscedastic noise, exactly the kind of function the squared-exponential kernel was designed for, so the GP’s inductive bias is perfectly matched to the target.

Together, these three factors create a compounding advantage for the GP. The length-scale prior provides effective interpolation between sparse points; the look-ahead acquisition criterion uses this interpolation to plan efficient sampling trajectories; and the low dimensionality ensures that the kernel’s hyperparameters are well-estimated from the available data. Remove any one of these conditions, increase the dimension, add non-smoothness, or expand the budget, and the GP’s advantage shrinks or reverses.

There is an instructive way to visualize the difference between the two methods’ acquisition patterns on this benchmark. If we plot the 90 acquired points in the \((x_1, x_2)\) plane, the DGP+ALC acquisitions cluster near the peak of the function at \((x_1, x_2) \approx (0.7, 0)\) and its mirror image at \((-0.7, 0)\), with a secondary concentration along the ridge where \(f\) transitions from positive to negative values. The GBC-AL acquisitions are more diffuse, spreading across the domain with less concentration near the function’s features. This diffuseness reflects the ensemble disagreement’s lack of look-ahead: it does not “know” that the function has its largest gradients near \((0.7, 0)\), so it distributes points more uniformly. On a smooth function, this uniformity is wasteful, points in the flat tails of the Gaussian envelope contribute little to reducing RMSE. The GP’s ALC criterion, using its length-scale estimate, correctly identifies the peak regions as information-rich and concentrates points there.

The learning curve tells a more nuanced story than the final RMSE alone. Both methods start at similar RMSE after the initial \(n_0 = 10\) points, and both improve steadily as points are acquired. The DGP’s RMSE decreases roughly as \(n^{-1}\) (consistent with its squared-exponential kernel on a smooth function), while GBC-AL’s RMSE decreases more slowly, roughly as \(n^{-0.7}\). The gap opens early, by \(n = 30\) the DGP is already visibly ahead, and remains roughly constant in relative terms through \(n = 100\). There is no crossover: the GP maintains its advantage at every sample size on this benchmark.

It is natural to ask whether the 21% gap could be closed by using a more sophisticated IQN architecture or a better training procedure. We investigated several modifications: increasing the ensemble size from \(K = 3\) to \(K = 10\) (which reduced RMSE by 3% but tripled retraining time), doubling the training epochs to 6,000 (no significant improvement), varying the prior scale over \(\alpha \in [0.1, 2.0]\) (best results at \(\alpha = 0.5\), the default), and using a wider network (512 hidden units instead of 128, which reduced RMSE by 2%). None of these modifications closed the gap by more than 5%, suggesting that the 21% advantage is structural rather than a tuning artifact. The GP’s smoothness prior provides genuine information about this particular function, and no amount of neural network tuning can replicate that information without explicitly encoding it in the architecture.

Could we close the gap by giving the IQN a smoothness prior? In principle, yes: we could add a regularization term that penalizes large gradients of the IQN’s output with respect to its inputs, effectively encouraging smooth predictions. This would be analogous to a squared-exponential kernel prior in the GP framework. However, doing so would sacrifice the IQN’s main advantage, its ability to handle non-smooth functions without modification. The strength of GBC-AL is its generality: the same algorithm works on smooth functions, non-smooth functions, and everything in between. Adding a smoothness prior would improve performance on smooth benchmarks at the cost of degrading performance on non-smooth ones. For the 2D Exponential specifically, this tradeoff might be worthwhile, but for a general-purpose tool it is not.

This benchmark establishes an important baseline: on smooth, low-dimensional problems with small budgets, GP-based active learning has a structural advantage. The 21% gap quantifies what the GP’s prior is worth in this favorable regime. As we will see next, the balance shifts dramatically when the target function violates the GP’s assumptions.

9.5 Rocket Booster (LGBB)

The Langley Glide-Back Booster (LGBB) benchmark (Pamadi et al. 2004; Gramacy 2020; Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon 2023) models the aerodynamics of a reusable NASA rocket booster gliding back to Earth after stage separation. The LGBB concept was developed at NASA Langley Research Center as a fly-back first-stage booster for a two-stage-to-orbit launch system. The simulator computes aerodynamic coefficients using panel methods and wind tunnel data. The three inputs are Mach number (ranging from 0.4 to 4.0, covering subsonic through supersonic regimes), angle of attack (0 to 20 degrees), and side-slip angle (0 to 6 degrees); the response is the side-force coefficient, one of six aerodynamic coefficients produced by the simulator.

What makes LGBB interesting for active learning is the sound barrier. At Mach \(\approx 1\), the aerodynamic regime transitions from subsonic to supersonic, creating a sharp nonlinear ridge in the response surface. This ridge runs through the Mach–angle-of-attack plane and is not aligned with any coordinate axis. A stationary GP with a squared-exponential kernel systematically oversmooths this ridge, because the kernel assigns positive correlation to points on opposite sides of the discontinuity. The side-force coefficient can change by a factor of five over a narrow range of Mach numbers near the sound barrier, while varying smoothly at Mach numbers far from one. This non-stationarity, the function’s behavior varies dramatically across the input space, is precisely the type of structure that GBC handles naturally (see Chapter 8) but that GP kernels struggle with.

The LGBB benchmark has been a standard test case in the computer experiments literature since Pamadi et al. (2004) introduced the simulator. Gramacy (2020) uses it to illustrate the limitations of stationary GP kernels and the advantages of local GP methods. Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon (2023) uses it as one of three active learning benchmarks for deep GPs. Our comparison follows their protocol exactly, providing a controlled evaluation of GBC-AL against the state-of-the-art GP method on the same problem.

Would a non-stationary GP, one with spatially varying length-scales or a treed partition of the input space, close the gap with GBC-AL on this benchmark? Gramacy (2020) demonstrates that local GPs (which fit separate GPs in different regions of the input space) can improve on stationary GPs for the LGBB problem. However, local GPs require specifying or learning the partition, which adds complexity and hyperparameters. The DGP+ALC method used as our comparator is already a non-stationary GP method (the deep GP uses a two-layer architecture that can learn input warping), and it still underperforms GBC-AL by a factor of three. This suggests that the issue is not just stationarity but the GP’s fundamental reliance on kernel-based interpolation, which assigns positive correlation to nearby points even when they lie on opposite sides of a sharp feature.

Following Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon (2023) exactly, we use \(n_0 = 50\) initial LHS points, acquire 250 additional points via ensemble disagreement, and evaluate on a 1,000-point test set after each acquisition. We run 30 independent replicates.

Figure 9.1 shows the learning curves across all 30 replicates. The 90% confidence bands separate completely by \(n \approx 150\), and every GBC replicate outperforms every DGP replicate.

GBC-AL achieves \(2.95\times\) lower RMSE than DGP+ALC. The result is not just a difference in means, the replicate distributions do not overlap. Every single one of the 30 GBC replicates achieves lower RMSE than every single one of the 50 DGP replicates. The 90% confidence bands separate completely by approximately \(n = 150\), and the gap widens as more points are acquired.

The acquisition geography on the LGBB surface reveals why GBC-AL succeeds so decisively. The ensemble disagreement concentrates on three distinct types of regions, and this concentration pattern is the key to the \(2.95\times\) improvement.

First, the ensemble places the majority of its acquisitions near the Mach \(= 1\) ridge. In the Mach–angle-of-attack plane, the sound barrier creates a narrow band where the side-force coefficient changes rapidly. The randomized-prior ensemble members, each learning this ridge at a slightly different location and with a different slope, produce maximum disagreement precisely along the ridge crest. The result is a dense line of acquisitions that traces the boundary between subsonic and supersonic regimes. This is exactly where additional data is most valuable: each point along the ridge tightens the surrogate’s estimate of the ridge location and sharpness, reducing RMSE throughout the surrounding region. The DGP’s ALC criterion, by contrast, distributes acquisitions more uniformly across the domain because its stationary kernel assigns positive correlation across the ridge, blurring the posterior variance signal.

Second, the ensemble directs acquisitions to high-variance regions at large angles of attack, where the side-force coefficient exhibits large magnitude and complex behavior. These regions have fewer initial LHS points (the corners of the input space are undersampled by Latin hypercube designs), so the ensemble members disagree because their prior networks dominate the trainable component. Once a few points are acquired in these regions, the disagreement drops sharply as the trainable components learn the function, and the ensemble redirects its attention elsewhere.

Third, as the budget increases beyond \(n \approx 200\), the ensemble begins filling in the subsonic and supersonic regimes with more evenly spaced points, a pattern that resembles space-filling design applied separately within each regime. By this point, the ridge has been well characterized and the remaining epistemic uncertainty is distributed more uniformly.

This three-phase acquisition pattern, ridge detection, corner filling, uniform refinement, emerges naturally from the ensemble disagreement criterion without any explicit programming. The algorithm does not “know” about the sound barrier; it simply selects the point of maximum disagreement at each iteration. The fact that this greedy criterion produces a sensible sampling strategy tailored to the response surface geometry reflects the quality of the ensemble disagreement signal. The DGP’s ALC criterion, by contrast, produces a more uniform sampling pattern throughout the experiment because its stationary kernel cannot distinguish the ridge from the smooth regions on either side.

Why does GBC win so decisively here? The sound-barrier ridge creates a sharp nonlinearity that the GP’s stationary kernel cannot represent without an unreasonably large number of inducing points, while the IQN, as a universal approximator with no smoothness constraint, learns the ridge geometry naturally. The acquisition signals differ in character as well: the randomized-prior ensemble concentrates disagreement near the ridge, where the function changes rapidly, whereas the GP’s ALC criterion assigns positive correlation across the discontinuity and therefore spreads evaluations more uniformly rather than concentrating them near the sharp feature. Finally, with 300 total points in 3D, the IQN has enough data to learn the function well, so the GP’s prior advantage, which helped on the 2D Exponential at \(n = 100\), is now outweighed by its smoothness bias.

The side-slip angle dimension is also informative. The LGBB problem is three-dimensional, but the non-stationarity is primarily in the Mach–angle-of-attack plane. The side-slip angle modulates the response smoothly without introducing additional discontinuities. The ensemble disagreement criterion correctly identifies this asymmetry: it concentrates most acquisitions in the Mach–angle-of-attack plane (particularly near the ridge) while distributing side-slip acquisitions more uniformly. This adaptive allocation of acquisitions across dimensions is another advantage of the data-driven ensemble disagreement criterion over the GP’s ALC, which treats all dimensions symmetrically through its isotropic (or at best anisotropic with learned length-scales) kernel.

The learning curve on LGBB shows how the two methods interact with the response surface geometry over time. In the initial phase (\(n_0 = 50\) to \(n \approx 100\)), both methods achieve similar RMSE because the initial LHS design provides broad coverage and neither method has yet acquired enough points to differentiate itself. From \(n \approx 100\) to \(n \approx 150\), the GBC-AL curve drops sharply as the ensemble begins concentrating points along the sound-barrier ridge. The DGP curve, by contrast, flattens, the GP is “wasting” acquisitions by distributing them too uniformly across the domain rather than concentrating near the ridge. From \(n \approx 150\) onward, the 90% confidence bands separate completely, and every subsequent acquisition widens the gap. The DGP continues to oversmooth the ridge regardless of how many points it places there, while the IQN’s flexible architecture learns the ridge geometry with increasing fidelity.

The replicate statistics are striking: every one of the 30 GBC-AL replicates achieves lower RMSE than every one of the 50 DGP+ALC replicates. This means the distributions do not just differ in their means, they are completely separated. Even the worst GBC-AL run (RMSE \(\approx 0.0092\)) outperforms the best DGP run (RMSE \(\approx 0.018\)) by a factor of two. This level of separation is unusual in benchmark comparisons, where results often overlap within one or two standard errors. It reflects the fundamental mismatch between the DGP’s stationary kernel and the LGBB’s non-stationary response surface.

The complete separation of replicate distributions is important for statistical inference. In many benchmark comparisons, the conclusion depends on the number of replicates: with 10 replicates, a 10% difference might not be statistically significant, but with 100 replicates it would be. Here, significance is not in question: all 1500 pairwise comparisons favor GBC (Mann-Whitney \(U = 1500\) out of \(1500\), \(p < 10^{-15}\)), corresponding to the extreme value of the Mann-Whitney \(U\)-statistic with a \(p\)-value that is effectively zero regardless of the assumed distribution.

The variability across GBC-AL replicates (standard deviation of RMSE \(\approx 0.0008\)) is also informative. This variability comes from two sources: the random initial LHS design and the stochastic training of the ensemble members. Across 30 replicates, different initial designs lead to different acquisition trajectories, yet the final RMSE is consistent. This suggests that the ensemble disagreement criterion is robust to the choice of initial design: even when the initial points are in suboptimal locations, the acquisition function quickly identifies the important features of the response surface (particularly the sound-barrier ridge) and directs evaluations accordingly.

The LGBB benchmark illustrates the core advantage of GBC for active learning: on non-smooth response surfaces, the IQN’s flexibility translates directly into better surrogate quality, and the ensemble disagreement criterion directs evaluations toward the features that matter most. The \(2.95\times\) advantage is not merely a statistical curiosity, it represents a qualitative difference in how the two methods allocate their acquisition budget, driven by the interaction between the surrogate’s inductive bias and the response surface geometry.

For practitioners working with aerodynamic or fluid dynamics simulators, the LGBB result has immediate implications. Any simulator that involves shock waves, phase transitions, or turbulence onset will have sharp features in its response surface that stationary GP kernels cannot represent efficiently. On such problems, GBC-AL should be the default choice for sequential design, regardless of the input dimension or acquisition budget. The \(2.95\times\) improvement translates directly into a \(2.95\times\) reduction in the number of simulator evaluations needed to achieve a given RMSE target, a substantial saving when each evaluation costs hours of compute time.

9.6 Satellite GRACE

The Satellite GRACE benchmark (Sun et al. 2019; Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon 2023) uses a Test Particle Monte Carlo (TPMC) simulator developed at Los Alamos National Laboratory to compute drag coefficients for satellites in low-Earth orbit. The seven inputs describe thermospheric conditions, atmospheric composition (relative concentrations of atomic oxygen, molecular nitrogen, helium, and other species), velocity, and temperature, encountered by the GRACE satellite during its orbit. The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) mission, launched in 2002, consisted of two identical satellites flying in formation roughly 220 kilometers apart to measure Earth’s gravitational field with unprecedented precision. The gravitational measurements rely on tracking the distance between the two satellites to micrometer accuracy using an onboard microwave ranging system. Accurate knowledge of non-gravitational forces, particularly atmospheric drag, is essential for isolating the gravitational signal from the orbit data, any unmodeled drag force corrupts the gravitational measurement.

The response is the satellite’s drag coefficient, which is needed for orbit determination and collision avoidance. Accurate drag prediction is operationally critical: small errors in the drag coefficient compound over time, leading to position errors of kilometers within days, which matters for satellite constellation management and space debris tracking. The TPMC simulator models the interaction between individual gas molecules and the satellite surface, tracking thousands of test particles as they collide with and reflect off the spacecraft. Each evaluation takes approximately 86 seconds and involves Monte Carlo noise from the random particle trajectories.

Unlike LGBB, the satellite drag surface is smooth and the simulator is stochastic (approximately 86 seconds per evaluation, with Monte Carlo noise). This is a high-dimensional, expensive-to-evaluate problem where the GP’s smoothness prior is well-matched to the true function. Mehta et al. (2014) show that a GP trained on a static 1,000-point Latin hypercube achieves less than 1% RMSPE, establishing the accuracy target; the active learning question is whether sequential design can reach this accuracy with fewer runs.

The GRACE benchmark is particularly important because it represents the typical use case for computer experiment surrogates: a smooth, expensive, moderately high-dimensional simulator where the practitioner wants to build a global surrogate with as few runs as possible. Most real-world engineering simulators fall into this category. The LGBB benchmark, with its sharp sound-barrier ridge, is the exception rather than the rule. Understanding how GBC-AL performs on the GRACE benchmark is therefore more informative for general practice than the LGBB result, impressive as it is.

The stochastic nature of the TPMC simulator adds an additional layer of complexity. Each evaluation produces a noisy estimate of the drag coefficient, with Monte Carlo noise that varies in magnitude across the input space. The GP handles this noise through its nugget parameter, which must be estimated from the data (the replicate pairs in the initial design help with this estimation). The IQN handles noise implicitly through the quantile loss, which adapts to the local noise level without requiring an explicit noise model. In principle, the IQN’s ability to model heteroscedastic noise should give it an advantage on stochastic simulators, but in practice the GRACE simulator’s noise is approximately homoscedastic, so this advantage does not materialize here.

Stochastic simulators also raise the question of replication versus exploration. Should the active learning algorithm evaluate the same input twice to reduce Monte Carlo noise, or should it always evaluate at a new input? The GP framework handles this naturally: the ALC criterion can recommend replication at an existing input if the noise reduction would produce a larger decrease in integrated posterior variance than evaluating at a new input. The GBC framework does not handle replication as naturally, because the ensemble disagreement is driven by inter-model variation at new inputs rather than noise reduction at existing inputs. In our GRACE experiments, we did not allow replication during the active learning phase (all acquisitions were at new inputs), which may have contributed to GBC-AL’s advantage in the early phase: by always exploring new regions, GBC-AL covers the input space faster, even if individual predictions are noisier.

Following Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon (2023), we use \(n_0 = 110\) initial points (including 10 replicate pairs to estimate the noise variance) and acquire 390 additional points (10 independent replicates). The replicate pairs are important for the GP: they provide a direct estimate of the Monte Carlo noise variance \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon\), which the GP needs as an input to its likelihood. The IQN does not need noise variance estimates because the pinball loss adapts to the noise level implicitly through the conditional quantile function.

The satellite results reveal a crossover: GBC-AL leads throughout the early acquisition phase, achieving 16% better RMSPE at \(n = 200\). But after approximately \(n = 300\) points, DGP overtakes GBC, ultimately reaching RMSPE = 0.967 at \(n = 500\) compared to GBC’s 1.192, a 23% advantage for the GP.

The crossover interpretation is central to understanding when GBC-AL should be preferred. Think of the crossover point \(n^*\), here approximately 300, as the budget threshold below which GBC-AL dominates and above which GP-AL takes over. This threshold is determined by three factors that interact with the training set size in different ways.

The first factor is acquisition signal quality. At small \(n\), the ensemble disagreement signal is relatively cheap to compute (no matrix inversions) and updates immediately after each acquisition. The three ensemble members, each with their own frozen prior network, produce robust disagreement estimates even from very limited data because the prior perturbation guarantees diversity regardless of training set size. The GP’s ALC criterion, by contrast, depends on a posterior variance estimate that requires well-estimated kernel hyperparameters. In seven dimensions, the GP kernel has at least seven length-scale parameters, a signal variance, and a noise variance to estimate. With only \(n_0 = 110\) initial points in \(\mathbb{R}^7\), maximum likelihood estimation of these hyperparameters is unreliable, and the resulting ALC criterion may direct acquisitions to suboptimal locations.

The second factor is smoothness prior value. As data accumulate on a smooth surface, the GP’s kernel hyperparameters converge to their optimal values and the posterior variance provides an increasingly accurate map of remaining uncertainty. The GP’s smoothness prior, which was neutral early on, now becomes a powerful inductive bias that interpolates correctly between observed points while the neural network must learn that interpolation from data. On a smooth 7D surface, the GP’s prior is doing real work, and by \(n = 300\) it has enough data to identify the correct length-scales.

The third factor is dimension-data ratio. In seven dimensions, the “curse of dimensionality” means that covering the input space requires substantially more points than in two or three dimensions. The crossover at \(n^* \approx 300\) corresponds to roughly 40 points per input dimension, a regime where the GP’s parametric kernel begins to provide reliable interpolation. Had the problem been smooth in three dimensions (like a hypothetical smooth LGBB), the crossover would have occurred much earlier, perhaps at \(n^* \approx 100\).

These three factors interact multiplicatively. The GP needs enough data both to estimate its kernel hyperparameters (which depends on \(d\)) and to fill in the function between observations (which also depends on \(d\)). The crossover point \(n^*\) thus scales roughly linearly with \(d\): in the benchmarks, we observe \(n^* \approx 40d\) for smooth functions, though this rule of thumb is based on limited evidence and may not hold for \(d > 10\). For the 2D Exponential, \(40d = 80\), which is consistent with the GP’s advantage at \(n = 100\) (already past the crossover). For the Satellite, \(40d = 280\), which is consistent with the observed crossover at \(n^* \approx 300\). The SatMini benchmark at \(n = 60\) is well below this threshold (\(60 / 7 \approx 8.6\) points per dimension), explaining its strong 33% advantage for GBC-AL. The 7D Proxy at \(n = 70\) gives \(70 / 7 = 10\) points per dimension, also well below 40, consistent with its 38% GBC advantage. These data points, while limited, suggest a consistent relationship between the data-to-dimension ratio and the GBC-AL advantage on smooth problems.

Two additional smaller-scale benchmarks confirm these patterns, providing independent validation of the crossover phenomenon on related problems.

SatMini (\(n_0 = 110\), \(+80\) acquisitions, \(n = 190\)) is a reduced-budget version of the GRACE problem, using the same TPMC simulator and 7-dimensional inputs but restricting the acquisition budget to test active learning performance at very small sample sizes where every simulator evaluation (\(\approx 86\) seconds) is costly. At this smaller budget, GBC-AL achieves RMSPE = 5.30 vs. DGP+ALC’s 7.91, a 33% advantage, firmly in the “early phase” where GBC excels. The advantage is even larger than at \(n = 200\) on the full Satellite benchmark (33% vs. 16%), consistent with GBC-AL’s increasing advantage at smaller sample sizes.

The 7D Proxy (\(n_0 = 50\), \(+150\) acquisitions, \(n = 200\)) is a synthetic smooth function in \([0,1]^7\) designed to mimic the smooth, moderate-complexity structure of the satellite drag surface while being cheap to evaluate (enabling larger replicate counts and controlled experimentation). Like the full Satellite problem, the response is a smooth function of seven inputs with no discontinuities, testing whether the IQN ensemble can compete with the GP’s smoothness prior in moderate dimension. GBC-AL achieves RMSE = 0.344 vs. DGP+ALC’s 0.558, a 38% advantage at \(n = 70\). This is the strongest GBC-AL advantage among the smooth-function benchmarks, occurring at the smallest ratio of \(n/d\) (\(70/7 = 10\) points per dimension), further supporting the pattern that GBC-AL’s advantage grows as the data become sparser relative to the input dimension.

Both proxy benchmarks operate in the moderate-\(n\) regime where GBC-AL’s acquisition signal is most effective. The satellite result at \(n = 500\) shows what happens when the GP is given enough data to fully exploit its smoothness prior.

We can examine the crossover more carefully. At \(n = 200\), GBC-AL has acquired 90 points via ensemble disagreement, and these points are distributed across the 7D input space in a pattern that reflects the ensemble’s uncertainty estimates. The GP has also acquired 90 points, but via ALC, which distributes them differently, more uniformly in regions where the GP’s posterior variance is highest. The key question is: what happens between \(n = 200\) and \(n = 300\) that causes the GP to catch up?

The answer is that by \(n \approx 250\), the GP has accumulated enough data to estimate its seven length-scale parameters reliably. Before this point, the length-scale estimates are noisy, and the ALC criterion based on these estimates directs acquisitions to suboptimal locations. A length-scale that is estimated too large causes the GP to treat distant points as correlated, blurring the posterior variance and distributing acquisitions too uniformly. A length-scale that is estimated too small causes the GP to treat nearby points as independent, concentrating acquisitions locally and missing the global structure. Both types of misestimation reduce the effectiveness of ALC relative to the simpler ensemble disagreement criterion.

After this point, the length-scale estimates stabilize, and ALC begins directing acquisitions with the precision that makes GP-based sequential design so effective on smooth surfaces. The IQN, which has no kernel hyperparameters to estimate, does not experience this transition, its acquisition signal quality is roughly constant throughout the experiment. This stability is an advantage in the early phase but becomes a limitation in the late phase, when the GP’s calibrated posterior variance provides a more informative acquisition signal than the ensemble disagreement. GBC-AL has a consistent but unsophisticated acquisition signal, while GP-AL has a noisy signal early on that becomes precise once the kernel hyperparameters are well-estimated.

The crossover phenomenon has a practical implication for budget planning. If a practitioner has a fixed budget of 200 simulator runs on a smooth 7D problem, GBC-AL will produce a better surrogate than GP-AL. If the budget is 500 runs, GP-AL will produce the better surrogate. This means the choice of method should be made before the experiment begins, based on the anticipated budget and the expected properties of the response surface. One cannot simply start with GBC-AL “because it is better early” and switch to GP-AL later, because the design of experiment produced by GBC-AL during the early phase will not be the same as what GP-AL would have produced, and the GP cannot retroactively “undo” the GBC-AL acquisition choices.

An alternative strategy is to use GBC-AL throughout the experiment but apply a GP-based surrogate to the final training set. This decouples the acquisition criterion from the surrogate model, using GBC’s ensemble disagreement to choose which points to evaluate and the GP’s smoothness prior to fit the final model. The decoupled approach has a precedent in the Bayesian optimization literature, where the acquisition function is sometimes computed using one model (e.g., a neural network for scalability) while the final predictions use a different model (e.g., a GP for uncertainty quantification). We have not tested this hybrid approach systematically, but it is a natural direction for future work.

A third strategy is to monitor the crossover in real time. At each iteration, fit both a GBC surrogate and a GP surrogate to the current training set, and compare their leave-one-out prediction errors. When the GP begins outperforming the GBC surrogate on the LOO metric, switch the acquisition criterion from ensemble disagreement to ALC. This adaptive approach would automatically identify the crossover point and select the appropriate method for each phase of the experiment. The challenge is that computing LOO errors for the GP requires \(O(n^3)\) computation at each iteration, which doubles the cost of the experiment. For expensive simulators where the surrogate retraining cost is negligible compared to the simulator cost, this overhead is acceptable.

We note that the crossover phenomenon is specific to smooth response surfaces. On non-smooth surfaces like the LGBB, there is no crossover: GBC-AL leads at every sample size, and the gap grows with \(n\) rather than shrinking. This is because the GP’s smoothness prior is not just neutral on non-smooth surfaces, it is actively harmful, causing the GP to oversmooth the sharp features regardless of how much data it accumulates. The IQN, with no smoothness constraint, learns the sharp features with increasing fidelity as more data become available. The practical implication is simple: if the response surface is known or expected to be non-smooth, use GBC-AL without reservation. The crossover analysis is only relevant for smooth problems.

The crossover also depends on the GP implementation. The DGP+ALC method used here is a non-stationary extension of the standard GP that can, in principle, learn input warping through its two-layer architecture. A simpler stationary GP might cross over later (because it takes longer to learn the function) or might never fully catch up (because its stationarity assumption limits its ultimate accuracy). Conversely, a more sophisticated non-stationary GP, such as one with a treed partition or a locally adaptive kernel, might cross over earlier. Our results are specific to the DGP+ALC comparator and should not be interpreted as a universal comparison of “neural networks vs. Gaussian processes.”

One might wonder whether the results would change with a different GP comparator, for example, a stationary GP with ALC instead of the deep GP, or a GP with a different kernel (Mat'ern 3/2 or 5/2 instead of squared-exponential). The answer is likely yes for specific benchmarks but no for the overall pattern. A simpler stationary GP would perform worse on the LGBB benchmark (where non-stationarity is the key challenge) but might perform comparably on the 2D Exponential and Satellite benchmarks (where the function is smooth). The relative advantage of GBC-AL would therefore increase on non-smooth problems and remain roughly the same on smooth problems if we used a weaker GP baseline.

The benchmarks do not cover several important settings. All three benchmarks have continuous inputs, scalar outputs, and static simulators (the output is a fixed function of the inputs). Many real-world simulators have discrete or mixed inputs, vector or functional outputs, time-varying behavior, or input constraints that restrict the feasible design space. Extending GBC-AL to these settings is straightforward in principle, the IQN can handle any type of input through appropriate encoding (one-hot encoding for discrete variables, mixed continuous-discrete architectures for hybrid inputs), and the ensemble disagreement criterion applies regardless of the output dimension, but the benchmarks in this chapter do not validate these extensions.

Input constraints are particularly common in engineering applications: certain combinations of inputs may be physically impossible (e.g., a rocket cannot simultaneously have zero thrust and maximum drag), infeasible for the simulator (e.g., parameter combinations that cause numerical divergence), or outside the region of interest. The candidate pool \(\mathcal{C}\) can be restricted to the feasible region, and the ensemble disagreement will automatically concentrate acquisitions within this constrained space. No modification to the acquisition function is needed, only the candidate pool changes.

9.7 Batch Acquisition

The active learning loop described in Section 9.3 acquires one point per iteration. In practice, this is often impractical: if the simulator runs on a cluster with 32 cores, we want to submit 32 evaluations simultaneously rather than waiting for each one to finish before selecting the next. This is the batch acquisition problem: select \(B\) points per iteration instead of one.

The naive approach is top-\(B\) disagreement: compute the ensemble disagreement at every candidate point, then select the \(B\) candidates with the highest scores. This is fast and simple but suffers from a well-known failure mode, clustering. When the disagreement surface has a single dominant peak (as it often does near a ridge or boundary), the top-\(B\) candidates all cluster in the same narrow region. Instead of \(B\) informative points spread across the input space, we get \(B\) near-duplicates that provide the same information as a single evaluation. The marginal value of the second through \(B\)-th points in the cluster is negligible.

This clustering problem is not unique to GBC-AL, GP-based batch acquisition faces the same challenge, and the solutions are analogous. The GP literature has developed several approaches: the constant liar heuristic (equivalent to our kriging believer), local penalization (González et al. 2016), and determinantal point processes (DPPs) that enforce diversity through a kernel-based repulsion mechanism. The GBC equivalents are simpler because the ensemble disagreement is a scalar function that can be penalized directly, without the matrix operations required by DPPs.

The solution is to promote diversity within each batch. The core idea is to ensure that the selected points are spread across the input space, covering multiple regions of high disagreement rather than clustering in one. After selecting the first point \(x_1^*\) as the global maximizer of the disagreement, we penalize candidates that are too close to \(x_1^*\) before selecting the second point, and so on. One effective strategy is the kriging believer heuristic adapted to the GBC setting: after selecting \(x_j^*\), we impute a “fantasy” observation \(y_j = \bar{\tilde{y}}(x_j^*)\) (the ensemble mean at the selected point), add it to the training set, and recompute the disagreement. Because the fantasy observation reduces disagreement near \(x_j^*\), the next selected point will be pushed toward a different high-disagreement region. This approach requires \(B\) forward passes through the ensemble but avoids the expensive retraining that a fully sequential acquisition would require.

A simpler alternative is distance-penalized selection: after selecting \(x_j^*\), multiply the disagreement scores by a penalty factor \(\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}(1 - \exp(-\|x - x_i^*\|^2 / 2\ell^2))\), where \(\ell\) is a length-scale parameter controlling the exclusion zone around previously selected points. This is faster than the kriging-believer approach because it requires no imputation, but the choice of \(\ell\) introduces a new hyperparameter.

Why does naive top-\(B\) cluster? The root cause is that the disagreement surface is typically smooth (because each ensemble member’s prediction is a smooth function of \(x\)), so the maximum of the disagreement surface is surrounded by a neighborhood of high disagreement. When \(B\) is small relative to the candidate pool, the top-\(B\) candidates are all drawn from this neighborhood. On the LGBB benchmark, for example, the disagreement surface often has its maximum along the Mach \(= 1\) ridge, and naive top-10 selection would place all 10 points within a narrow band along the ridge, leaving the rest of the domain unsampled. The distance-penalized approach avoids this by creating a “repulsion zone” around each selected point, forcing subsequent selections to explore different regions.

The choice of batch size \(B\) itself involves a tradeoff. Larger \(B\) reduces the number of retraining iterations (which is expensive for GBC, as discussed in Section 9.8) but also reduces the quality of the acquisition signal, because the ensemble disagreement computed at the start of a batch iteration does not account for the information gained from the first \(B - 1\) points in the batch. In the extreme, \(B = n_{\text{total}} - n_0\) reduces active learning to a single round of batch selection from the initial design, essentially a one-shot experimental design problem, which sacrifices the sequential nature of active learning entirely.

In our experiments, we used batch sizes of \(B = 1\) for all benchmarks to match the comparison protocol of Sauer, Gramacy, and Higdon (2023). However, for practitioners deploying GBC-AL on large computing clusters, batch acquisition is essential. We recommend the distance-penalized approach with \(\ell\) set to the median nearest-neighbor distance in the current training set, which adapts naturally as the design fills in.

A practical compromise is \(B \in [5, 20]\) with distance-penalized selection, balancing parallelism against sequential information gain. For very expensive simulators (hours per evaluation), even \(B = 2\) or \(B = 3\) can provide a meaningful speedup by utilizing multiple cores simultaneously, while the information loss from batch acquisition is minimal at such small batch sizes.

The batch acquisition problem also interacts with the retraining schedule. In the sequential (\(B = 1\)) setting, the ensemble is retrained from scratch after each acquisition, which is expensive. In the batch setting, the ensemble is retrained only once per batch of \(B\) acquisitions, reducing the total number of retraining episodes by a factor of \(B\). This means that batch acquisition is not just a parallelism convenience, it also reduces the total computational cost of the active learning loop. For example, with \(B = 10\) and 300 total acquisitions, the number of retraining episodes drops from 300 to 30, reducing total GBC-AL retraining time from roughly 30 hours to 3 hours (plus the parallelized simulator time for \(B\) simultaneous evaluations).

The tradeoff is that the acquisition signal computed at the start of each batch becomes increasingly stale as points are selected within the batch, particularly for the diversity-promoting strategies (kriging believer, distance penalization) that modify the disagreement surface within the batch. The staleness effect is most pronounced when the batch contains a point near a sharp feature (like the LGBB ridge): after evaluating this point, the function value at the ridge is now known, and the disagreement should decrease dramatically in that neighborhood. But the within-batch diversity strategies do not account for this information gain because they do not retrain the ensemble.

A related approach is asynchronous batch acquisition, where new simulator evaluations arrive one at a time and the ensemble is updated (or the acquisition is recomputed) as each result becomes available. This is the natural operating mode for simulators running on shared computing clusters, where evaluations finish at unpredictable times. The asynchronous setting introduces the additional challenge of selecting the next evaluation point while previous evaluations are still running. One approach is to treat the pending evaluations as “fantasy” observations with imputed values (the ensemble mean) and compute the disagreement on the augmented dataset. This is equivalent to the kriging believer approach applied to pending evaluations rather than within-batch selections.

9.8 Computational Cost Analysis

Active learning’s appeal lies in reducing the number of expensive simulator evaluations. But the surrogate retraining cost at each iteration is also a consideration, particularly for methods like GBC that require training neural networks from scratch.

The dominant cost in each GBC-AL iteration is retraining the \(K\) ensemble members. Unlike GPs, where the posterior update after adding one point can be computed in \(O(n^2)\) via rank-one Cholesky updates, the IQN ensemble must be retrained from scratch at each iteration. Why from scratch? The natural alternative, warm-starting from the previous iteration’s weights, fails because of distribution shift. When a new training point is added, the loss landscape changes, and the previous weights may be a local minimum of the old loss but not the new one. More importantly, warm-starting from the previous weights undermines the randomized-prior mechanism: the trainable component \(g_{\phi_k}\) retains “memory” of the old training set, and the diversity injected by the frozen prior \(g_{0,k}\) is partially cancelled by the warm-started trainable component even in regions where the new point provides no information. The result is that warm-started ensembles exhibit collapsed disagreement after a few iterations, losing the epistemic uncertainty signal that drives acquisition. We observed this empirically on the LGBB benchmark: warm-started GBC-AL degraded to performance comparable to random selection by iteration 50.

The wall-clock comparison at \(n = 300\) is rough and reflects both algorithmic and hardware differences (GBC runs on GPU, GP on CPU). The asymptotic difference is what matters: GBC scales as \(O(n)\) per SGD epoch (linear in training set size), while the GP scales as \(O(n^3)\) for a full retrain or \(O(n^2)\) for a rank-one update. For the benchmarks in this chapter (\(n \leq 500\)), the cubic cost is manageable, but at \(n > 5{,}000\) the GP retrain becomes prohibitive while GBC remains feasible.

To put these costs in perspective: for the Satellite GRACE benchmark with 390 acquisition iterations, the total GBC-AL retraining time is approximately \(390 \times 3 \times 2\text{ min} = 39\) hours on a single GPU (or 13 hours with parallelized ensemble training). The total simulator time for 390 evaluations at 86 seconds each is approximately 9.3 hours. So the surrogate retraining cost exceeds the simulator cost by a factor of four. This is unusual, for most computer experiment applications, the simulator cost dominates by orders of magnitude. The satellite benchmark is a moderately expensive simulator; for truly expensive simulators (hours per evaluation), the retraining cost is negligible.

The warm-starting failure deserves further explanation because it is counterintuitive. In standard deep learning, warm-starting (also called transfer learning or fine-tuning) is a widely successful technique: initialize from a pretrained model and continue training on new data. Why does it fail for active learning with randomized priors? The answer lies in the interaction between the trainable and frozen components. After training on \(\mathcal{D}_n\), the trainable component \(g_{\phi_k}\) has learned to predict \(f(x)\) in data-rich regions by effectively “subtracting out” the frozen prior’s contribution: \(g_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) \approx f(x) - \alpha \, g_{0,k}(\tau, x)\) where data are available. When we warm-start from these weights and add a new point \(x_{n+1}\), the trainable component already has a large magnitude in the neighborhood of \(x_{n+1}\) (from fitting the function at nearby points), and this magnitude partially cancels the frozen prior even if \(x_{n+1}\) is in a previously data-sparse region. The ensemble members therefore agree more than they should, and the disagreement signal is suppressed. Retraining from scratch with fresh random initialization restores the proper balance: the trainable component starts small and can only grow where the data support it.

The practical implication is that GBC-AL has higher constant-factor cost (training a neural network from scratch takes thousands of SGD steps) but better asymptotic scaling. For problems where the total acquisition budget is small (\(n < 500\)) and individual simulator evaluations are very expensive (hours per run), the surrogate retraining cost is negligible compared to the simulator cost and the choice should be driven by prediction quality, not computational overhead. For problems with moderate simulator cost (seconds to minutes) and large budgets (\(n > 1{,}000\)), GBC’s linear scaling becomes a decisive advantage.

One practical mitigation strategy is to reduce the number of training epochs as the training set grows. With \(n = 50\) points, 3,000 epochs may be needed for convergence; with \(n = 300\) points, the same level of fit may be achieved in 1,000 epochs because each epoch presents more data. An adaptive epoch schedule that scales inversely with \(\sqrt{n}\) can reduce the total retraining time by a factor of two or more without degrading surrogate quality.

A second mitigation strategy is parallelized ensemble training. The \(K\) ensemble members are trained independently on the same data, making them embarrassingly parallel. On a modern GPU with sufficient memory, all three members can be trained simultaneously as a single batched model, reducing wall-clock time by a factor of \(K\). This is a straightforward implementation optimization that does not affect the acquisition signal quality. In our experiments on a single NVIDIA A100, training three ensemble members in parallel took approximately the same wall-clock time as training a single model, because the GPU had sufficient capacity to handle the combined batch.

The parallelized ensemble can be achieved without creating separate model objects. Instead, one can create a single “fat” network whose hidden layers are \(K\) times wider, with block-diagonal weight matrices that prevent information flow between ensemble members. The forward pass computes all \(K\) predictions simultaneously, and the loss is the sum of the individual pinball losses. This approach has the same computational cost as a single forward pass through a wider network and avoids the overhead of managing \(K\) separate model objects and optimizers. The frozen prior components are handled similarly: the prior network’s weights occupy the same block-diagonal structure and are excluded from the optimizer’s parameter list.

A third consideration is the candidate pool size. Computing the ensemble disagreement at \(n_{\text{cand}}\) candidate points requires \(K \cdot n_{\text{cand}}\) forward passes through the network. For \(n_{\text{cand}} = 10{,}000\) and \(K = 3\), this is fast (milliseconds on a GPU). But for very high-dimensional problems where the candidate pool must be large to adequately cover the input space, the forward-pass cost can become significant. In such cases, a two-stage strategy, evaluate disagreement on a coarse random subset, then refine with a dense grid around the top candidates, can reduce the cost without sacrificing acquisition quality.

9.9 Choosing Between GBC-AL and GP-AL

The benchmarks suggest clear guidelines for practitioners. Favor GBC-AL when the response surface has discontinuities, ridges, or other non-smooth features (as in the Rocket LGBB), when the acquisition budget is moderate relative to the input dimension, the “expensive simulator, limited budget” regime where each new point matters most (SatMini, 7D Proxy, Satellite early phase), or when retraining cost matters, since GBC retrains in \(O(n)\) time via SGD while the GP requires \(O(n^3)\) Cholesky factorizations. Favor GP-AL instead when the response surface is smooth and the kernel assumptions are well-matched to the function (2D Exponential, Satellite late phase), when the total budget is large enough for the GP to identify kernel hyperparameters reliably, or when dimension is low (\(d \leq 4\)) and \(n\) is small (\(n < 200\)), where the GP’s prior provides regularization that the neural network cannot replicate.

The crossover in the satellite benchmark is particularly informative: GBC-AL is not uniformly better or worse than GP-AL. The two methods have complementary strengths, and the choice should be guided by the expected properties of the response surface and the available budget. For expensive simulators where each evaluation costs hours or days, the early-phase advantage of GBC-AL may be decisive, a 16% improvement at \(n = 200\) could save weeks of compute time.

A useful rule of thumb emerges from our experiments: if the practitioner expects the response surface to be smooth and the total acquisition budget will exceed roughly 40 points per input dimension (i.e., \(n > 40d\)), the GP is likely to eventually overtake GBC-AL. Below this threshold, GBC-AL’s faster acquisition signal and lack of smoothness bias make it the safer default. When the response surface is known to have discontinuities or sharp features, GBC-AL is preferred regardless of budget, the \(2.95\times\) advantage on LGBB shows that no amount of additional data will help the GP overcome its smoothness bias on a non-smooth function.

In many practical settings, the practitioner does not know in advance whether the response surface is smooth. An initial exploratory phase can help: run \(n_0\) points via LHS, fit both a GP and an IQN to the initial data, and compare their leave-one-out cross-validation errors. If the GP achieves substantially better LOO error, the response surface is likely smooth in the neighborhood of the data and GP-AL is favored. If the IQN matches or exceeds the GP, the surface may have non-smooth features and GBC-AL is favored. This diagnostic is not foolproof, the initial LHS design may miss a discontinuity that appears only in a specific region of the input space, but it provides a data-driven starting point for the method selection decision.

Domain knowledge is often more informative than any diagnostic. If the simulator models a physical process with known phase transitions, shock waves, or regime changes (as in the LGBB), the response surface is likely non-smooth and GBC-AL should be preferred. If the simulator models a smooth physical process (heat diffusion, elastic deformation, laminar flow), the response surface is likely smooth and the GP’s kernel prior will be well-matched. The practitioner’s understanding of the underlying physics should always inform the choice of surrogate and acquisition criterion.

A final consideration is scalability beyond \(d = 7\). All benchmarks in this chapter have \(d \leq 7\) input dimensions. As \(d\) grows, both methods face the curse of dimensionality: the number of points needed to cover the input space grows exponentially with \(d\). However, the two methods are affected differently. The GP’s cubic cost \(O(n^3)\) becomes prohibitive at large \(n\), limiting the total number of evaluations that can be used even when the simulator is fast. The IQN’s linear cost \(O(n)\) allows much larger training sets, potentially offsetting the curse of dimensionality by sheer volume of data. For \(d > 10\), where standard GP methods become impractical without sparse variational approximations (Titsias 2009; Hensman, Fusi, and Lawrence 2013), GBC-AL may be the only feasible neural-network-based active learning approach. Sparse GPs reduce the cost to \(O(nm^2)\) where \(m\) is the number of inducing points, but they introduce additional approximation error and require careful placement of the inducing points. We have not tested the high-dimensional regime and cannot make definitive claims, but the asymptotic scaling argument strongly favors GBC-AL in high dimensions with large budgets.

Another consideration is the dimensionality of the output. This chapter has focused on scalar-output simulators (\(y \in \mathbb{R}\)), but many simulators produce vector outputs (\(y \in \mathbb{R}^p\)). The LGBB simulator, for instance, produces six aerodynamic coefficients (we used only the side-force coefficient). GP surrogates for vector outputs require either \(p\) independent GPs (ignoring correlations) or a multi-output GP (which adds substantial complexity). The IQN can be extended to vector outputs by training \(p\) separate IQN heads that share a common feature extractor, or by treating each output dimension as a separate active learning problem with shared acquisitions. The ensemble disagreement criterion generalizes naturally: compute the disagreement for each output dimension and combine them (e.g., by summing or taking the maximum). This is an area where GBC-AL has a natural architectural advantage over GP-AL.

We emphasize that the guidelines in this section are derived from six benchmarks in \(d \leq 7\) dimensions with total budgets of \(n \leq 500\). The conclusions may not generalize to very high dimensions (\(d > 20\)), very large budgets (\(n > 5{,}000\)), or response surfaces with qualitatively different structure (e.g., periodic functions, highly anisotropic surfaces, or functions with many local optima). As the GBC framework matures and more benchmarks are published, these guidelines will need to be updated. For now, they represent our best understanding of the GBC-AL/GP-AL tradeoff based on the available evidence.

There is also the question of robustness to misspecification. If the practitioner believes the function is smooth and chooses GP-AL, but the function turns out to have a sharp feature in an unexplored region, the GP will oversmooth this feature and the ALC criterion will not direct acquisitions toward it (because the GP’s posterior variance does not account for model misspecification). GBC-AL is more robust in this sense: because the IQN has no smoothness assumption, it cannot be “misspecified” in the same way, and the ensemble disagreement will naturally increase near any feature that the ensemble members learn at different rates. This robustness argument favors GBC-AL as a default choice when the response surface is unknown, even if the total budget exceeds the \(40d\) threshold.

The robustness advantage is particularly relevant for exploratory studies, where the practitioner is building a surrogate for a simulator that has not been extensively studied. In this setting, the response surface may contain unexpected features, regime transitions, boundary layers, or resonances, that are not anticipated by the practitioner’s prior knowledge. GP-AL with a misspecified kernel will systematically avoid these features because the posterior variance does not signal their presence. GBC-AL, through the ensemble disagreement, will naturally detect these features when different ensemble members learn them at different rates, directing acquisitions toward them even without prior knowledge of their existence. This “model-free discovery” capability is a strong practical argument for GBC-AL in scientific computing applications.

9.10 Open Problems

Several fundamental questions about GBC-based active learning remain unresolved.

Regret bounds. There is currently no formal optimality theory for IQN-based acquisition functions analogous to the IMSE/ALC theory for GPs (Gramacy 2020; Sacks et al. 1989). How fast should GBC-AL converge theoretically? The difficulty is that the ensemble disagreement is a frequentist quantity (standard deviation of point estimates across models) rather than a Bayesian posterior quantity, so the information-theoretic tools used to analyze ALC do not directly apply. For GPs, the IMSE under ALC is known to converge at the rate \(O((\log n / n)^{2\nu/d})\) for a Mat'ern-\(\nu\) kernel in \(d\) dimensions, matching the optimal rate for nonparametric regression in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. Establishing convergence rates for ensemble-disagreement acquisition under neural network surrogates, even in the simplest fixed-design setting, would connect GBC-AL to the broader literature on sequential experimental design. A natural conjecture is that the regret scales as \(O(n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)})\) for \(\beta\)-smooth functions, matching the minimax rate for nonparametric regression, but no proof exists. The main obstacle is that the ensemble disagreement depends on the training algorithm (SGD), the network architecture, and the prior scale in ways that are difficult to analyze theoretically.

Adaptive prior scale. The prior scale \(\alpha\) in Eq. 9.3 is fixed throughout the active learning loop. Should it adapt? At the start of the loop, when \(n\) is small and the trainable component is poorly trained, a large \(\alpha\) ensures that the ensemble members are diverse and the acquisition signal is informative. As \(n\) grows and the trainable component improves, a smaller \(\alpha\) would allow the members to agree more quickly in data-rich regions, sharpening the acquisition signal. We experimented with linear decay schedules for \(\alpha\) but found no consistent improvement over a fixed \(\alpha = 0.5\). A principled schedule, perhaps tied to the ensemble’s training loss or an estimate of the remaining epistemic uncertainty, remains an open question. One could draw an analogy with the “temperature” parameter in simulated annealing or the exploration parameter in \(\varepsilon\)-greedy reinforcement learning: start with high exploration (large \(\alpha\)) and gradually reduce it as the surrogate improves. But unlike those settings, where the exploration-exploitation tradeoff is well-understood theoretically, there is no formal theory guiding the decay of \(\alpha\) in the active learning context.

Thompson sampling connection. In the reinforcement learning literature, randomized-prior ensembles were introduced as a practical approximation to Thompson sampling (Thompson 1933; Osband, Aslanides, and Cassirer 2018). Thompson sampling selects actions by sampling from the posterior and acting greedily with respect to the sample. The randomized-prior ensemble approximates this by treating each member as a “posterior sample” and selecting the action that is optimal under a randomly chosen member. Can this connection be made formal in the active learning setting? Specifically, instead of selecting the point of maximum disagreement, one could select the point that a randomly chosen ensemble member judges most uncertain. This would implement approximate Thompson sampling for sequential design. Whether this exploration strategy offers advantages over the deterministic maximum-disagreement criterion is unexplored.

Multi-fidelity active learning. Many engineering simulators can be run at different fidelity levels, a coarse mesh that runs in seconds and a fine mesh that runs in hours. GP-based multi-fidelity methods use auto-regressive kernels to model the correlation between fidelities (Kennedy and O’Hagan 2000). Extending GBC-AL to the multi-fidelity setting would require an ensemble that captures uncertainty across fidelity levels, directing cheap low-fidelity evaluations to regions of broad uncertainty and expensive high-fidelity evaluations to regions where the fidelity gap matters most. The IQN’s architecture can naturally accommodate a fidelity indicator as an additional input, but designing an acquisition criterion that trades off evaluation cost against information gain remains open.

Calibration of the disagreement signal. The ensemble disagreement is an ordinal uncertainty measure: we can rank candidate points by their disagreement, but we cannot interpret the disagreement value as a probability or a predicted error magnitude. Can the disagreement be calibrated so that it provides meaningful uncertainty quantification in addition to acquisition guidance? One approach is to use conformal prediction (Chapter 14) on the disagreement values themselves, computing empirical coverage rates on held-out data and adjusting the disagreement scale accordingly. Another approach is to train a separate calibration model that maps disagreement values to predicted RMSE, using past active learning iterations as training data. Neither approach has been tested systematically.

Stopping criteria. When should active learning stop? With a fixed budget, the answer is predetermined. But for applications where simulator time is not strictly rationed, a natural stopping criterion would be “stop when the surrogate is good enough.” The ensemble disagreement provides a candidate metric: stop when the maximum disagreement over the candidate pool drops below a threshold. The challenge is choosing the threshold, which depends on the scale of the response and the desired accuracy. An alternative is to monitor the improvement in RMSE (estimated by leave-one-out cross-validation) and stop when the improvement per acquisition drops below a threshold. This “diminishing returns” criterion is more interpretable but requires computing LOO predictions at each iteration, which adds computational cost.

9.11 Summary

This chapter has developed a complete framework for active learning with GBC surrogates. The components are: (1) a randomized-prior ensemble that injects structural diversity through frozen random networks, producing an epistemic uncertainty signal that persists regardless of training set size; (2) an ensemble disagreement acquisition function that selects points where the ensemble members disagree most, directing simulator evaluations toward regions of high uncertainty; and (3) a retraining-from-scratch protocol that maintains the integrity of the prior perturbation across active learning iterations.

The benchmark results reveal a clear division of labor between GBC-AL and GP-AL. GBC-AL excels when the response surface has non-smooth features (LGBB, \(2.95\times\) improvement), when the acquisition budget is moderate relative to the input dimension (SatMini and 7D Proxy, 33–38% improvement), or when the GP’s kernel hyperparameters cannot be reliably estimated from the available data. GP-AL excels on smooth surfaces with large budgets (Satellite at \(n = 500\), 23% improvement) or low dimensions with small budgets (2D Exponential, 21% improvement). The crossover phenomenon observed on the Satellite benchmark is the most practically informative result: it demonstrates that the choice between GBC-AL and GP-AL depends not just on the response surface properties but on the interaction between those properties and the available budget.

For practitioners, we offer a concrete rule of thumb: use GBC-AL as the default for non-smooth problems at any budget, and for smooth problems when \(n < 40d\). Switch to GP-AL on smooth problems when \(n > 40d\) and the kernel is well-specified. When in doubt, the initial LOO diagnostic described in Section 9.9 provides a data-driven method selection criterion.

The active learning framework developed in this chapter extends to several downstream applications. In Chapter 10, we will see how the IQN can be used for Bayesian posterior inference rather than surrogate modeling, and the ensemble disagreement criterion can be adapted to direct data collection toward regions of the parameter space where the posterior is most uncertain. In Chapter 13, the causal inference framework builds on the surrogate modeling ideas from this chapter, and active data collection for causal effect estimation is a natural application of the GBC-AL methodology. The insight that randomized-prior ensembles provide a practical and effective epistemic uncertainty signal for neural-network-based surrogates carries over to all of these downstream applications.

The neural network’s lack of a built-in uncertainty model is not a fatal flaw, it can be remedied through ensemble methods that inject and measure structural diversity. The randomized-prior approach is one specific instantiation of this idea, but the principle is general: any mechanism that produces diverse predictions in data-sparse regions and convergent predictions in data-rich regions can serve as an epistemic uncertainty signal for active learning. Future work may discover better mechanisms than frozen random priors, but the framework, ensemble diversity as a proxy for epistemic uncertainty, disagreement as an acquisition function, will remain applicable.

The practical takeaway is simple. Take your existing IQN code, duplicate it \(K = 3\) times, add a frozen random network to each copy, train all three on the same data, compute the standard deviation of their predictions at each candidate point, and select the candidate with the highest standard deviation. Retrain from scratch after each acquisition. That is the entire algorithm. The complexity is in understanding why it works (which we have discussed at length in this chapter) and when it works (which the benchmarks have mapped out), not in the implementation itself.

The exercises that follow are designed to help the reader build intuition about the key components of GBC-AL: the randomized-prior mechanism, the ensemble disagreement criterion, the effect of hyperparameters like \(\alpha\) and \(K\), and the practical challenges of batch acquisition and warm-starting. We recommend starting with Exercise 9.1, which implements the full active learning loop in one dimension, before attempting the higher-dimensional exercises.

We close with a remark on the relationship between this chapter and the broader computer experiments literature. Sequential design has been studied for over three decades, beginning with the pioneering work of Sacks et al. (1989) and Cohn (1994). The GP has been the dominant surrogate model throughout this period, and the ALC/IMSPE acquisition criteria have been refined through extensive theoretical and empirical work (Gramacy 2020). GBC-AL is a newcomer to this field, and its theoretical foundations are less developed than those of GP-based methods. What the benchmarks in this chapter demonstrate is that GBC-AL is a competitive alternative to GP-based active learning, with distinct advantages on non-smooth problems and at moderate sample sizes. Whether these advantages extend to the broader class of problems encountered in scientific computing remains to be seen, and we encourage practitioners to test both approaches on their specific applications.

# Sequential Design and Active Learning {#sec-active-learning}

In the previous chapters, we built GBC surrogates for fixed datasets: a training set was given, and we trained an IQN to approximate the conditional quantile function. But in many scientific and engineering applications, the training data are not given, they are *chosen*. A computer simulator takes inputs $x$ and returns outputs $y = f(x)$, possibly with stochastic noise, and each evaluation costs minutes, hours, or days of compute time. The question is not just "how do I fit a surrogate?" but "where should I run the simulator next to learn the most?"

This is the *sequential design* problem, also known as *active learning* in the machine learning literature. Starting from a small initial design of $n_0$ points, we iteratively select new inputs, evaluate the simulator, retrain the surrogate, and repeat until the budget is exhausted. The challenge is the *acquisition function*: a criterion that tells us which input $x_{n+1}$ to evaluate next. A good acquisition function directs simulator evaluations toward regions of high uncertainty, reducing global prediction error faster than random sampling.

Gaussian processes have dominated sequential design for decades, largely because the GP posterior variance provides a natural, closed-form uncertainty estimate that feeds directly into acquisition criteria like Active Learning Cohn [ALC; @cohn1994neural] and expected improvement [EI; @jones1998efficient]. The GBC framework, built on neural networks rather than GPs, does not have a posterior variance in the Bayesian sense. So how do we do active learning with GBC?

The answer is *randomized-prior ensembles* [@osband2018randomized]. We train $K$ IQN models, each augmented with a frozen random prior network whose weights are drawn once and never updated. In data-sparse regions, the ensemble members disagree because their prior networks pull them in different directions; in data-rich regions, the trained components dominate and the members converge. This disagreement is the acquisition signal: we sample where the ensemble disagrees most.

This chapter develops GBC-based active learning from first principles. We introduce the sequential design framework (@sec-al-problem), describe the randomized-prior ensemble and why it outperforms alternatives (@sec-al-randprior), define the acquisition function (@sec-al-acquisition), and then evaluate on three benchmarks of increasing complexity: a smooth 2D function where GBC loses to GP-based methods (@sec-al-2d), a rocket aerodynamics problem where GBC wins by a factor of three (@sec-al-rocket), and a satellite drag application that reveals a nuanced crossover between the two approaches (@sec-al-satellite). We then address two practical considerations that arise in real deployments: acquiring multiple points per iteration (@sec-al-batch) and the computational cost of retraining at each step (@sec-al-cost). We close with practical guidance on choosing between GBC-AL and GP-AL (@sec-al-guidance).

The chapter connects to the rest of the book as follows. The IQN surrogate from @sec-iqn provides the predictive engine; the ensemble technique extends the single-model IQN to produce epistemic uncertainty estimates. The boundary augmentation from @sec-jumps can be combined with active learning when the response surface has jump discontinuities, though we do not demonstrate this combination here. The conformal calibration from @sec-calibration can be applied post-hoc to the GBC-AL surrogate to provide formal coverage guarantees on the actively learned model.

The active learning problem also connects to broader themes in the GBC framework. The noise outsourcing theorem (@sec-noise-outsourcing) guarantees that any conditional distribution can be represented as a deterministic function of the input and a uniform random variable. In the active learning context, this means that the IQN surrogate can, in principle, learn the full conditional distribution $Y \mid X = x$, not just the mean, as more data are acquired. The ensemble disagreement criterion directs acquisitions toward regions where this conditional distribution is poorly determined, and the pinball loss ensures that the acquired data are used to improve the quantile function across the entire distribution, not just the conditional mean.

In @sec-bayesian-inference, we will use the IQN to approximate posterior distributions $\theta \mid y$ by training on simulated prior-predictive pairs $(\theta, y)$. The active learning question in that context becomes: which prior-predictive pairs should we simulate to learn the posterior most efficiently? The ensemble disagreement criterion provides a natural answer: simulate parameters in regions where the posterior quantile function is most uncertain, as measured by ensemble disagreement. This is *active Bayesian computation*, using sequential design principles to make Bayesian inference more sample-efficient. The connection is concrete: the same `RandomPriorNet` code, the same `ensemble_disagreement` function, and the same `select_next` function can be applied to the Bayesian inference problem by replacing the simulator output $y = f(x)$ with the prior-predictive pair $(\theta, y) \sim p(\theta) p(y \mid \theta)$.


## The Sequential Design Problem {#sec-al-problem}

Consider a simulator $f : \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R}$ that maps a $d$-dimensional input $x \in \mathcal{X} \subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ to a scalar output $y = f(x) + \varepsilon$, where $\varepsilon$ represents either stochastic noise (for Monte Carlo simulators) or is zero (for deterministic codes). Each evaluation of $f$ is expensive, perhaps a finite-element solve, a particle simulation, or a climate model run. The goal is to build a surrogate $\hat{f}$ that approximates $f$ over $\mathcal{X}$ using as few evaluations as possible.

The sequential design procedure works as follows:

1. *Initialize.* Select $n_0$ initial design points $\{x_1, \ldots, x_{n_0}\}$, typically via Latin hypercube sampling (LHS), and evaluate the simulator to obtain $\mathcal{D}_0 = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n_0}$.

2. *Fit surrogate.* Train a surrogate model on $\mathcal{D}_n$.

3. *Acquire.* Evaluate an acquisition function $a(x)$ over a candidate set $\mathcal{C} \subset \mathcal{X}$ and select
$$
x_{n+1}^* = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{C}} \; a(x).
$$ {#eq-al-acquire}

4. *Evaluate.* Run the simulator at $x_{n+1}^*$ to obtain $y_{n+1}$, and update $\mathcal{D}_{n+1} = \mathcal{D}_n \cup \{(x_{n+1}^*, y_{n+1})\}$.

5. *Repeat* steps 2--4 until the budget is exhausted.

The entire procedure hinges on the acquisition function $a(x)$. Random sampling, choosing $x_{n+1}$ uniformly from $\mathcal{C}$, ignores the surrogate's predictions entirely and is the baseline. A good acquisition function directs evaluations toward regions where the surrogate is uncertain, achieving lower prediction error for the same budget.

Several design choices in this procedure deserve comment. The initial design $\mathcal{D}_0$ is typically a Latin hypercube sample (LHS), which provides better space-filling coverage than a simple random sample of the same size [@mckay1979comparison]. The size $n_0$ should be large enough to provide at least minimal coverage of the input space, a common rule of thumb is $n_0 = 10d$ where $d$ is the input dimension, but small enough to leave most of the budget for sequential acquisition, where the active learning criterion can direct evaluations more efficiently than the initial space-filling design. For the benchmarks in this chapter, we use $n_0 = 10$ for $d = 2$, $n_0 = 50$ for $d = 3$, and $n_0 = 110$ for $d = 7$, following the protocols of @sauer2023active.

The candidate set $\mathcal{C}$ is typically a large random or quasi-random sample over $\mathcal{X}$ (e.g., 10,000 Sobol points), and the acquisition function is evaluated exhaustively over $\mathcal{C}$ rather than optimized continuously. This discretization introduces a small approximation error but avoids the difficulty of optimizing the acquisition function over a continuous domain, which can be non-convex and multimodal. The size of $\mathcal{C}$ matters: too small and the best candidate may miss important regions; too large and the evaluation cost becomes prohibitive. In our experiments, $|\mathcal{C}| = 10{,}000$ provides a good balance for $d \leq 7$. For higher dimensions, larger candidate sets may be needed, though the evaluation cost remains modest because the ensemble forward pass is fast on GPUs.

The stopping criterion is typically a fixed budget: "evaluate the simulator $n_{\text{total}}$ times." More sophisticated stopping criteria are possible, for example, stop when the ensemble disagreement drops below a threshold, or when the RMSE on a held-out validation set stabilizes, but fixed budgets are the standard in the computer experiments literature and are what we use here.

The candidate pool $\mathcal{C}$ can also be refreshed during the active learning loop. Starting with a fixed candidate pool and removing selected points (as in the `active_learning_loop` code above) gradually depletes the pool, potentially leaving gaps in regions where the remaining candidates are sparse. An alternative is to regenerate the candidate pool at each iteration by sampling a fresh set of quasi-random points. This ensures that the pool always covers the input space uniformly, at the cost of slightly increased computational overhead for generating new Sobol sequences at each iteration. For the benchmarks in this chapter, the fixed pool is sufficient because the initial pool (10,000 points) is much larger than the number of acquisitions (at most 390), so depletion is not a concern. For problems with very large acquisition budgets ($n > 5{,}000$), refreshing the candidate pool is recommended to avoid gaps in coverage.

A more sophisticated approach is *gradient-based acquisition optimization*: instead of evaluating the disagreement on a discrete candidate pool, compute the gradient of the disagreement with respect to the input $x$ and use gradient ascent to find the continuous maximizer. This requires differentiating through the ensemble's forward passes, which is straightforward in PyTorch using `torch.autograd`. The advantage is that gradient-based optimization can find sharp peaks in the disagreement surface that might fall between candidate pool points, potentially identifying features that a coarse candidate pool would miss. The disadvantage is that the optimization landscape is typically non-convex, requiring multiple random restarts to avoid local maxima. In our experiments, we found that 50 random restarts with L-BFGS optimization produced essentially the same acquisition points as a 10,000-point candidate pool, but took longer to compute. In practice, the discrete candidate pool approach is simpler, faster, and produces nearly identical results because the disagreement surface is smooth enough that a 10,000-point candidate pool captures its features adequately. We recommend the gradient-based approach only for very high-dimensional problems ($d > 20$) where a candidate pool of adequate density would be prohibitively large.

The Gaussian process provides the canonical acquisition function for surrogate-based sequential design. Because the GP posterior is a Gaussian distribution at every point, the posterior variance $\sigma^2_n(x) = \text{Var}[f(x) \mid \mathcal{D}_n]$ is available in closed form. The *Active Learning Cohn* (ALC) criterion [@cohn1994neural] selects the point that maximally reduces the integrated posterior variance:
$$
x_{n+1}^{\text{ALC}} = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{C}} \int_{\mathcal{X}} \bigl[\sigma^2_n(x') - \sigma^2_{n+1}(x')\bigr] \, dx',
$$ {#eq-alc}
where $\sigma^2_{n+1}(x')$ is the posterior variance that *would* result from adding $x$ to the training set (regardless of the observed $y$, since GP variance depends only on input locations). The integral is typically approximated over a reference grid.

ALC is model-based (uses the GP posterior), look-ahead (considers the effect of adding a point), and global (integrates over the entire input space). Its effectiveness has been demonstrated across many computer experiments benchmarks [@gramacy2020surrogates; @sauer2023active].

Other GP-based acquisition functions share these properties. *Expected improvement* (EI) [@jones1998efficient], originally developed for optimization rather than global approximation, selects the point where the expected improvement over the current best observation is maximized; it is the standard criterion for Bayesian optimization. *Integrated mean squared prediction error* (IMSPE) is closely related to ALC but integrates the posterior mean squared error rather than the variance reduction. For global surrogate building (as opposed to optimization), ALC and IMSPE are the most common choices.

Active learning for *surrogate building* (our focus here) differs from active learning for *optimization* (Bayesian optimization). Surrogate building aims to learn the function everywhere: a global approximation with low RMSE over the entire input domain. Optimization aims to find the input that maximizes (or minimizes) the function, without caring about the function's value at other points. EI is designed for the optimization setting: it concentrates evaluations near the current best observation and in high-uncertainty regions that might contain a better optimum. ALC is designed for the surrogate setting: it distributes evaluations to reduce global prediction uncertainty. The ensemble disagreement criterion is closer in spirit to ALC than to EI, because it measures global uncertainty rather than local improvement potential. We use DGP+ALC as the GP comparator throughout this chapter because it represents the state of the art for GP-based sequential design for computer experiments [@sauer2023active].

The challenge for GBC is that neural networks do not have a posterior variance. An IQN trained on $\mathcal{D}_n$ produces a point prediction and predictive quantiles, but these reflect *aleatoric* uncertainty (noise in $y \mid x$), not *epistemic* uncertainty (uncertainty about the function $f$ due to limited data). A well-trained IQN in a data-sparse region may produce narrow, confident intervals, not because the function is well-determined, but because the network has no mechanism to signal "I have not seen data here."

This distinction is the central obstacle for neural-network-based active learning, and it is what the randomized-prior ensemble is designed to overcome.

To make the distinction concrete, consider an IQN trained on 20 data points in $[0,1]^2$, with all points concentrated in $[0, 0.5]^2$. The IQN's predicted 90% interval at $x = (0.3, 0.3)$ (a data-rich region) might be $[1.2, 1.8]$, and at $x = (0.9, 0.9)$ (a data-free region) it might also be $[1.2, 1.8]$. Both intervals have the same width, yet they mean entirely different things. The first interval reflects genuine noise in the data; the second reflects the network's extrapolation, which has no basis in observation. A GP would assign a wide posterior variance at $(0.9, 0.9)$ because it is far from training points, and a narrow variance at $(0.3, 0.3)$ where data constrain the posterior. The IQN makes no such distinction because its intervals are driven by the loss function, which only sees the training data.

This is not a bug in the IQN, it is a fundamental consequence of the neural network's parametric structure. The IQN is trained to minimize the pinball loss over the *training distribution*, which does not cover $(0.9, 0.9)$. The network's behavior at $(0.9, 0.9)$ is determined by its inductive bias (the architecture, activation functions, and implicit regularization of SGD) rather than by any observed data. Different random seeds will produce different IQN models that agree near $(0.3, 0.3)$ (where the data constrain the predictions) but disagree near $(0.9, 0.9)$ (where the predictions are unconstrained). This observation is the foundation for the ensemble-based approach to epistemic uncertainty that we develop in the next section.

The aleatoric/epistemic distinction has practical consequences beyond acquisition function design.
When reporting the uncertainty of a GBC surrogate's predictions, it is important to distinguish between the two types.
The IQN's quantile estimates (e.g., the 90% prediction interval) reflect aleatoric uncertainty and should be reported as such.
The ensemble disagreement reflects epistemic uncertainty and should be reported separately.
Conflating the two, for example, by treating the ensemble spread as a prediction interval, can lead to miscalibrated uncertainty estimates.


## Randomized-Prior Ensembles {#sec-al-randprior}

The idea of @osband2018randomized, originally developed for exploration in reinforcement learning, is to augment each ensemble member with a *frozen random prior network* that injects structural diversity. Each member $k$ computes
$$
G_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) = g_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) + \alpha \, g_{0,k}(\tau, x),
$$ {#eq-randprior}
where $g_{\phi_k}$ is a standard trainable IQN, $g_{0,k}$ is a frozen network whose weights are drawn from an initialization distribution (e.g., Xavier uniform) at the start of training and never updated, and $\alpha > 0$ is a prior scale parameter. In our experiments, we use $\alpha = 0.5$ and $K = 3$ ensemble members.

::: {.callout-tip appearance="simple" title="Definition 9.1: Randomized-Prior IQN Ensemble"}
A *randomized-prior IQN ensemble* of size $K$ consists of $K$ IQN models, each with output
$$
G_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) = g_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) + \alpha \, g_{0,k}(\tau, x), \quad k = 1, \ldots, K,
$$
where $g_{\phi_k}$ is trainable, $g_{0,k}$ has frozen random weights, and $\alpha > 0$ controls the prior strength. All $K$ members are trained independently on the same data $\mathcal{D}_n$ with different random seeds.
:::

The prior scale $\alpha$ controls the strength of the perturbation. When $\alpha$ is too small, the frozen network's contribution is negligible and the ensemble collapses to a standard ensemble, all members converge to the same predictions everywhere, regardless of data density, and the disagreement signal vanishes. When $\alpha$ is too large, the frozen network dominates even in data-rich regions, preventing the trainable component from fitting the data and producing high disagreement everywhere, the acquisition signal becomes uninformative because it no longer distinguishes data-rich from data-sparse regions. The value $\alpha = 0.5$ is a compromise that works well across the benchmarks in this chapter.

The choice of $\alpha$ is analogous to the choice of kernel variance $\sigma_f^2$ in a GP: it sets the scale of the prior's contribution to the prediction. In a GP, $\sigma_f^2$ is typically estimated from the data via maximum marginal likelihood. For the randomized-prior ensemble, there is no analogous marginal likelihood to optimize, so $\alpha$ must be set by the user or tuned via cross-validation. In practice, values in the range $[0.1, 1.0]$ work well for most problems; the sensitivity to $\alpha$ within this range is modest, as we will see in @exr-al-prior-scale.

The choice of $K = 3$ ensemble members is also a compromise: more members would provide a more stable estimate of disagreement, but each additional member multiplies the retraining cost proportionally. In our experiments, the improvement from $K = 3$ to $K = 5$ was typically less than 5%, while the retraining cost increased by 67%. With $K = 2$ members, the disagreement reduces to the absolute difference between two predictions, which is a noisy estimator of the standard deviation. We found $K = 2$ to be adequate for exploratory runs but too noisy for definitive benchmarks. The choice $K = 3$ provides sufficient stability for the acquisition function while keeping the computational cost manageable. We recommend starting with $K = 3$ for exploratory experiments and increasing to $K = 5$ only if the acquisition function appears noisy (i.e., if the selected points change substantially across independent runs with different random seeds).

Why does the randomized-prior mechanism work? The intuition is rooted in the contrast between GP posteriors and neural network ensembles.

*GP posterior samples* are draws from a stochastic process whose diversity is guaranteed by the posterior covariance. Even as $n \to \infty$, the posterior samples remain distinct wherever posterior uncertainty is nonzero. The GP prior encodes a belief about function smoothness (via the kernel), and the posterior shrinks this belief toward the data. In regions far from training points, the posterior is wide, and samples are diverse.

*Standard NN ensembles*, multiple networks trained with different random seeds, behave very differently. As $n$ grows, SGD drives all members toward the same empirical risk minimizer. With enough data, the members converge to nearly identical predictions everywhere, including regions far from the training data. The ensemble "collapses" and disagreement vanishes, even where epistemic uncertainty should be high. This collapse has been documented extensively in the deep learning literature [@lakshminarayanan2017simple; @fort2019deep]: while deep ensembles provide useful uncertainty estimates on classification tasks where the function is complex enough that different random initializations explore different modes of the loss landscape, they are unreliable for regression tasks with smooth functions and moderate $n$, which is precisely the setting of computer experiments.

*Bootstrap ensembles* partially restore diversity by training each member on a resampled version of the data. The idea is sound in principle: each member sees a different version of the data, so they learn slightly different functions, and their disagreement reflects the sensitivity of the predictions to the specific training points. This is the basis of bagging [@breiman1996bagging], which is highly effective for decision trees and random forests. But at small $n$, exactly the regime where active learning operates, bootstrap resampling produces degenerate training sets (some points duplicated, others missing entirely), leading to poorly trained individual members. Neural networks are much more sensitive to training set perturbations than decision trees, because the loss landscape is highly non-convex and a degenerate training set can cause the optimizer to converge to a qualitatively different local minimum. In our experiments, bootstrap ensembles were consistently the *worst* acquisition strategy: 17% worse than random selection on a 7D benchmark and 30% worse on a satellite drag problem.

*Randomized priors* solve the collapse problem by injecting *structural* diversity that persists regardless of $n$. In data-sparse regions, the trainable component $g_{\phi_k}$ has not seen enough data to learn the function and therefore cannot cancel the random contribution $\alpha \, g_{0,k}$; different members make different predictions because their prior networks differ, so disagreement is high, signaling epistemic uncertainty. In data-rich regions, the trainable component has converged to the true function and its magnitude dominates the prior perturbation; all members agree and disagreement is low, correctly indicating that the function is well-determined.

There is a useful analogy with regularization in classical statistics. Ridge regression adds a penalty $\lambda \|\beta\|_2^2$ to the least-squares loss, shrinking the coefficient vector toward zero. The penalty acts as a prior: it encodes the belief that the coefficients should be small unless the data provide strong evidence otherwise. The randomized prior plays a similar role: it encodes the belief that the function should look like the frozen random network unless the data provide strong evidence otherwise. The key difference is that the prior is different for each ensemble member, so the "default" prediction varies across members. In regions where the data are informative, all members are pulled toward the same fit (just as ridge regression with strong data approaches OLS). In regions where the data are uninformative, each member stays near its own random prior, creating disagreement.

This analogy can be pushed further. In Bayesian linear regression, the posterior variance is $\Sigma_{\text{post}} = (\Sigma_{\text{prior}}^{-1} + X^\top X / \sigma^2)^{-1}$. Where $X^\top X$ is large (data-rich), the posterior variance is small regardless of the prior. Where $X^\top X$ is zero (data-free), the posterior variance equals the prior variance. The randomized-prior ensemble exhibits exactly this behavior: where data are abundant, the trainable component dominates and the prior's contribution is negligible; where data are absent, the prior network determines the prediction and different members disagree. The ensemble disagreement is thus a nonparametric, distribution-free analog of the Bayesian posterior variance, obtained without specifying a likelihood, a prior distribution, or a posterior inference algorithm.

To see this mathematically, consider two ensemble members $k$ and $k'$ at a point $x$ where no training data are available. Their predictions are
$$
G_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) = g_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) + \alpha \, g_{0,k}(\tau, x), \quad
G_{\phi_{k'}}(\tau, x) = g_{\phi_{k'}}(\tau, x) + \alpha \, g_{0,k'}(\tau, x).
$$
Without data near $x$, the trainable components $g_{\phi_k}$ and $g_{\phi_{k'}}$ are determined by the loss at distant training points and are roughly equal (both extrapolate similarly). The disagreement is therefore driven by the frozen prior difference: $G_{\phi_k} - G_{\phi_{k'}} \approx \alpha(g_{0,k} - g_{0,k'})$, which is nonzero because $g_{0,k}$ and $g_{0,k'}$ have different random weights. At a point $x'$ where training data are abundant, the trainable components have each converged to approximately $f(x') - \alpha \, g_{0,k}(\tau, x')$, so $G_{\phi_k}(\tau, x') \approx f(x')$ regardless of $k$, and the disagreement vanishes.

This is exactly the behavior we want from an acquisition signal: high where we need more data, low where we have enough. The randomized-prior ensemble achieves this without any of the machinery required by GP posteriors: no kernel function, no Cholesky factorization, no posterior sampling. The implementation is simple: define a frozen random network, add it to the trainable network, and train with the standard pinball loss. The result is a practical epistemic uncertainty estimate that works for any neural network architecture, including the IQN.

The `RandomPriorNet` class implements this idea for a simple MLP. The same principle applies to IQN members, where we add a frozen random network to the IQN's output.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import numpy as np

class RandomPriorNet(nn.Module):
    """Trainable MLP + frozen random prior (Osband et al. 2018).

    The output is: train_net(x) + prior_scale * prior_net(x),
    where prior_net has frozen random weights.
    """
    def __init__(self, xdim, hdim=128, nlayers=3, prior_scale=0.5):
        super().__init__()
        # Trainable network
        layers = [nn.Linear(xdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        for _ in range(nlayers - 1):
            layers += [nn.Linear(hdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        layers.append(nn.Linear(hdim, 1))
        self.train_net = nn.Sequential(*layers)

        # Frozen prior network (same architecture, random weights)
        prior_layers = [nn.Linear(xdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        for _ in range(nlayers - 1):
            prior_layers += [nn.Linear(hdim, hdim), nn.ReLU()]
        prior_layers.append(nn.Linear(hdim, 1))
        self.prior_net = nn.Sequential(*prior_layers)
        for p in self.prior_net.parameters():
            p.requires_grad = False  # freeze!

        self.prior_scale = prior_scale

    def forward(self, x):
        return self.train_net(x) + self.prior_scale * self.prior_net(x)

# Verify: three ensemble members give different predictions at the same point
torch.manual_seed(0)
ensemble = [RandomPriorNet(xdim=2, hdim=64, nlayers=2, prior_scale=0.5)
            for _ in range(3)]

x_test = torch.tensor([[0.5, 0.5]])  # a single test point
for k, model in enumerate(ensemble):
    with torch.no_grad():
        pred = model(x_test).item()
    print(f"  Member {k}: prediction = {pred:.4f}")
```

Even before training, the three members produce different predictions at the same input because their frozen prior networks have different random weights. After training on data, members will agree in data-rich regions and continue to disagree in data-sparse regions. The training data "wash out" the prior perturbation where they are abundant, but in data-sparse regions the perturbation persists, creating a measurable signal of epistemic uncertainty.

The connection to Bayesian reasoning is explicit. In a GP, the posterior variance at a point $x$ measures how much the posterior distribution of $f(x)$ would change if we obtained different training data. The randomized-prior ensemble achieves something analogous: the ensemble disagreement at $x$ measures how much the prediction at $x$ would change if we used a different prior network. In data-rich regions, the prediction is determined by the data regardless of the prior, so disagreement is low. In data-sparse regions, the prediction depends on the prior, so disagreement is high. This is the neural-network analog of the GP's prior-to-posterior contraction.

There is an important subtlety regarding the frozen prior network's architecture. In our implementation, the prior network has the same architecture (depth, width, activation function) as the trainable network. This is a design choice, not a requirement. Using a simpler prior network (e.g., a single-layer MLP with fewer hidden units) would produce smoother prior perturbations, biasing the disagreement signal toward large-scale spatial variation rather than fine-grained variation. Using a more complex prior network would produce more rapidly varying perturbations, potentially creating spurious disagreement peaks at small spatial scales. We found that matching the architectures provides a good balance: the prior perturbation varies at roughly the same spatial scale as the trainable network's predictions, so the disagreement signal is sensitive to the same features that the surrogate is trying to learn.

The weight initialization distribution for the frozen network also matters. We use the default PyTorch initialization (Kaiming uniform for linear layers), which produces weights scaled to maintain unit variance through the network. Other initializations would produce different prior perturbation scales. For instance, initializing the frozen network with larger weights would effectively increase $\alpha$ without changing the prior scale parameter, while initializing with smaller weights would decrease the effective prior contribution. In practice, the combination of default initialization and $\alpha = 0.5$ produces perturbations of appropriate magnitude relative to the trainable network's predictions, and we have not found it necessary to tune the initialization distribution separately.

One might also consider using structured prior networks rather than random ones. For example, if the practitioner has prior knowledge about the response surface's structure, such as periodicity, monotonicity, or known symmetries, this knowledge could be encoded in the frozen prior network's architecture or initialization. A periodic prior network (using sinusoidal activation functions) would produce periodic perturbations, making the ensemble more sensitive to periodic features in the response surface. We have not explored structured priors in this chapter, but they represent a natural extension that would bridge the gap between the GP's explicit prior (encoded in the kernel function) and the randomized prior's implicit prior (encoded in the random network architecture).

Each of these failure modes deserves closer examination, because they illuminate why epistemic uncertainty is fundamentally difficult for neural networks.

*Bootstrap ensembles* fail because active learning operates in the small-$n$ regime where bootstrap resampling is most destructive. With $n = 50$ training points and sampling with replacement, approximately $1 - (1 - 1/n)^n \approx 63\%$ of the original points appear in each resample, meaning roughly a third of the data is missing from each member's training set. When $n$ is small, those missing points may constitute the only observations in an entire region of input space, so the ensemble member trained without them produces wildly inaccurate predictions there, not because epistemic uncertainty is high, but because the training set is deficient. The resulting disagreement is driven by data poverty rather than genuine model uncertainty, creating an acquisition signal that is essentially noise.

The problem is compounded by the sequential nature of active learning. After the first acquisition, the training set grows to $n + 1$ points. The bootstrap resample of this slightly larger set will still be degenerate, and the new point (which was selected based on the noisy acquisition signal) may not improve the situation. Over multiple iterations, the bootstrap ensemble can enter a pathological cycle: it selects uninformative points because its disagreement signal is noisy, and the uninformative points do not improve the disagreement signal, so the next selection is equally uninformative. In our 7D proxy benchmark, bootstrap ensemble acquisition performed 17% *worse* than uniform random sampling, meaning the acquisition signal was actively harmful, it directed acquisitions to worse locations than random chance would have produced. On the SatMini benchmark, the degradation was 30%, confirming that bootstrap ensembles are unsuitable for active learning with neural networks at small sample sizes.

*SNGP* (Spectral Normalized GP) replaces the neural network's last layer with a GP, using spectral normalization of the preceding layers to preserve distance information. The idea is appealing: get the GP's calibrated uncertainty from the last layer while retaining the neural network's feature extraction in the preceding layers. The spectral normalization ensures that the feature map does not collapse distances, if two inputs $x$ and $x'$ are far apart in the input space, they remain far apart in the feature space, so the GP last layer can assign high uncertainty between them. In practice, however, the spectral normalization constraint limits the network's representational capacity, and the GP last layer operates in a learned feature space whose geometry may not support meaningful distance-based uncertainty for sequential design. The feature space changes each time the network is retrained (because spectral normalization does not fix the feature map, only constrain it), so the GP's uncertainty estimates are not comparable across iterations. On our low-dimensional benchmarks ($d = 2, 3$), SNGP performed comparably to randomized priors, but on $d = 7$ it produced acquisition signals that were no better than random selection. We suspect this is because the spectral normalization becomes increasingly conservative in high dimensions, constraining the network's ability to learn a useful feature representation while providing diminishing uncertainty benefits.

*Anchored ensembles* [@pearce2020uncertainty] add an L2 penalty that pulls each member's weights toward their random initialization, rather than freezing the prior network entirely. The loss for member $k$ becomes $\mathcal{L}_k = \mathcal{L}_{\text{data}} + \lambda \|\phi_k - \phi_{0,k}\|_2^2$, where $\phi_{0,k}$ are the random initial weights and $\lambda$ controls the penalty strength. This is mathematically similar to the randomized-prior approach, in both cases, member predictions in data-sparse regions are influenced by their random initialization. However, the anchored approach requires tuning the regularization strength $\lambda$, and in sequential settings where the training set changes at every iteration, the optimal regularization strength changes too. When $\lambda$ is too large, the trainable weights cannot move far from initialization and the ensemble underfits the data. When $\lambda$ is too small, the weights converge to the empirical risk minimizer and the ensemble collapses, just like a standard ensemble. We found that anchored ensembles were less stable across iterations than randomized priors, with occasional pathological rounds where the acquisition signal collapsed. The frozen-prior approach avoids this tuning problem entirely by separating the trainable and prior components architecturally rather than through regularization.

*Single-model quantile spread* is the most natural GBC approach and therefore the most instructive failure. The IQN produces quantile estimates $\hat{Q}(\tau \mid x)$ for any $\tau$, so a natural acquisition function is the predicted interval width:
$$
a_{\text{spread}}(x) = \hat{Q}(0.95 \mid x) - \hat{Q}(0.05 \mid x).
$$ {#eq-al-spread}
Note: this uses a 90% predictive interval ($\tau \in \{0.05, 0.95\}$), chosen here because it is more sensitive to local variation in data-sparse regions than the 95% interval ($\tau \in \{0.025, 0.975\}$) used elsewhere in the book. The rest of the book standardizes on $\hat{Q}(0.975) - \hat{Q}(0.025)$; either convention is valid, but this chapter uses the 90% interval throughout.

This measures the IQN's *predictive* uncertainty. The problem is that this spread captures aleatoric uncertainty (noise in $y \mid x$) rather than epistemic uncertainty (ignorance about $f$). Worse, as the IQN overfits the growing training set in sequential settings, the predictive spread can actually *shrink* in data-sparse regions, directing acquisition away from where it is needed most. The mechanism is subtle: in regions with no data, the IQN's quantile function is determined entirely by the random initialization and the implicit regularization of SGD. An overparameterized network trained to convergence on the available data can produce any shape of quantile function in data-free regions, including narrow intervals that appear confident. As the training set grows in sequential settings, the IQN fits the data more tightly, and the quantile spread in data-free regions can shrink even further, the network becomes more confident in its extrapolation even though no new data have appeared in those regions. This is the opposite of what we want: the acquisition function should *increase* in data-free regions as the model becomes better calibrated in data-rich regions, directing future acquisitions toward the remaining gaps.

The quantile spread failure has a formal analog in the GP literature: a GP with a misspecified noise variance will produce posterior intervals that are too narrow, causing the ALC criterion to underweight uncertain regions. The difference is that the GP's miscalibration can be diagnosed and corrected by estimating the noise variance from residuals, while the IQN's quantile spread miscalibration has no simple fix within the single-model framework. The ensemble disagreement criterion, described next, avoids this failure mode by measuring *inter-model* variation rather than *intra-model* variation.


## Acquisition Functions {#sec-al-acquisition}

We now have a mechanism (randomized-prior ensembles) for generating diverse predictions that reflect epistemic uncertainty. The next step is to convert this diversity into a scalar *acquisition function*, a number at each candidate point that tells us how valuable it would be to evaluate the simulator there. The acquisition function must satisfy two properties: it should be high in regions where the surrogate is uncertain (encouraging exploration) and it should decrease after a point is acquired in that region (preventing the algorithm from repeatedly selecting the same location). The ensemble disagreement satisfies both properties naturally.

Before defining the acquisition function, we note that other ways of converting ensemble diversity into a scalar score are possible. One alternative is the *range* of predictions: $a_{\text{range}}(x) = \max_k \tilde{y}_k(x) - \min_k \tilde{y}_k(x)$. The range is more sensitive to outlier ensemble members than the standard deviation, which may be desirable if one member has detected a feature that the others have missed. Another alternative is the *interquartile range* of predictions, which is more robust to outliers but requires $K \geq 4$ ensemble members. We use the standard deviation (equivalently, the variance) throughout this chapter because it is the most common choice in the ensemble learning literature and performs well with $K = 3$ members.

With a trained randomized-prior ensemble of $K$ IQN members, we define the acquisition function as the *ensemble disagreement*: the standard deviation of median predictions across members.

If multiple models trained on the same data make different predictions at a point $x$, then the data have not determined the function value at $x$. The standard deviation across predictions quantifies this indeterminacy. What makes randomized priors special is that they guarantee this disagreement persists in data-sparse regions, whereas a standard ensemble would eventually collapse as all members converge to the same fit.

::: {.callout-tip appearance="simple" title="Definition 9.2: Ensemble Disagreement Acquisition"}
Given $K$ ensemble members with median predictions $\tilde{y}_k(x) = \text{median}_b \, G_{\phi_k}(\tau^{(b)}, x)$ for $b = 1, \ldots, B$ samples, the *ensemble disagreement* at $x$ is
$$
a(x) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{K-1} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \bigl(\tilde{y}_k(x) - \bar{\tilde{y}}(x)\bigr)^2}, \quad \bar{\tilde{y}}(x) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} \tilde{y}_k(x).
$$ {#eq-ensemble-disagreement}
The next acquisition point is $x_{n+1}^* = \arg\max_{x \in \mathcal{C}} \, a(x)$.
:::

In practice, we compute the median prediction of each member by averaging $B = 100$ forward passes with $\tau^{(b)} \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ and taking the empirical median. The disagreement is then the standard deviation across the $K$ medians.

Note that we use the *median* rather than the mean of each member's predictions. The median is more robust to the long tails that can arise in IQN predictions at extreme quantile levels, especially in the early rounds of active learning when the ensemble members are not yet well-trained. This robustness matters: if one ensemble member produces an outlier prediction at $\tau = 0.99$, it will inflate the mean but not the median, and the disagreement computed from medians will better reflect the genuine spread in the members' central estimates.

An alternative formulation computes the disagreement not at the median but at a specific quantile level $\tau_0$. Setting $\tau_0 = 0.5$ (the median) is the most natural choice for symmetric distributions, but for skewed distributions one might want to acquire points where the ensemble disagrees most at the tails. For instance, setting $\tau_0 = 0.95$ would direct acquisitions toward regions where the upper tail of the conditional distribution is most uncertain. This could be useful for reliability engineering applications where the tail behavior matters more than the central tendency. We have not explored this direction systematically, but it is a natural extension of the framework.

One could also combine disagreement across multiple quantile levels into a single acquisition function. For example, one could compute the disagreement at $\tau \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9\}$ and take the maximum across these levels. This would direct acquisitions toward regions where the ensemble disagrees about *any* aspect of the conditional distribution, not just the center. On heteroscedastic problems where the noise level varies across the input space, this multi-quantile acquisition function might identify regions where the ensemble members disagree about the width of the conditional distribution even if they agree about its center.

def ensemble_disagreement(models, X):
    """Compute ensemble disagreement as acquisition function.

    Args:
        models: list of K trained ensemble members
        X: (n_candidates, d) tensor of candidate points

    Returns:
        (n_candidates,) array of disagreement scores
    """
    preds = []
    for m in models:
        m.eval()
        with torch.no_grad():
            preds.append(m(X).squeeze(-1).cpu().numpy())
    preds = np.stack(preds)  # (K, n_candidates)
    return preds.std(axis=0, ddof=1)  # sample std across ensemble members (matches Definition 9.2)

    Returns:
        Indices into X_candidates of selected points.
    """
    scores = ensemble_disagreement(models, X_candidates)
    return np.argsort(scores)[-batch_size:][::-1].copy()

The implementation choices are: (1) retraining from scratch at each iteration (as opposed to warm-starting), (2) removing selected points from the candidate pool (to avoid reselecting the same point), and (3) using a fixed number of training epochs rather than an adaptive convergence criterion. def active_learning_loop(
    f_simulator,        # simulator function: x -> y
    X_init, y_init,     # initial design
    X_candidates,       # candidate pool
    n_acquire,          # number of points to acquire
    K=3,                # ensemble size
    epochs=3000,        # training epochs per round
):
    """GBC active learning with randomized-prior ensemble.

    train_model: trains a RandomPriorNet on (X, y) for the given
    number of epochs using Adam and pinball loss. In practice, use
    gbc.ensemble.train_het_mlp or a custom training loop.
    """
    X_train = X_init.clone()
    y_train = y_init.clone()

    for step in range(n_acquire):
        # Train K ensemble members from scratch
        ensemble = []
        for k in range(K):
            model = RandomPriorNet(
                xdim=X_train.shape[1], hdim=128,
                nlayers=3, prior_scale=0.5
            )
            train_model(model, X_train, y_train, epochs=epochs)
            ensemble.append(model)

        # Select next point by maximum disagreement
        idx = select_next(ensemble, X_candidates, batch_size=1).item()
        x_new = X_candidates[idx].unsqueeze(0)   # (1, d)
        y_new = f_simulator(x_new).reshape(-1)

        # Update training set
        X_train = torch.cat([X_train, x_new])
        y_train = torch.cat([y_train, y_new])

        # Remove selected point from candidate pool
        mask = torch.ones(len(X_candidates), dtype=torch.bool)
        mask[idx] = False
        X_candidates = X_candidates[mask]

    return X_train, y_train

The GP's ALC criterion (@eq-alc) and the ensemble disagreement criterion (@eq-ensemble-disagreement) target the same goal, identifying regions of high epistemic uncertainty, but through fundamentally different mechanisms.

| Property | GP (ALC) | GBC (Ensemble Disagreement) |
|-------|-------|----------------|
| Uncertainty source | Posterior covariance (Bayesian) | Inter-model variation (frequentist) |
| Look-ahead | Yes (computes reduction in integrated variance) | No (greedy: max current disagreement) |
| Smoothness prior | Explicit (kernel hyperparameters) | Implicit (network architecture + prior scale) |
| Cost per acquisition | $O(n^2)$ update, $O(n_{\text{cand}} \cdot n)$ evaluation | $O(K \cdot \text{training})$ retrain from scratch |
| Scaling | $O(n^3)$ per retrain (Cholesky) | $O(n)$ per retrain (SGD) |

: Comparison of GP-ALC and GBC ensemble disagreement for active learning. {#tbl-al-comparison}

The table reveals a fundamental architectural difference: the GP computes uncertainty analytically from a probabilistic model, while GBC computes uncertainty empirically from an ensemble of point estimators. This difference has consequences that go beyond computational cost. The GP's uncertainty is *calibrated* by construction (assuming the kernel is correctly specified): the posterior variance at a test point has a precise Bayesian interpretation as the expected squared error of the posterior mean. The ensemble disagreement has no such calibration guarantee, it is a heuristic measure of inter-model variation that correlates with true epistemic uncertainty but is not equal to it. On our benchmarks, the correlation between ensemble disagreement and true squared error ranges from 0.6 to 0.8 depending on the problem, which is sufficient for acquisition but not for downstream uncertainty quantification.

The GP's look-ahead property is a genuine advantage: ALC does not just find the point of highest uncertainty, it finds the point whose addition would most reduce *total* uncertainty. The ensemble disagreement criterion is greedy, it picks the point of highest current disagreement without considering the downstream effect. In practice, the greedy strategy works well because ensemble disagreement tends to be highest in the same regions where adding a point would most reduce global uncertainty, but the correspondence is not exact.

The GP's smoothness prior (discussed in @sec-surr-gp-limits) is both a strength and a weakness: powerful regularization on smooth functions with small $n$, but a poor fit for discontinuous or heterogeneous response surfaces.

There is a deeper connection between ALC and ensemble disagreement. Both criteria are attempting to estimate the same quantity: the *reduction in prediction error* that would result from evaluating the simulator at a candidate point. ALC estimates this directly, using the GP's posterior to compute the expected variance reduction. Ensemble disagreement estimates it indirectly: points of high disagreement are points where the current surrogate is unreliable, and evaluating the simulator there will reduce the disagreement (and hence the prediction error) by providing data that resolves the inter-model ambiguity. The two criteria would be identical if the ensemble disagreement were perfectly calibrated, that is, if the standard deviation of ensemble predictions equaled the true standard deviation of the prediction error. In practice, the calibration is imperfect, but the correlation between disagreement and true error is strong enough (typically 0.6--0.8 in our experiments) that the greedy selection of maximum-disagreement points produces near-optimal acquisition sequences.

The computational architecture of the two approaches also differs in a way that matters for implementation. The per-candidate cost is $O(n^2)$ for GP-ALC versus $O(Kw^2L)$ for the ensemble, comparable at small $n$, but the GP cost grows quadratically while the network cost is independent of $n$. See @sec-surr-scaling for the full flop-count comparison.

There is another operational difference that matters in practice: the GP-based ALC criterion is typically implemented in R (via packages like `deepgp` or `hetGP`), while the IQN ensemble is implemented in Python/PyTorch. The GP implementation runs on CPU, while the IQN runs on GPU. This means that the wall-clock times for the two methods are not directly comparable, the GPU provides a substantial constant-factor speedup for the matrix operations involved in the network's forward and backward passes. A fair comparison would require either running both methods on the same hardware or reporting asymptotic operation counts rather than wall-clock times. We have tried to do the latter in @tbl-al-comparison, but the reader should be aware that the wall-clock advantage of GBC-AL is partly architectural (GPU vs. CPU) rather than purely algorithmic.


## 2D Exponential Benchmark {#sec-al-2d}

We now evaluate GBC-AL on three benchmarks from the computer experiments literature, following the experimental protocol of @sauer2023active exactly. Each benchmark reveals a different aspect of the GBC-vs-GP tradeoff. The 2D Exponential (@sec-al-2d) is smooth, low-dimensional, and favors the GP. The Rocket LGBB (@sec-al-rocket) has a sharp nonlinear ridge and favors GBC. The Satellite GRACE (@sec-al-satellite) is smooth and high-dimensional, producing a crossover where GBC leads early and the GP leads late. Together, these three benchmarks map out the frontier of GBC-AL's advantages and limitations.

The benchmarks are ordered by increasing complexity and increasing favorability to GBC-AL. This ordering is deliberate: we begin with a benchmark where GBC loses (2D Exponential) to establish that the comparison is fair and to identify the conditions under which GPs have a genuine advantage. We then move to a benchmark where GBC wins decisively (Rocket LGBB) to demonstrate the potential of the method. Finally, we examine a benchmark with a nuanced crossover (Satellite GRACE) that captures the full complexity of the GBC-vs-GP tradeoff. Three supplementary benchmarks (SatMini, 7D Proxy, and the full Satellite at $n = 500$) provide additional evidence and confirm the patterns observed in the primary benchmarks.

The 2D Exponential function is the simplest benchmark from @sauer2023active:
$$
f(x_1, x_2) = 10 \, x_1 \exp(-x_1^2 - x_2^2), \quad (x_1, x_2) \in [-2, 2]^2,
$$ {#eq-2d-exp}
with additive Gaussian noise $\varepsilon \sim N(0, 0.1^2)$. The function is smooth, unimodal, and well-suited to a squared-exponential kernel. It is a setting where GPs should excel, and they do.

Following @sauer2023active, we start with $n_0 = 10$ initial LHS points and acquire 90 additional points one at a time ($n_{\text{total}} = 100$), retraining after each acquisition. We use $K = 3$ randomized-prior IQN members with 3,000 training epochs per round. The comparator is DGP+ALC, a two-layer deep GP with elliptical slice sampling, using the ALC acquisition criterion [@sauer2023active]. We run 30 independent replicates.

|-----|-----------|------|
| DGP+ALC | *0.072* | 30 |
| GBC-AL (randprior, $K$=3) | 0.087 $\pm$ 0.008 | 30 |

The DGP achieves 21% lower RMSE than GBC-AL. This gap is *structural*, not a tuning failure. We investigated several modifications (more ensemble members, longer training, different prior scales) and none closed the gap substantially.

Why does GBC lose here? Three factors combine to favor the GP on this benchmark, and understanding each one clarifies when GBC-AL should and should not be used.

The first factor is *exploration--exploitation balance*. ALC is a look-ahead criterion: it evaluates how much adding a candidate point would reduce the integrated posterior variance over the entire domain. This means ALC inherently balances exploration (sampling in regions of high uncertainty) with exploitation (refining predictions where the function varies most). The ensemble disagreement criterion, by contrast, is greedy, it simply picks the point of maximum current disagreement. On a smooth 2D surface, ALC's ability to plan ahead matters: it recognizes that adding a point at one location will reduce uncertainty not just locally but over a neighborhood determined by the kernel length-scale. The greedy GBC criterion does not account for this spatial spillover, and occasionally selects points that are informative locally but redundant given nearby observations.

The second factor is *boundary detection*. The 2D Exponential function has its steepest gradients near $x_1 \approx \pm 0.7$ where the exponential decay begins. The GP kernel, with its learned length-scale, provides an implicit model of the function's smoothness that concentrates posterior variance exactly where the gradient is large. The IQN ensemble, lacking an explicit smoothness model, distributes its disagreement more uniformly, sometimes wasting acquisitions in the flat tails of the function where additional data does little to improve the fit.

The third factor is *variance estimation from limited data*. With only 100 total points (starting from 10), there is very little data for the neural network to learn from. The GP's prior encodes smoothness and length-scale, providing strong regularization that effectively fills in the function between observed points. The IQN must learn its implicit smoothness from data alone. In two dimensions the GP kernel has only a few hyperparameters (length-scale, signal variance, noise variance) to estimate, and maximum likelihood estimation of these is reliable even from $n = 10$ points. As $d$ grows, the number of kernel hyperparameters increases and MLE becomes less reliable, a setting that increasingly favors the neural network. The 2D Exponential has no discontinuities, ridges, or heteroscedastic noise, exactly the kind of function the squared-exponential kernel was designed for, so the GP's inductive bias is perfectly matched to the target.

Together, these three factors create a compounding advantage for the GP. The length-scale prior provides effective interpolation between sparse points; the look-ahead acquisition criterion uses this interpolation to plan efficient sampling trajectories; and the low dimensionality ensures that the kernel's hyperparameters are well-estimated from the available data. Remove any one of these conditions, increase the dimension, add non-smoothness, or expand the budget, and the GP's advantage shrinks or reverses.

There is an instructive way to visualize the difference between the two methods' acquisition patterns on this benchmark. If we plot the 90 acquired points in the $(x_1, x_2)$ plane, the DGP+ALC acquisitions cluster near the peak of the function at $(x_1, x_2) \approx (0.7, 0)$ and its mirror image at $(-0.7, 0)$, with a secondary concentration along the ridge where $f$ transitions from positive to negative values. The GBC-AL acquisitions are more diffuse, spreading across the domain with less concentration near the function's features. This diffuseness reflects the ensemble disagreement's lack of look-ahead: it does not "know" that the function has its largest gradients near $(0.7, 0)$, so it distributes points more uniformly. On a smooth function, this uniformity is wasteful, points in the flat tails of the Gaussian envelope contribute little to reducing RMSE. The GP's ALC criterion, using its length-scale estimate, correctly identifies the peak regions as information-rich and concentrates points there.

The learning curve tells a more nuanced story than the final RMSE alone. Both methods start at similar RMSE after the initial $n_0 = 10$ points, and both improve steadily as points are acquired. The DGP's RMSE decreases roughly as $n^{-1}$ (consistent with its squared-exponential kernel on a smooth function), while GBC-AL's RMSE decreases more slowly, roughly as $n^{-0.7}$. The gap opens early, by $n = 30$ the DGP is already visibly ahead, and remains roughly constant in relative terms through $n = 100$. There is no crossover: the GP maintains its advantage at every sample size on this benchmark.

It is natural to ask whether the 21% gap could be closed by using a more sophisticated IQN architecture or a better training procedure. We investigated several modifications: increasing the ensemble size from $K = 3$ to $K = 10$ (which reduced RMSE by 3% but tripled retraining time), doubling the training epochs to 6,000 (no significant improvement), varying the prior scale over $\alpha \in [0.1, 2.0]$ (best results at $\alpha = 0.5$, the default), and using a wider network (512 hidden units instead of 128, which reduced RMSE by 2%). None of these modifications closed the gap by more than 5%, suggesting that the 21% advantage is structural rather than a tuning artifact. The GP's smoothness prior provides genuine information about this particular function, and no amount of neural network tuning can replicate that information without explicitly encoding it in the architecture.

Could we close the gap by giving the IQN a smoothness prior? In principle, yes: we could add a regularization term that penalizes large gradients of the IQN's output with respect to its inputs, effectively encouraging smooth predictions. This would be analogous to a squared-exponential kernel prior in the GP framework. However, doing so would sacrifice the IQN's main advantage, its ability to handle non-smooth functions without modification. The strength of GBC-AL is its generality: the same algorithm works on smooth functions, non-smooth functions, and everything in between. Adding a smoothness prior would improve performance on smooth benchmarks at the cost of degrading performance on non-smooth ones. For the 2D Exponential specifically, this tradeoff might be worthwhile, but for a general-purpose tool it is not.

This benchmark establishes an important baseline: on smooth, low-dimensional problems with small budgets, GP-based active learning has a structural advantage. The 21% gap quantifies what the GP's prior is worth in this favorable regime. As we will see next, the balance shifts dramatically when the target function violates the GP's assumptions.


## Rocket Booster (LGBB) {#sec-al-rocket}

The Langley Glide-Back Booster (LGBB) benchmark [@pamadi2004aerodynamic; @gramacy2020surrogates; @sauer2023active] models the aerodynamics of a reusable NASA rocket booster gliding back to Earth after stage separation. The LGBB concept was developed at NASA Langley Research Center as a fly-back first-stage booster for a two-stage-to-orbit launch system. The simulator computes aerodynamic coefficients using panel methods and wind tunnel data. The three inputs are Mach number (ranging from 0.4 to 4.0, covering subsonic through supersonic regimes), angle of attack (0 to 20 degrees), and side-slip angle (0 to 6 degrees); the response is the side-force coefficient, one of six aerodynamic coefficients produced by the simulator.

What makes LGBB interesting for active learning is the *sound barrier*. At Mach $\approx 1$, the aerodynamic regime transitions from subsonic to supersonic, creating a sharp nonlinear ridge in the response surface. This ridge runs through the Mach--angle-of-attack plane and is not aligned with any coordinate axis. A stationary GP with a squared-exponential kernel systematically oversmooths this ridge, because the kernel assigns positive correlation to points on opposite sides of the discontinuity. The side-force coefficient can change by a factor of five over a narrow range of Mach numbers near the sound barrier, while varying smoothly at Mach numbers far from one. This *non-stationarity*, the function's behavior varies dramatically across the input space, is precisely the type of structure that GBC handles naturally (see @sec-jumps) but that GP kernels struggle with.

The LGBB benchmark has been a standard test case in the computer experiments literature since @pamadi2004aerodynamic introduced the simulator. @gramacy2020surrogates uses it to illustrate the limitations of stationary GP kernels and the advantages of local GP methods. @sauer2023active uses it as one of three active learning benchmarks for deep GPs. Our comparison follows their protocol exactly, providing a controlled evaluation of GBC-AL against the state-of-the-art GP method on the same problem.

Would a non-stationary GP, one with spatially varying length-scales or a treed partition of the input space, close the gap with GBC-AL on this benchmark? @gramacy2020surrogates demonstrates that local GPs (which fit separate GPs in different regions of the input space) can improve on stationary GPs for the LGBB problem. However, local GPs require specifying or learning the partition, which adds complexity and hyperparameters. The DGP+ALC method used as our comparator is already a non-stationary GP method (the deep GP uses a two-layer architecture that can learn input warping), and it still underperforms GBC-AL by a factor of three. This suggests that the issue is not just stationarity but the GP's fundamental reliance on kernel-based interpolation, which assigns positive correlation to nearby points even when they lie on opposite sides of a sharp feature.

The *acquisition geography* on the LGBB surface reveals why GBC-AL succeeds so decisively. The ensemble disagreement concentrates on three distinct types of regions, and this concentration pattern is the key to the $2.95\times$ improvement.

First, the ensemble places the majority of its acquisitions near the *Mach $= 1$ ridge*. In the Mach--angle-of-attack plane, the sound barrier creates a narrow band where the side-force coefficient changes rapidly. The randomized-prior ensemble members, each learning this ridge at a slightly different location and with a different slope, produce maximum disagreement precisely along the ridge crest. The result is a dense line of acquisitions that traces the boundary between subsonic and supersonic regimes. This is exactly where additional data is most valuable: each point along the ridge tightens the surrogate's estimate of the ridge location and sharpness, reducing RMSE throughout the surrounding region. The DGP's ALC criterion, by contrast, distributes acquisitions more uniformly across the domain because its stationary kernel assigns positive correlation across the ridge, blurring the posterior variance signal.

Second, the ensemble directs acquisitions to *high-variance regions* at large angles of attack, where the side-force coefficient exhibits large magnitude and complex behavior. These regions have fewer initial LHS points (the corners of the input space are undersampled by Latin hypercube designs), so the ensemble members disagree because their prior networks dominate the trainable component. Once a few points are acquired in these regions, the disagreement drops sharply as the trainable components learn the function, and the ensemble redirects its attention elsewhere.

Third, as the budget increases beyond $n \approx 200$, the ensemble begins *filling in* the subsonic and supersonic regimes with more evenly spaced points, a pattern that resembles space-filling design applied separately within each regime. By this point, the ridge has been well characterized and the remaining epistemic uncertainty is distributed more uniformly.

This three-phase acquisition pattern, ridge detection, corner filling, uniform refinement, emerges naturally from the ensemble disagreement criterion without any explicit programming. The algorithm does not "know" about the sound barrier; it simply selects the point of maximum disagreement at each iteration. The fact that this greedy criterion produces a sensible sampling strategy tailored to the response surface geometry reflects the quality of the ensemble disagreement signal. The DGP's ALC criterion, by contrast, produces a more uniform sampling pattern throughout the experiment because its stationary kernel cannot distinguish the ridge from the smooth regions on either side.

Why does GBC win so decisively here? The sound-barrier ridge creates a sharp nonlinearity that the GP's stationary kernel cannot represent without an unreasonably large number of inducing points, while the IQN, as a universal approximator with no smoothness constraint, learns the ridge geometry naturally. The acquisition signals differ in character as well: the randomized-prior ensemble concentrates disagreement near the ridge, where the function changes rapidly, whereas the GP's ALC criterion assigns positive correlation across the discontinuity and therefore spreads evaluations more uniformly rather than concentrating them near the sharp feature. Finally, with 300 total points in 3D, the IQN has enough data to learn the function well, so the GP's prior advantage, which helped on the 2D Exponential at $n = 100$, is now outweighed by its smoothness bias.

The *side-slip angle* dimension is also informative. The LGBB problem is three-dimensional, but the non-stationarity is primarily in the Mach--angle-of-attack plane. The side-slip angle modulates the response smoothly without introducing additional discontinuities. The ensemble disagreement criterion correctly identifies this asymmetry: it concentrates most acquisitions in the Mach--angle-of-attack plane (particularly near the ridge) while distributing side-slip acquisitions more uniformly. This adaptive allocation of acquisitions across dimensions is another advantage of the data-driven ensemble disagreement criterion over the GP's ALC, which treats all dimensions symmetrically through its isotropic (or at best anisotropic with learned length-scales) kernel.

Even the worst GBC-AL run (RMSE $\approx 0.0092$) outperforms the best DGP run (RMSE $\approx 0.018$) by a factor of two. This level of separation is unusual in benchmark comparisons, where results often overlap within one or two standard errors. It reflects the fundamental mismatch between the DGP's stationary kernel and the LGBB's non-stationary response surface.

The complete separation of replicate distributions is important for statistical inference. In many benchmark comparisons, the conclusion depends on the number of replicates: with 10 replicates, a 10% difference might not be statistically significant, but with 100 replicates it would be. Here, significance is not in question: all 1500 pairwise comparisons favor GBC (Mann-Whitney $U = 1500$ out of $1500$, $p < 10^{-15}$), corresponding to the extreme value of the Mann-Whitney $U$-statistic with a $p$-value that is effectively zero regardless of the assumed distribution.

The variability across GBC-AL replicates (standard deviation of RMSE $\approx 0.0008$) is also informative. This variability comes from two sources: the random initial LHS design and the stochastic training of the ensemble members. Across 30 replicates, different initial designs lead to different acquisition trajectories, yet the final RMSE is consistent. This suggests that the ensemble disagreement criterion is robust to the choice of initial design: even when the initial points are in suboptimal locations, the acquisition function quickly identifies the important features of the response surface (particularly the sound-barrier ridge) and directs evaluations accordingly.

The LGBB benchmark illustrates the core advantage of GBC for active learning: on non-smooth response surfaces, the IQN's flexibility translates directly into better surrogate quality, and the ensemble disagreement criterion directs evaluations toward the features that matter most. The $2.95\times$ advantage is not merely a statistical curiosity, it represents a qualitative difference in how the two methods allocate their acquisition budget, driven by the interaction between the surrogate's inductive bias and the response surface geometry.

For practitioners working with aerodynamic or fluid dynamics simulators, the LGBB result has immediate implications. Any simulator that involves shock waves, phase transitions, or turbulence onset will have sharp features in its response surface that stationary GP kernels cannot represent efficiently. On such problems, GBC-AL should be the default choice for sequential design, regardless of the input dimension or acquisition budget. The $2.95\times$ improvement translates directly into a $2.95\times$ reduction in the number of simulator evaluations needed to achieve a given RMSE target, a substantial saving when each evaluation costs hours of compute time.


## Satellite GRACE {#sec-al-satellite}

The Satellite GRACE benchmark [@sun2019emulating; @sauer2023active] uses a Test Particle Monte Carlo (TPMC) simulator developed at Los Alamos National Laboratory to compute drag coefficients for satellites in low-Earth orbit. The seven inputs describe thermospheric conditions, atmospheric composition (relative concentrations of atomic oxygen, molecular nitrogen, helium, and other species), velocity, and temperature, encountered by the GRACE satellite during its orbit. The Gravity Recovery and Climate Experiment (GRACE) mission, launched in 2002, consisted of two identical satellites flying in formation roughly 220 kilometers apart to measure Earth's gravitational field with unprecedented precision. The gravitational measurements rely on tracking the distance between the two satellites to micrometer accuracy using an onboard microwave ranging system. Accurate knowledge of non-gravitational forces, particularly atmospheric drag, is essential for isolating the gravitational signal from the orbit data, any unmodeled drag force corrupts the gravitational measurement.

The response is the satellite's drag coefficient, which is needed for orbit determination and collision avoidance. Accurate drag prediction is operationally critical: small errors in the drag coefficient compound over time, leading to position errors of kilometers within days, which matters for satellite constellation management and space debris tracking. The TPMC simulator models the interaction between individual gas molecules and the satellite surface, tracking thousands of test particles as they collide with and reflect off the spacecraft. Each evaluation takes approximately 86 seconds and involves Monte Carlo noise from the random particle trajectories.

Unlike LGBB, the satellite drag surface is *smooth* and the simulator is *stochastic* (approximately 86 seconds per evaluation, with Monte Carlo noise). This is a high-dimensional, expensive-to-evaluate problem where the GP's smoothness prior is well-matched to the true function. @mehta2014modeling show that a GP trained on a static 1,000-point Latin hypercube achieves less than 1% RMSPE, establishing the accuracy target; the active learning question is whether sequential design can reach this accuracy with fewer runs.

The GRACE benchmark is particularly important because it represents the *typical* use case for computer experiment surrogates: a smooth, expensive, moderately high-dimensional simulator where the practitioner wants to build a global surrogate with as few runs as possible. Most real-world engineering simulators fall into this category. The LGBB benchmark, with its sharp sound-barrier ridge, is the exception rather than the rule. Understanding how GBC-AL performs on the GRACE benchmark is therefore more informative for general practice than the LGBB result, impressive as it is.

The stochastic nature of the TPMC simulator adds an additional layer of complexity. Each evaluation produces a noisy estimate of the drag coefficient, with Monte Carlo noise that varies in magnitude across the input space. The GP handles this noise through its nugget parameter, which must be estimated from the data (the replicate pairs in the initial design help with this estimation). The IQN handles noise implicitly through the quantile loss, which adapts to the local noise level without requiring an explicit noise model. In principle, the IQN's ability to model heteroscedastic noise should give it an advantage on stochastic simulators, but in practice the GRACE simulator's noise is approximately homoscedastic, so this advantage does not materialize here.

Stochastic simulators also raise the question of *replication versus exploration*. Should the active learning algorithm evaluate the same input twice to reduce Monte Carlo noise, or should it always evaluate at a new input? The GP framework handles this naturally: the ALC criterion can recommend replication at an existing input if the noise reduction would produce a larger decrease in integrated posterior variance than evaluating at a new input. The GBC framework does not handle replication as naturally, because the ensemble disagreement is driven by inter-model variation at *new* inputs rather than noise reduction at *existing* inputs. In our GRACE experiments, we did not allow replication during the active learning phase (all acquisitions were at new inputs), which may have contributed to GBC-AL's advantage in the early phase: by always exploring new regions, GBC-AL covers the input space faster, even if individual predictions are noisier.

Following @sauer2023active, we use $n_0 = 110$ initial points (including 10 replicate pairs to estimate the noise variance) and acquire 390 additional points (10 independent replicates). The replicate pairs are important for the GP: they provide a direct estimate of the Monte Carlo noise variance $\sigma^2_\varepsilon$, which the GP needs as an input to its likelihood. The IQN does not need noise variance estimates because the pinball loss adapts to the noise level implicitly through the conditional quantile function.

The satellite results reveal a *crossover*: GBC-AL leads throughout the early acquisition phase, achieving 16% better RMSPE at $n = 200$. But after approximately $n = 300$ points, DGP overtakes GBC, ultimately reaching RMSPE = 0.967 at $n = 500$ compared to GBC's 1.192, a 23% advantage for the GP.

The crossover interpretation is central to understanding when GBC-AL should be preferred. Think of the crossover point $n^*$, here approximately 300, as the budget threshold below which GBC-AL dominates and above which GP-AL takes over. This threshold is determined by three factors that interact with the training set size in different ways.

The first factor is *acquisition signal quality*. At small $n$, the ensemble disagreement signal is relatively cheap to compute (no matrix inversions) and updates immediately after each acquisition. The three ensemble members, each with their own frozen prior network, produce robust disagreement estimates even from very limited data because the prior perturbation guarantees diversity regardless of training set size. The GP's ALC criterion, by contrast, depends on a posterior variance estimate that requires well-estimated kernel hyperparameters. In seven dimensions, the GP kernel has at least seven length-scale parameters, a signal variance, and a noise variance to estimate. With only $n_0 = 110$ initial points in $\mathbb{R}^7$, maximum likelihood estimation of these hyperparameters is unreliable, and the resulting ALC criterion may direct acquisitions to suboptimal locations.

The second factor is *smoothness prior value*. As data accumulate on a smooth surface, the GP's kernel hyperparameters converge to their optimal values and the posterior variance provides an increasingly accurate map of remaining uncertainty. The GP's smoothness prior, which was neutral early on, now becomes a powerful inductive bias that interpolates correctly between observed points while the neural network must learn that interpolation from data. On a smooth 7D surface, the GP's prior is doing real work, and by $n = 300$ it has enough data to identify the correct length-scales.

The third factor is *dimension-data ratio*. In seven dimensions, the "curse of dimensionality" means that covering the input space requires substantially more points than in two or three dimensions. The crossover at $n^* \approx 300$ corresponds to roughly 40 points per input dimension, a regime where the GP's parametric kernel begins to provide reliable interpolation. Had the problem been smooth in three dimensions (like a hypothetical smooth LGBB), the crossover would have occurred much earlier, perhaps at $n^* \approx 100$.

These three factors interact multiplicatively. The GP needs enough data both to estimate its kernel hyperparameters (which depends on $d$) and to fill in the function between observations (which also depends on $d$). The crossover point $n^*$ thus scales roughly linearly with $d$: in the benchmarks, we observe $n^* \approx 40d$ for smooth functions, though this rule of thumb is based on limited evidence and may not hold for $d > 10$. For the 2D Exponential, $40d = 80$, which is consistent with the GP's advantage at $n = 100$ (already past the crossover). For the Satellite, $40d = 280$, which is consistent with the observed crossover at $n^* \approx 300$. The SatMini benchmark at $n = 60$ is well below this threshold ($60 / 7 \approx 8.6$ points per dimension), explaining its strong 33% advantage for GBC-AL. The 7D Proxy at $n = 70$ gives $70 / 7 = 10$ points per dimension, also well below 40, consistent with its 38% GBC advantage. These data points, while limited, suggest a consistent relationship between the data-to-dimension ratio and the GBC-AL advantage on smooth problems.

Two additional smaller-scale benchmarks confirm these patterns, providing independent validation of the crossover phenomenon on related problems.

SatMini ($n_0 = 110$, $+80$ acquisitions, $n = 190$) is a reduced-budget version of the GRACE problem, using the same TPMC simulator and 7-dimensional inputs but restricting the acquisition budget to test active learning performance at very small sample sizes where every simulator evaluation ($\approx 86$ seconds) is costly. At this smaller budget, GBC-AL achieves RMSPE = 5.30 vs. DGP+ALC's 7.91, a 33% advantage, firmly in the "early phase" where GBC excels. The advantage is even larger than at $n = 200$ on the full Satellite benchmark (33% vs. 16%), consistent with GBC-AL's increasing advantage at smaller sample sizes.

The 7D Proxy ($n_0 = 50$, $+150$ acquisitions, $n = 200$) is a synthetic smooth function in $[0,1]^7$ designed to mimic the smooth, moderate-complexity structure of the satellite drag surface while being cheap to evaluate (enabling larger replicate counts and controlled experimentation). Like the full Satellite problem, the response is a smooth function of seven inputs with no discontinuities, testing whether the IQN ensemble can compete with the GP's smoothness prior in moderate dimension. GBC-AL achieves RMSE = 0.344 vs. DGP+ALC's 0.558, a 38% advantage at $n = 70$. This is the strongest GBC-AL advantage among the smooth-function benchmarks, occurring at the smallest ratio of $n/d$ ($70/7 = 10$ points per dimension), further supporting the pattern that GBC-AL's advantage grows as the data become sparser relative to the input dimension.

Both proxy benchmarks operate in the moderate-$n$ regime where GBC-AL's acquisition signal is most effective. The satellite result at $n = 500$ shows what happens when the GP is given enough data to fully exploit its smoothness prior.

We can examine the crossover more carefully. At $n = 200$, GBC-AL has acquired 90 points via ensemble disagreement, and these points are distributed across the 7D input space in a pattern that reflects the ensemble's uncertainty estimates. The GP has also acquired 90 points, but via ALC, which distributes them differently, more uniformly in regions where the GP's posterior variance is highest. The key question is: what happens between $n = 200$ and $n = 300$ that causes the GP to catch up?

The answer is that by $n \approx 250$, the GP has accumulated enough data to estimate its seven length-scale parameters reliably. Before this point, the length-scale estimates are noisy, and the ALC criterion based on these estimates directs acquisitions to suboptimal locations. A length-scale that is estimated too large causes the GP to treat distant points as correlated, blurring the posterior variance and distributing acquisitions too uniformly. A length-scale that is estimated too small causes the GP to treat nearby points as independent, concentrating acquisitions locally and missing the global structure. Both types of misestimation reduce the effectiveness of ALC relative to the simpler ensemble disagreement criterion.

After this point, the length-scale estimates stabilize, and ALC begins directing acquisitions with the precision that makes GP-based sequential design so effective on smooth surfaces. The IQN, which has no kernel hyperparameters to estimate, does not experience this transition, its acquisition signal quality is roughly constant throughout the experiment. This stability is an advantage in the early phase but becomes a limitation in the late phase, when the GP's calibrated posterior variance provides a more informative acquisition signal than the ensemble disagreement. GBC-AL has a consistent but unsophisticated acquisition signal, while GP-AL has a noisy signal early on that becomes precise once the kernel hyperparameters are well-estimated.

The crossover phenomenon has a practical implication for budget planning. If a practitioner has a fixed budget of 200 simulator runs on a smooth 7D problem, GBC-AL will produce a better surrogate than GP-AL. If the budget is 500 runs, GP-AL will produce the better surrogate. This means the choice of method should be made *before* the experiment begins, based on the anticipated budget and the expected properties of the response surface. One cannot simply start with GBC-AL "because it is better early" and switch to GP-AL later, because the design of experiment produced by GBC-AL during the early phase will not be the same as what GP-AL would have produced, and the GP cannot retroactively "undo" the GBC-AL acquisition choices.

An alternative strategy is to use GBC-AL throughout the experiment but apply a GP-based surrogate to the final training set. This *decouples* the acquisition criterion from the surrogate model, using GBC's ensemble disagreement to choose which points to evaluate and the GP's smoothness prior to fit the final model. The decoupled approach has a precedent in the Bayesian optimization literature, where the acquisition function is sometimes computed using one model (e.g., a neural network for scalability) while the final predictions use a different model (e.g., a GP for uncertainty quantification). We have not tested this hybrid approach systematically, but it is a natural direction for future work.

A third strategy is to *monitor the crossover* in real time. At each iteration, fit both a GBC surrogate and a GP surrogate to the current training set, and compare their leave-one-out prediction errors. When the GP begins outperforming the GBC surrogate on the LOO metric, switch the acquisition criterion from ensemble disagreement to ALC. This adaptive approach would automatically identify the crossover point and select the appropriate method for each phase of the experiment. The challenge is that computing LOO errors for the GP requires $O(n^3)$ computation at each iteration, which doubles the cost of the experiment. For expensive simulators where the surrogate retraining cost is negligible compared to the simulator cost, this overhead is acceptable.

We note that the crossover phenomenon is specific to *smooth* response surfaces. On non-smooth surfaces like the LGBB, there is no crossover: GBC-AL leads at every sample size, and the gap grows with $n$ rather than shrinking. This is because the GP's smoothness prior is not just neutral on non-smooth surfaces, it is actively harmful, causing the GP to oversmooth the sharp features regardless of how much data it accumulates. The IQN, with no smoothness constraint, learns the sharp features with increasing fidelity as more data become available. The practical implication is simple: if the response surface is known or expected to be non-smooth, use GBC-AL without reservation. The crossover analysis is only relevant for smooth problems.

The crossover also depends on the GP implementation. The DGP+ALC method used here is a non-stationary extension of the standard GP that can, in principle, learn input warping through its two-layer architecture. A simpler stationary GP might cross over later (because it takes longer to learn the function) or might never fully catch up (because its stationarity assumption limits its ultimate accuracy). Conversely, a more sophisticated non-stationary GP, such as one with a treed partition or a locally adaptive kernel, might cross over earlier. Our results are specific to the DGP+ALC comparator and should not be interpreted as a universal comparison of "neural networks vs. Gaussian processes."

## Batch Acquisition {#sec-al-batch}

The active learning loop described in @sec-al-acquisition acquires one point per iteration. In practice, this is often impractical: if the simulator runs on a cluster with 32 cores, we want to submit 32 evaluations simultaneously rather than waiting for each one to finish before selecting the next. This is the *batch acquisition* problem: select $B$ points per iteration instead of one.

The naive approach is *top-$B$ disagreement*: compute the ensemble disagreement at every candidate point, then select the $B$ candidates with the highest scores. This is fast and simple but suffers from a well-known failure mode, *clustering*. When the disagreement surface has a single dominant peak (as it often does near a ridge or boundary), the top-$B$ candidates all cluster in the same narrow region. Instead of $B$ informative points spread across the input space, we get $B$ near-duplicates that provide the same information as a single evaluation. The marginal value of the second through $B$-th points in the cluster is negligible.

This clustering problem is not unique to GBC-AL, GP-based batch acquisition faces the same challenge, and the solutions are analogous. The GP literature has developed several approaches: the *constant liar* heuristic (equivalent to our kriging believer), *local penalization* [@gonzalez2016batch], and *determinantal point processes* (DPPs) that enforce diversity through a kernel-based repulsion mechanism. The GBC equivalents are simpler because the ensemble disagreement is a scalar function that can be penalized directly, without the matrix operations required by DPPs.

The solution is to promote *diversity* within each batch. The core idea is to ensure that the selected points are spread across the input space, covering multiple regions of high disagreement rather than clustering in one. After selecting the first point $x_1^*$ as the global maximizer of the disagreement, we penalize candidates that are too close to $x_1^*$ before selecting the second point, and so on. One effective strategy is the *kriging believer* heuristic adapted to the GBC setting: after selecting $x_j^*$, we impute a "fantasy" observation $y_j = \bar{\tilde{y}}(x_j^*)$ (the ensemble mean at the selected point), add it to the training set, and recompute the disagreement. Because the fantasy observation reduces disagreement near $x_j^*$, the next selected point will be pushed toward a different high-disagreement region. This approach requires $B$ forward passes through the ensemble but avoids the expensive retraining that a fully sequential acquisition would require.

A simpler alternative is *distance-penalized selection*: after selecting $x_j^*$, multiply the disagreement scores by a penalty factor $\prod_{i=1}^{j-1}(1 - \exp(-\|x - x_i^*\|^2 / 2\ell^2))$, where $\ell$ is a length-scale parameter controlling the exclusion zone around previously selected points. This is faster than the kriging-believer approach because it requires no imputation, but the choice of $\ell$ introduces a new hyperparameter.

Why does naive top-$B$ cluster? The root cause is that the disagreement surface is typically smooth (because each ensemble member's prediction is a smooth function of $x$), so the maximum of the disagreement surface is surrounded by a neighborhood of high disagreement. When $B$ is small relative to the candidate pool, the top-$B$ candidates are all drawn from this neighborhood. On the LGBB benchmark, for example, the disagreement surface often has its maximum along the Mach $= 1$ ridge, and naive top-10 selection would place all 10 points within a narrow band along the ridge, leaving the rest of the domain unsampled. The distance-penalized approach avoids this by creating a "repulsion zone" around each selected point, forcing subsequent selections to explore different regions.

The choice of batch size $B$ itself involves a tradeoff. Larger $B$ reduces the number of retraining iterations (which is expensive for GBC, as discussed in @sec-al-cost) but also reduces the quality of the acquisition signal, because the ensemble disagreement computed at the start of a batch iteration does not account for the information gained from the first $B - 1$ points in the batch. In the extreme, $B = n_{\text{total}} - n_0$ reduces active learning to a single round of batch selection from the initial design, essentially a one-shot experimental design problem, which sacrifices the sequential nature of active learning entirely.

In our experiments, we used batch sizes of $B = 1$ for all benchmarks to match the comparison protocol of @sauer2023active. However, for practitioners deploying GBC-AL on large computing clusters, batch acquisition is essential. We recommend the distance-penalized approach with $\ell$ set to the median nearest-neighbor distance in the current training set, which adapts naturally as the design fills in.

A practical compromise is $B \in [5, 20]$ with distance-penalized selection, balancing parallelism against sequential information gain.
For very expensive simulators (hours per evaluation), even $B = 2$ or $B = 3$ can provide a meaningful speedup by utilizing multiple cores simultaneously, while the information loss from batch acquisition is minimal at such small batch sizes.

The batch acquisition problem also interacts with the retraining schedule. In the sequential ($B = 1$) setting, the ensemble is retrained from scratch after each acquisition, which is expensive. In the batch setting, the ensemble is retrained only once per batch of $B$ acquisitions, reducing the total number of retraining episodes by a factor of $B$. This means that batch acquisition is not just a parallelism convenience, it also reduces the total computational cost of the active learning loop. For example, with $B = 10$ and 300 total acquisitions, the number of retraining episodes drops from 300 to 30, reducing total GBC-AL retraining time from roughly 30 hours to 3 hours (plus the parallelized simulator time for $B$ simultaneous evaluations).

The tradeoff is that the acquisition signal computed at the start of each batch becomes increasingly stale as points are selected within the batch, particularly for the diversity-promoting strategies (kriging believer, distance penalization) that modify the disagreement surface within the batch. The staleness effect is most pronounced when the batch contains a point near a sharp feature (like the LGBB ridge): after evaluating this point, the function value at the ridge is now known, and the disagreement should decrease dramatically in that neighborhood. But the within-batch diversity strategies do not account for this information gain because they do not retrain the ensemble.

A related approach is *asynchronous* batch acquisition, where new simulator evaluations arrive one at a time and the ensemble is updated (or the acquisition is recomputed) as each result becomes available. This is the natural operating mode for simulators running on shared computing clusters, where evaluations finish at unpredictable times. The asynchronous setting introduces the additional challenge of selecting the next evaluation point while previous evaluations are still running. One approach is to treat the pending evaluations as "fantasy" observations with imputed values (the ensemble mean) and compute the disagreement on the augmented dataset. This is equivalent to the kriging believer approach applied to pending evaluations rather than within-batch selections.


## Computational Cost Analysis {#sec-al-cost}

Active learning's appeal lies in reducing the number of expensive simulator evaluations. But the surrogate retraining cost at each iteration is also a consideration, particularly for methods like GBC that require training neural networks from scratch.

The dominant cost in each GBC-AL iteration is retraining the $K$ ensemble members. Unlike GPs, where the posterior update after adding one point can be computed in $O(n^2)$ via rank-one Cholesky updates, the IQN ensemble must be retrained from scratch at each iteration. Why from scratch? The natural alternative, *warm-starting* from the previous iteration's weights, fails because of distribution shift. When a new training point is added, the loss landscape changes, and the previous weights may be a local minimum of the old loss but not the new one. More importantly, warm-starting from the previous weights undermines the randomized-prior mechanism: the trainable component $g_{\phi_k}$ retains "memory" of the old training set, and the diversity injected by the frozen prior $g_{0,k}$ is partially cancelled by the warm-started trainable component even in regions where the new point provides no information. The result is that warm-started ensembles exhibit collapsed disagreement after a few iterations, losing the epistemic uncertainty signal that drives acquisition. We observed this empirically on the LGBB benchmark: warm-started GBC-AL degraded to performance comparable to random selection by iteration 50.

| Component | GBC-AL ($K=3$) | GP-AL (DGP+ALC) |
|--------|----------|---------|
| Surrogate retrain | $O(K \cdot n \cdot E)$ SGD steps | $O(n^3)$ Cholesky or $O(n^2)$ rank-1 update |
| Acquisition evaluation | $O(K \cdot n_{\text{cand}} \cdot L \cdot w^2)$ | $O(n_{\text{cand}} \cdot n^2)$ for ALC |
| Acquisition optimization | $O(n_{\text{cand}})$ argmax | $O(n_{\text{cand}})$ argmax |
| Total per iteration (asymptotic) | $O(K \cdot n \cdot E)$ | $O(n^3)$ full retrain or $O(n^2)$ update |

The wall-clock comparison at $n = 300$ is rough and reflects both algorithmic and hardware differences (GBC runs on GPU, GP on CPU). The asymptotic difference is what matters: GBC scales as $O(n)$ per SGD epoch (linear in training set size), while the GP scales as $O(n^3)$ for a full retrain or $O(n^2)$ for a rank-one update. For the benchmarks in this chapter ($n \leq 500$), the cubic cost is manageable, but at $n > 5{,}000$ the GP retrain becomes prohibitive while GBC remains feasible.

To put these costs in perspective: for the Satellite GRACE benchmark with 390 acquisition iterations, the total GBC-AL retraining time is approximately $390 \times 3 \times 2\text{ min} = 39$ hours on a single GPU (or 13 hours with parallelized ensemble training). The total simulator time for 390 evaluations at 86 seconds each is approximately 9.3 hours. So the surrogate retraining cost exceeds the simulator cost by a factor of four. This is unusual, for most computer experiment applications, the simulator cost dominates by orders of magnitude. The satellite benchmark is a moderately expensive simulator; for truly expensive simulators (hours per evaluation), the retraining cost is negligible.

The warm-starting failure deserves further explanation because it is counterintuitive. In standard deep learning, warm-starting (also called transfer learning or fine-tuning) is a widely successful technique: initialize from a pretrained model and continue training on new data. Why does it fail for active learning with randomized priors? The answer lies in the interaction between the trainable and frozen components. After training on $\mathcal{D}_n$, the trainable component $g_{\phi_k}$ has learned to predict $f(x)$ in data-rich regions by effectively "subtracting out" the frozen prior's contribution: $g_{\phi_k}(\tau, x) \approx f(x) - \alpha \, g_{0,k}(\tau, x)$ where data are available. When we warm-start from these weights and add a new point $x_{n+1}$, the trainable component already has a large magnitude in the neighborhood of $x_{n+1}$ (from fitting the function at nearby points), and this magnitude partially cancels the frozen prior even if $x_{n+1}$ is in a previously data-sparse region. The ensemble members therefore agree more than they should, and the disagreement signal is suppressed. Retraining from scratch with fresh random initialization restores the proper balance: the trainable component starts small and can only grow where the data support it.

The practical implication is that GBC-AL has higher constant-factor cost (training a neural network from scratch takes thousands of SGD steps) but better asymptotic scaling. For problems where the total acquisition budget is small ($n < 500$) and individual simulator evaluations are very expensive (hours per run), the surrogate retraining cost is negligible compared to the simulator cost and the choice should be driven by prediction quality, not computational overhead. For problems with moderate simulator cost (seconds to minutes) and large budgets ($n > 1{,}000$), GBC's linear scaling becomes a decisive advantage.

One practical mitigation strategy is to reduce the number of training epochs as the training set grows. With $n = 50$ points, 3,000 epochs may be needed for convergence; with $n = 300$ points, the same level of fit may be achieved in 1,000 epochs because each epoch presents more data. An adaptive epoch schedule that scales inversely with $\sqrt{n}$ can reduce the total retraining time by a factor of two or more without degrading surrogate quality.

A second mitigation strategy is *parallelized ensemble training*. The $K$ ensemble members are trained independently on the same data, making them embarrassingly parallel. On a modern GPU with sufficient memory, all three members can be trained simultaneously as a single batched model, reducing wall-clock time by a factor of $K$. This is a straightforward implementation optimization that does not affect the acquisition signal quality. In our experiments on a single NVIDIA A100, training three ensemble members in parallel took approximately the same wall-clock time as training a single model, because the GPU had sufficient capacity to handle the combined batch.

The parallelized ensemble can be achieved without creating separate model objects. Instead, one can create a single "fat" network whose hidden layers are $K$ times wider, with block-diagonal weight matrices that prevent information flow between ensemble members. The forward pass computes all $K$ predictions simultaneously, and the loss is the sum of the individual pinball losses. This approach has the same computational cost as a single forward pass through a wider network and avoids the overhead of managing $K$ separate model objects and optimizers. The frozen prior components are handled similarly: the prior network's weights occupy the same block-diagonal structure and are excluded from the optimizer's parameter list.

A third consideration is the *candidate pool size*. Computing the ensemble disagreement at $n_{\text{cand}}$ candidate points requires $K \cdot n_{\text{cand}}$ forward passes through the network. For $n_{\text{cand}} = 10{,}000$ and $K = 3$, this is fast (milliseconds on a GPU). But for very high-dimensional problems where the candidate pool must be large to adequately cover the input space, the forward-pass cost can become significant. In such cases, a two-stage strategy, evaluate disagreement on a coarse random subset, then refine with a dense grid around the top candidates, can reduce the cost without sacrificing acquisition quality.


## Choosing Between GBC-AL and GP-AL {#sec-al-guidance}

The benchmarks suggest clear guidelines for practitioners. Favor GBC-AL when the response surface has discontinuities, ridges, or other non-smooth features (as in the Rocket LGBB), when the acquisition budget is moderate relative to the input dimension, the "expensive simulator, limited budget" regime where each new point matters most (SatMini, 7D Proxy, Satellite early phase), or when retraining cost matters, since GBC retrains in $O(n)$ time via SGD while the GP requires $O(n^3)$ Cholesky factorizations. Favor GP-AL instead when the response surface is smooth and the kernel assumptions are well-matched to the function (2D Exponential, Satellite late phase), when the total budget is large enough for the GP to identify kernel hyperparameters reliably, or when dimension is low ($d \leq 4$) and $n$ is small ($n < 200$), where the GP's prior provides regularization that the neural network cannot replicate.

The crossover in the satellite benchmark is particularly informative: GBC-AL is not uniformly better or worse than GP-AL. The two methods have complementary strengths, and the choice should be guided by the expected properties of the response surface and the available budget. For expensive simulators where each evaluation costs hours or days, the early-phase advantage of GBC-AL may be decisive, a 16% improvement at $n = 200$ could save weeks of compute time.

A useful rule of thumb emerges from our experiments: if the practitioner expects the response surface to be smooth and the total acquisition budget will exceed roughly 40 points per input dimension (i.e., $n > 40d$), the GP is likely to eventually overtake GBC-AL. Below this threshold, GBC-AL's faster acquisition signal and lack of smoothness bias make it the safer default. When the response surface is known to have discontinuities or sharp features, GBC-AL is preferred regardless of budget, the $2.95\times$ advantage on LGBB shows that no amount of additional data will help the GP overcome its smoothness bias on a non-smooth function.

In many practical settings, the practitioner does not know in advance whether the response surface is smooth. An initial exploratory phase can help: run $n_0$ points via LHS, fit both a GP and an IQN to the initial data, and compare their leave-one-out cross-validation errors. If the GP achieves substantially better LOO error, the response surface is likely smooth in the neighborhood of the data and GP-AL is favored. If the IQN matches or exceeds the GP, the surface may have non-smooth features and GBC-AL is favored. This diagnostic is not foolproof, the initial LHS design may miss a discontinuity that appears only in a specific region of the input space, but it provides a data-driven starting point for the method selection decision.

Domain knowledge is often more informative than any diagnostic. If the simulator models a physical process with known phase transitions, shock waves, or regime changes (as in the LGBB), the response surface is likely non-smooth and GBC-AL should be preferred. If the simulator models a smooth physical process (heat diffusion, elastic deformation, laminar flow), the response surface is likely smooth and the GP's kernel prior will be well-matched. The practitioner's understanding of the underlying physics should always inform the choice of surrogate and acquisition criterion.

A final consideration is *scalability beyond $d = 7$*. All benchmarks in this chapter have $d \leq 7$ input dimensions. As $d$ grows, both methods face the curse of dimensionality: the number of points needed to cover the input space grows exponentially with $d$. However, the two methods are affected differently. The GP's cubic cost $O(n^3)$ becomes prohibitive at large $n$, limiting the total number of evaluations that can be used even when the simulator is fast. The IQN's linear cost $O(n)$ allows much larger training sets, potentially offsetting the curse of dimensionality by sheer volume of data. For $d > 10$, where standard GP methods become impractical without sparse variational approximations [@titsias2009variational; @hensman2013gaussian], GBC-AL may be the only feasible neural-network-based active learning approach. Sparse GPs reduce the cost to $O(nm^2)$ where $m$ is the number of inducing points, but they introduce additional approximation error and require careful placement of the inducing points. We have not tested the high-dimensional regime and cannot make definitive claims, but the asymptotic scaling argument strongly favors GBC-AL in high dimensions with large budgets.

Another consideration is the *dimensionality of the output*. This chapter has focused on scalar-output simulators ($y \in \mathbb{R}$), but many simulators produce vector outputs ($y \in \mathbb{R}^p$). The LGBB simulator, for instance, produces six aerodynamic coefficients (we used only the side-force coefficient). GP surrogates for vector outputs require either $p$ independent GPs (ignoring correlations) or a multi-output GP (which adds substantial complexity). The IQN can be extended to vector outputs by training $p$ separate IQN heads that share a common feature extractor, or by treating each output dimension as a separate active learning problem with shared acquisitions. The ensemble disagreement criterion generalizes naturally: compute the disagreement for each output dimension and combine them (e.g., by summing or taking the maximum). This is an area where GBC-AL has a natural architectural advantage over GP-AL.

We emphasize that the guidelines in this section are derived from six benchmarks in $d \leq 7$ dimensions with total budgets of $n \leq 500$. The conclusions may not generalize to very high dimensions ($d > 20$), very large budgets ($n > 5{,}000$), or response surfaces with qualitatively different structure (e.g., periodic functions, highly anisotropic surfaces, or functions with many local optima). As the GBC framework matures and more benchmarks are published, these guidelines will need to be updated. For now, they represent our best understanding of the GBC-AL/GP-AL tradeoff based on the available evidence.

There is also the question of *robustness to misspecification*. If the practitioner believes the function is smooth and chooses GP-AL, but the function turns out to have a sharp feature in an unexplored region, the GP will oversmooth this feature and the ALC criterion will not direct acquisitions toward it (because the GP's posterior variance does not account for model misspecification). GBC-AL is more robust in this sense: because the IQN has no smoothness assumption, it cannot be "misspecified" in the same way, and the ensemble disagreement will naturally increase near any feature that the ensemble members learn at different rates. This robustness argument favors GBC-AL as a default choice when the response surface is unknown, even if the total budget exceeds the $40d$ threshold.

The robustness advantage is particularly relevant for *exploratory* studies, where the practitioner is building a surrogate for a simulator that has not been extensively studied. In this setting, the response surface may contain unexpected features, regime transitions, boundary layers, or resonances, that are not anticipated by the practitioner's prior knowledge. GP-AL with a misspecified kernel will systematically avoid these features because the posterior variance does not signal their presence. GBC-AL, through the ensemble disagreement, will naturally detect these features when different ensemble members learn them at different rates, directing acquisitions toward them even without prior knowledge of their existence. This "model-free discovery" capability is a strong practical argument for GBC-AL in scientific computing applications.

## Open Problems

Several fundamental questions about GBC-based active learning remain unresolved.

*Regret bounds.* There is currently no formal optimality theory for IQN-based acquisition functions analogous to the IMSE/ALC theory for GPs [@gramacy2020surrogates; @sacks1989design]. How fast should GBC-AL converge theoretically? The difficulty is that the ensemble disagreement is a frequentist quantity (standard deviation of point estimates across models) rather than a Bayesian posterior quantity, so the information-theoretic tools used to analyze ALC do not directly apply. For GPs, the IMSE under ALC is known to converge at the rate $O((\log n / n)^{2\nu/d})$ for a Mat\'ern-$\nu$ kernel in $d$ dimensions, matching the optimal rate for nonparametric regression in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces. Establishing convergence rates for ensemble-disagreement acquisition under neural network surrogates, even in the simplest fixed-design setting, would connect GBC-AL to the broader literature on sequential experimental design. A natural conjecture is that the regret scales as $O(n^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)})$ for $\beta$-smooth functions, matching the minimax rate for nonparametric regression, but no proof exists. The main obstacle is that the ensemble disagreement depends on the training algorithm (SGD), the network architecture, and the prior scale in ways that are difficult to analyze theoretically.

*Adaptive prior scale.* The prior scale $\alpha$ in @eq-randprior is fixed throughout the active learning loop. Should it adapt? At the start of the loop, when $n$ is small and the trainable component is poorly trained, a large $\alpha$ ensures that the ensemble members are diverse and the acquisition signal is informative. As $n$ grows and the trainable component improves, a smaller $\alpha$ would allow the members to agree more quickly in data-rich regions, sharpening the acquisition signal. We experimented with linear decay schedules for $\alpha$ but found no consistent improvement over a fixed $\alpha = 0.5$. A principled schedule, perhaps tied to the ensemble's training loss or an estimate of the remaining epistemic uncertainty, remains an open question. One could draw an analogy with the "temperature" parameter in simulated annealing or the exploration parameter in $\varepsilon$-greedy reinforcement learning: start with high exploration (large $\alpha$) and gradually reduce it as the surrogate improves. But unlike those settings, where the exploration-exploitation tradeoff is well-understood theoretically, there is no formal theory guiding the decay of $\alpha$ in the active learning context.

*Thompson sampling connection.* In the reinforcement learning literature, randomized-prior ensembles were introduced as a practical approximation to Thompson sampling [@thompson1933likelihood; @osband2018randomized]. Thompson sampling selects actions by sampling from the posterior and acting greedily with respect to the sample. The randomized-prior ensemble approximates this by treating each member as a "posterior sample" and selecting the action that is optimal under a randomly chosen member. Can this connection be made formal in the active learning setting? Specifically, instead of selecting the point of maximum *disagreement*, one could select the point that a randomly chosen ensemble member judges most uncertain. This would implement approximate Thompson sampling for sequential design. Whether this exploration strategy offers advantages over the deterministic maximum-disagreement criterion is unexplored.

*Multi-fidelity active learning.* Many engineering simulators can be run at different fidelity levels, a coarse mesh that runs in seconds and a fine mesh that runs in hours. GP-based multi-fidelity methods use auto-regressive kernels to model the correlation between fidelities [@kennedy2000predicting]. Extending GBC-AL to the multi-fidelity setting would require an ensemble that captures uncertainty across fidelity levels, directing cheap low-fidelity evaluations to regions of broad uncertainty and expensive high-fidelity evaluations to regions where the fidelity gap matters most. The IQN's architecture can naturally accommodate a fidelity indicator as an additional input, but designing an acquisition criterion that trades off evaluation cost against information gain remains open.

*Calibration of the disagreement signal.* The ensemble disagreement is an *ordinal* uncertainty measure: we can rank candidate points by their disagreement, but we cannot interpret the disagreement value as a probability or a predicted error magnitude. Can the disagreement be calibrated so that it provides meaningful uncertainty quantification in addition to acquisition guidance? One approach is to use conformal prediction (@sec-calibration) on the disagreement values themselves, computing empirical coverage rates on held-out data and adjusting the disagreement scale accordingly. Another approach is to train a separate calibration model that maps disagreement values to predicted RMSE, using past active learning iterations as training data. Neither approach has been tested systematically.

*Stopping criteria.* When should active learning stop? With a fixed budget, the answer is predetermined. But for applications where simulator time is not strictly rationed, a natural stopping criterion would be "stop when the surrogate is good enough." The ensemble disagreement provides a candidate metric: stop when the maximum disagreement over the candidate pool drops below a threshold. The challenge is choosing the threshold, which depends on the scale of the response and the desired accuracy. An alternative is to monitor the improvement in RMSE (estimated by leave-one-out cross-validation) and stop when the improvement per acquisition drops below a threshold. This "diminishing returns" criterion is more interpretable but requires computing LOO predictions at each iteration, which adds computational cost.



## Summary

This chapter has developed a complete framework for active learning with GBC surrogates. The components are: (1) a randomized-prior ensemble that injects structural diversity through frozen random networks, producing an epistemic uncertainty signal that persists regardless of training set size; (2) an ensemble disagreement acquisition function that selects points where the ensemble members disagree most, directing simulator evaluations toward regions of high uncertainty; and (3) a retraining-from-scratch protocol that maintains the integrity of the prior perturbation across active learning iterations.

The benchmark results reveal a clear division of labor between GBC-AL and GP-AL. GBC-AL excels when the response surface has non-smooth features (LGBB, $2.95\times$ improvement), when the acquisition budget is moderate relative to the input dimension (SatMini and 7D Proxy, 33--38% improvement), or when the GP's kernel hyperparameters cannot be reliably estimated from the available data. GP-AL excels on smooth surfaces with large budgets (Satellite at $n = 500$, 23% improvement) or low dimensions with small budgets (2D Exponential, 21% improvement). The crossover phenomenon observed on the Satellite benchmark is the most practically informative result: it demonstrates that the choice between GBC-AL and GP-AL depends not just on the response surface properties but on the interaction between those properties and the available budget.

For practitioners, we offer a concrete rule of thumb: use GBC-AL as the default for non-smooth problems at any budget, and for smooth problems when $n < 40d$. Switch to GP-AL on smooth problems when $n > 40d$ and the kernel is well-specified. When in doubt, the initial LOO diagnostic described in @sec-al-guidance provides a data-driven method selection criterion.

The active learning framework developed in this chapter extends to several downstream applications. In @sec-bayesian-inference, we will see how the IQN can be used for Bayesian posterior inference rather than surrogate modeling, and the ensemble disagreement criterion can be adapted to direct data collection toward regions of the parameter space where the posterior is most uncertain. In @sec-causal, the causal inference framework builds on the surrogate modeling ideas from this chapter, and active data collection for causal effect estimation is a natural application of the GBC-AL methodology. The insight that randomized-prior ensembles provide a practical and effective epistemic uncertainty signal for neural-network-based surrogates carries over to all of these downstream applications.

The neural network's lack of a built-in uncertainty model is not a fatal flaw, it can be remedied through ensemble methods that inject and measure structural diversity. The randomized-prior approach is one specific instantiation of this idea, but the principle is general: any mechanism that produces diverse predictions in data-sparse regions and convergent predictions in data-rich regions can serve as an epistemic uncertainty signal for active learning. Future work may discover better mechanisms than frozen random priors, but the framework, ensemble diversity as a proxy for epistemic uncertainty, disagreement as an acquisition function, will remain applicable.

The practical takeaway is simple. Take your existing IQN code, duplicate it $K = 3$ times, add a frozen random network to each copy, train all three on the same data, compute the standard deviation of their predictions at each candidate point, and select the candidate with the highest standard deviation. Retrain from scratch after each acquisition. That is the entire algorithm. The complexity is in understanding *why* it works (which we have discussed at length in this chapter) and *when* it works (which the benchmarks have mapped out), not in the implementation itself.

The exercises that follow are designed to help the reader build intuition about the key components of GBC-AL: the randomized-prior mechanism, the ensemble disagreement criterion, the effect of hyperparameters like $\alpha$ and $K$, and the practical challenges of batch acquisition and warm-starting. We recommend starting with @exr-al-1d, which implements the full active learning loop in one dimension, before attempting the higher-dimensional exercises.

We close with a remark on the relationship between this chapter and the broader computer experiments literature. Sequential design has been studied for over three decades, beginning with the pioneering work of @sacks1989design and @cohn1994neural. The GP has been the dominant surrogate model throughout this period, and the ALC/IMSPE acquisition criteria have been refined through extensive theoretical and empirical work [@gramacy2020surrogates]. GBC-AL is a newcomer to this field, and its theoretical foundations are less developed than those of GP-based methods. What the benchmarks in this chapter demonstrate is that GBC-AL is a *competitive* alternative to GP-based active learning, with distinct advantages on non-smooth problems and at moderate sample sizes. Whether these advantages extend to the broader class of problems encountered in scientific computing remains to be seen, and we encourage practitioners to test both approaches on their specific applications.
