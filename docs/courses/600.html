What this course is

The foundations of statistics and machine learning: probability, estimation, regression, decision making under uncertainty, model selection, and neural networks. All work is in R.

Given that AI assistants can write the code, fit the models, make the plots, and draft the report, and they can do all of that faster and often better than a first-year graduate student, there is no point in grading you on producing those things. Producing them is not the same as understanding them. You still have to know what your code is doing: what each piece computes, why it is there, and what would break if it were wrong, even when an assistant wrote it.

You will be graded on understanding and judgment. Understanding means you can explain what the analysis is doing, what the numbers mean, and what would change if a modelling choice were different, including when an assistant produced the work. Judgment means choosing what problem is worth solving. Deciding what being wrong costs, and in which direction. Noticing when an analysis is wrong, including when it is your own and including when it came out of a machine. Defending a choice out loud when someone pushes back on it.

I cannot certify either in one sitting. I will ask you to explain the work and then to defend a choice. If you cannot explain what the analysis is doing, I have no reason to trust the choice. That is what class is for.

Using AI in this course

You are expected to use AI assistants. Not permitted: expected. They will write code, explain concepts, and draft analyses for you, and that is fine, because nothing in your grade depends on producing code or text an assistant can produce. Your grade depends on whether you understand that output and whether the choices behind it are defensible.

The rules are short:

  1. Use whatever tools you want. There is no approved list.
  2. Disclose what you used and for what, in a short appendix on submitted work. No penalty attaches to the disclosure. A team that used an assistant for everything and can defend all of it gets full marks.
  3. You are responsible for everything you submit, including the parts you did not write. “The assistant produced that” is not a defense; it is a description of how the error got in.
  4. Three assignments require you to use an assistant and then take its work apart. The AI’s output is the input. Your job is to find what it got wrong.

Set up the course tutor in week 1. It is a version of an assistant configured for this course: it answers R questions directly, asks you a question back before explaining a concept, and refuses to answer “which model is better?” until you tell it what decision the model supports. Setup instructions will be posted in week 1. Ten minutes, once.

What you will be able to do by December

Choose and fit an appropriate model for a stated decision, in R, and say why that model and not a simpler or more complex one. State what being wrong costs, in both directions, and set thresholds and choose loss functions accordingly rather than by default. Quantify uncertainty and say what it means for the decision at hand, rather than reporting an interval and moving on. Diagnose a defective analysis from its output: name the defect, point at the evidence, and predict which way the corrected number moves. Establish where data came from and whether it is what it claims to be. Defend an analytical choice under live questioning, and change your mind out loud when the questioning is right.

How a week works

Before class, about 90 minutes. Read the module notes, work the material, and submit that week’s concept check: a short notebook with three or four auto-graded exercises and one written question. It is low-stakes and its job is to make sure you arrive able to participate. Ask the tutor anything you got stuck on.

In class, one block.

  • Live analysis, 25 minutes. I analyse a real, messy dataset on screen with an assistant open beside me, and I hit dead ends on purpose, because watching someone competent be wrong and recover is the part you cannot get from a tutor.
  • Studio, 75 to 90 minutes. Your team gets a dataset and a problem, usually an analysis that is already wrong in a way that is not obvious. You work it with whatever tools you like while I move between teams and ask hard questions.
  • Critique, 30 to 40 minutes. A team presents; the room takes the analytical choices apart. You will be on both sides of this.

After class. Your team adds one entry to its decision log: a choice you made, the alternative you rejected, and why.

Attendance is not separately graded, but 15% of your grade is participation observed in the room, and the practical and both oral checkpoints happen only in class. Missing studio is expensive and cannot be made up by reading.

Grading

Component Weight
Weekly concept checks (14 total, lowest 2 dropped) 15%
Studio participation and team work 15%

Midterm practical, in class, week 7 15%

Fourteen concept checks means eleven notebooks plus the three AI-critique assignments in weeks 5, 9 and 13, which replace that week’s notebook rather than adding to it. Your lowest two scores are dropped, which is your allowance for a bad week; there are no individual extensions on them for that reason.

Concept checks (15%). Auto-graded, due before class, no late credit because we discuss the answers in the studio.

Studio participation (15%). Scored on what I observe: whether you interrogate a result before accepting it, whether you can say why you chose an approach, and whether your team is actually working rather than dividing the assignment into four silent pieces. Not attendance, not talking the most.

Oral checkpoints (25%). Two conversations, both individually scored. The midterm viva is week 7, 8 minutes, one on one. Questions escalate: recall, then mechanism, then justification, then a challenge to something you just said. Being challenged is not a signal that you are wrong. Changing your mind for a good reason scores well; changing it because I pushed does not. The project defense is week 15, about 20 minutes with your team, but you are scored individually. Expect to be asked about parts of the project you did not personally build, including entries in the decision log written by someone else on your team.

You are graded on four things: whether you understand the work, including the parts an assistant produced; whether you can justify choices rather than describe them; whether your confidence matches your evidence; and how you handle being pushed. The full rubric is published in week 1, and there is an ungraded practice viva in week 4 so that the real one is not the first time.

Midterm practical (15%). Week 7, 60 minutes, in class. You get finished analyses that each contain one real defect, and for each you name the defect, point at the evidence in the output, and say which way the corrected number moves. You may use an AI assistant during the practical. That is not a concession. Pasting the handout into an assistant returns a list of six generic caveats, exactly one of which is the actual problem, and generic caveat lists score zero.

Final project (30%). Teams of 3 to 4, formed in week 4 after you have worked together in a few studios. Components: proposal (week 7), mid-build review (week 11), decision log (week 14), artifact (week 15), presentation (week 15). The defense is scored separately in the 25% oral bucket.

The proposal is graded harder than you expect and the artifact is graded on judgment rather than polish. A logistic regression that answers the decision beats a gradient-boosted ensemble that does not. Model sophistication, number of methods tried, and visual polish are not on the rubric.

There are no peer evaluations and no contribution percentages. The defense is individually scored, and that does the same job with evidence I observed myself.

Scores of each component are normalized to be out of 100. Grades will be posted on Canvas. Cut-offs: 97 (A+), 93 (A), 90 (A-), 87 (B+), 82 (B), 79 (B-), 77 (C+), 73 (C), 70 (C-), 67 (D+), 60 (D).

Policies

Late work. Concept checks are not accepted late, because we discuss them in class; the two dropped scores cover this. Project components lose credit per day late. Anything in the room (practical, vivas, defense) cannot be made up except through the process below.

Missing an in-class assessment. Tell me before it happens if you possibly can. There is a makeup version of the practical held back for exactly this. Vivas can be rescheduled. Do not simply not appear; that is the only version of this that becomes a real problem.

Accommodations. Students registered with Disability Services should contact me in the first two weeks so we can arrange things properly rather than hastily. This matters more than usual here because a quarter of the grade is oral. Accommodations for oral assessment are routine and include extended time, questions supplied in advance, a written component in place of a spoken one, and a separate quieter room. Ask; none of this is exceptional.

Academic integrity. Using AI is expected and disclosed, so the usual grey area does not arise. What remains prohibited is what has always been prohibited: submitting someone else’s work as your own, fabricating data or results, and misrepresenting what you did. Note the middle one. Inventing a number, a source, or an analysis you did not run is the serious offence in this course, and it is the one an assistant makes easiest.

Group work. Team projects are collaborative by design. Concept checks are individual: work with anyone you like, but the submission must be yours and you will be asked about it in the studio.

What to do in week 1

Install R and the Jupyter R kernel; instructions will be in the course repository README. Set up the course tutor (ten minutes). Read the oral-checkpoint rubric, posted with this syllabus. Knowing what you will be asked in week 7 changes how you read weeks 2 through 6. Start noticing datasets you have access to through work, research, or an organisation you are part of. The strongest projects use data the team had to negotiate for or assemble. The weakest use a clean file downloaded from a competition site. You will be choosing in week 7 and it is much easier if you start looking now.

One thing worth saying plainly

The most uncomfortable part of this course is being asked “why?” and not having an answer, in a room, out loud, while people wait. That happens to everyone here, including me, and it is the mechanism rather than a side effect. It is also survivable, which is what the ungraded practice viva in week 4 is for.

The second most uncomfortable part is that you will hand in work you did not fully understand, produced by a tool, and be asked to defend it. Better here than in a job.
