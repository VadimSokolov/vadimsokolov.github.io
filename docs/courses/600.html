What this course is

The foundations of statistics and machine learning: probability, estimation, regression, classification, decision making under uncertainty, model selection, and neural networks.

The term is in two parts. Weeks 1 through 7 are lectures and self-check labs, ending in an in-person midterm with no AI assistant. From week 8 the class flips: studio in the room, and the rest of your time is the project.

The grade is half midterm, half project. Of the midterm half, 70% is Understanding and 30% is Judgment. Of the project half, 50% is Understanding and 50% is Judgment. Across the course that is 60% Understanding, 40% Judgment.

Understanding means explaining what a computation or model does, interpreting its output in context, applying the basic principles without an assistant, and explaining the analysis your team produced. It also means knowing the basic concepts and being able to apply them. A correct number by itself is not enough. Judgment means the problem you choose, the alternative you reject, the cost of being wrong, and what would change the recommendation.

Given that AI assistants can write the code, fit the models, make the plots, and draft the report, I will not grade a take-home write-up, script, or report as a document.

Using AI in this course

You are expected to use AI assistants. Getting results from one is fine. I will not grade a take-home write-up, script, or report. Self-check labs score auto-graded computations and one written question. Everything else that counts is something I can still attach to you: the studio page in weeks 8 through 14, the midterm, and the project defense.

The rules are short:

  1. Use whatever tools you want. There is no approved list. The midterm is the exception: no AI assistant and no machine. That exam is how I check that you can apply the basic principles and concepts on your own.
  2. Disclose what you used and for what, in a short appendix on submitted work. No penalty attaches to the disclosure. A team that used an assistant for everything and can defend all of it gets full marks.
  3. You are responsible for everything you submit, including the parts you did not write. If you cannot explain it, that is on you.
  4. Three assignments require you to use an assistant and then check its work. The code will often run and the numbers will often be right. What you check is whether the assumptions are stated, whether the claims follow from what was computed, and whether the analysis answers the decision it was asked to support. Show how you checked.

Course tutor

The course tutor is an assistant configured for this course. It answers R questions directly, asks you a question back before explaining a concept, and will not answer “which model is better?” until you say what decision the model supports. Set it up once in week 1, about ten minutes.

Download tutor-instructions.md and course-context.md from Canvas. Then load them in whichever assistant you use.

  • Paid Claude: create a Project, paste tutor-instructions.md into the project instructions, and upload course-context.md as project knowledge. Use that project for the term.
  • Free Claude, ChatGPT, or any other assistant: start a new conversation, paste both files into the first message, and add the line “Use these as your instructions for this conversation.” Keep using that thread. Start a fresh one each week and paste the files again; long threads get worse.
  • Cursor, Claude Code, or a similar coding tool: put tutor-instructions.md in your course folder and set it as the tool’s instructions file so it applies when you work on course code.

Any assistant works. If you prefer a different one, paste the same two files into it. You can edit the instructions; it is a text file.

What you will be able to do by December

  • Apply the basic principles of probability, estimation, regression, and classification to a new problem without an assistant.
  • Choose and fit an appropriate model for a stated decision, in R, and say why that model and not a simpler or more complex one.
  • State what being wrong costs, in both directions, and set thresholds and choose loss functions accordingly rather than by default.
  • Quantify uncertainty and say what it means for the decision at hand, rather than reporting an interval and moving on.
  • Diagnose a defective analysis from its output: name the defect, point at the evidence, and predict which way the corrected number moves.
  • Establish where data came from and whether it is what it claims to be.
  • Evaluate a fitted solution against an AI risk framework: who is affected, in which direction errors hurt, and whether the data and the model support the use you are putting them to.
  • Defend an analytical choice under live questioning, and change your mind out loud when the questioning is right.

How a week works

Weeks 1 through 6. Before class, about 90 minutes on that week’s module: read the notes, work the material, and submit the self-check lab. The lab is a short notebook with three or four auto-graded exercises and one written question. Ask the tutor anything you got stuck on. In class the block is lecture and worked examples. There is no studio and no project yet.

Week 7. The seventh lab is due before class. In class, 90 minutes on paper for the midterm. After the exam, remaining class time is the project launch.

Weeks 8 through 14. Outside class you work on the project. In class, one block.

  • Live analysis, 25 minutes. I analyse a real, messy dataset on screen with an assistant open beside me, and I hit dead ends on purpose, because watching someone competent be wrong and recover is the part you cannot get from a tutor.
  • Studio, 75 to 90 minutes. Your team gets a dataset and a problem, usually an analysis that is already wrong in a way that is not obvious. You work it with whatever tools you like while I move between teams and ask hard questions.
  • Critique, 30 to 40 minutes. A team presents; the room takes the analytical choices apart. You will be on both sides of this.

After class. Your team adds one entry to its decision log: the decision, the alternative rejected, the evidence, and what would make you revisit the choice.

Attendance is not separately graded. There is no studio in weeks 1 through 7 or in week 15. The midterm and the defense happen only in class. Missing studio is expensive and cannot be made up by reading.

The same 0–3 scale is used on every assignment.

Understanding. 3: accurate mechanism, interpretation in context, and a correct prediction if a condition changes. 2: correct explanation, incomplete or needing a prompt. 1: repeats steps or output without saying why they work. 0: wrong, contradicted by the evidence, or cannot explain the submitted work.

Judgment. 3: names the decision and the loss, compares alternatives, calibrates the claim, and responds to a challenge for a stated reason. 2: the choice is defensible, but alternatives or consequences are thin. 1: a preference without a comparison or a decision. 0: no decision, no rationale, or keeps defending a claim the evidence contradicts.

I use the same five questions throughout. 1. What did you do? 2. What does this number mean? 3. Why does it support your claim? 4. What reasonable alternative did you reject? 5. What evidence would make you change your decision? The first three are mostly understanding. The last two are mostly judgment. A polished answer to 1 that cannot reach 3 is not understanding. A sophisticated model with no answer to 4 is not judgment.

Self-check labs. Seven notebooks, one for each of weeks 1 through 7. Due before class, no late credit, lowest two dropped, no individual extensions. Each notebook has three or four auto-graded computations and one written question. Lab 7 is a review of weeks 1 through 6, due before the midterm.

AI critiques. Three, in weeks 9, 11 and 13, counted in the project. Each requires the claim, the evidence against it, the corrected claim, and how the correction affects the decision.

Studio page. On each of the 7 studio weeks (8 through 14), one page before you leave, in this order: the decision; the finding; the evidence; the alternative you rejected; the strongest check that could have failed; what would change the decision.

Midterm. Week 7, 90 minutes, on paper. No AI assistant and no machine; notes and the textbook are fine. This is 40% of the course. You produce the analysis: choose the model, compute, interpret the result in context, and say what it does to the decision. A correct number is not enough. Of these 40 points, 25 are Understanding (mechanism, interpretation, prediction if a condition changes) and 15 are Judgment (the alternative you did not take and the consequence for the decision).

Defense. Eleven minutes per team, Wednesday 9 December, 7:30 to 10:15 pm. Slots posted in week 13, two minutes between teams. This is 25% of the course and the individual Understanding exam of the project. Each of you gets two short questions, including about work you did not personally build: one explanation or counterfactual from the project, and one from the common foundations, not only from your team’s chosen model. Week 8 includes an ungraded practice: I ask a few volunteers a question in this style in front of the room so December is not the first time.

Final project. Teams of 3 to 4, locked in week 8, after the midterm. Proposal due week 9 (a real decision, a stated loss, and a check on where the data came from). Mid-build in studio, week 11. Decision log and a five-minute video due week 14; you watch two assigned teams and name the weakest claim you saw. Artifact due week 15. These 15 points are Judgment: the problem, the alternatives, the cost of error, and what would change the recommendation. I do not grade the artifact as a polished document. A logistic regression that answers the decision beats a gradient-boosted ensemble that does not. Model sophistication, number of methods tried, and visual polish are not on the rubric. There are no peer evaluations and no contribution percentages.

The midterm half is how I see whether the foundations are yours. The project half is how I see whether you can use them with an assistant and a team. Team work is evidence about what the team produced. The midterm and the directed defense questions are how I score your understanding. If those conflict, for example a strong project and an unsuccessful defense, we have a second five-minute conversation. That is to resolve insufficient evidence, not to punish you.

If the team is not working, tell me by week 11. After that we can reduce your individual scope or reassign you. Waiting until the defense is too late. I will not ask the team to grade each other.

Scores of each component are normalized to be out of 100. Grades will be posted on Canvas. This course uses Mason’s graduate regular scale: A+, A, A-, B+, B, B-, C, F. Cut-offs: 97 (A+), 93 (A), 90 (A-), 87 (B+), 82 (B), 79 (B-), 70 (C). Below 70 is F.

Policies

Late work. Self-check labs are not accepted late, because the next lecture uses them; the two dropped scores cover this. Project components lose credit per day late. Anything in the room (midterm, defense) cannot be made up except through the process below.

Missing an in-class assessment. Tell me before it happens if you possibly can. There is a makeup version of the midterm held back for exactly this. A missed defense can be rescheduled. Do not simply not appear; that is the only version of this that becomes a real problem.

Accommodations. Students registered with Disability Services should contact me in the first two weeks so we can arrange things properly rather than hastily. This matters more than usual here because 25% of the grade is oral. Accommodations for oral assessment are routine and include extended time, questions supplied in advance, a written component in place of a spoken one, and a separate quieter room. Ask if you need them.

Academic integrity. Using AI is expected and disclosed, so the usual grey area does not arise. What remains prohibited is what has always been prohibited: submitting someone else’s work as your own, fabricating data or results, and misrepresenting what you did. Inventing a number, a source, or an analysis you did not run is the serious offence in this course, and it is the one an assistant makes easiest.

Group work. Team projects are collaborative by design. Self-check labs are individual: work with anyone you like, but the submission must be yours and you will see the same ideas on the midterm.

Recordings. Defenses are audio-recorded if you consent. The recording is so that a score you disagree with is something we can both go back and listen to. If you decline, the defense still happens and I score it from notes; there is then no recording to review later. Only I have access. Files live on Canvas and I delete them after the grade-appeal window for the term closes.

Project presentations are a required five-minute video, submitted on Canvas. They are visible to me and to the two teams assigned to review yours, not to the rest of the class. If you do not submit the video, that component is missing. Copies are deleted after the grade-appeal window closes.

What to do in week 1

Install R and the Jupyter R kernel from the README in the course folder. Set up the course tutor (see Course tutor). Read the defense rubric. Knowing what you will be asked in December changes how you work in studio. Start noticing datasets you have access to through work, research, or an organisation you are part of. The strongest projects use data the team had to negotiate for or assemble. The weakest use a clean file downloaded from a competition site. Teams form in week 8 and you will choose a dataset then; it is much easier if you start looking now.

Two things worth saying plainly

The most uncomfortable part of this course is being asked “why?” and not having an answer, in a room, out loud, while people wait. That happens to everyone here, including me. The ungraded practice in week 8 is there so the first time is not the graded defense.

You will encounter AI-produced work you do not initially understand. Your responsibility is to interrogate it before submitting and defending it. I would rather that happen here than at work.
