“Prediction is very difficult, especially about the future.” – attributed to Niels Bohr

The previous chapter developed GBC as a tool for Bayesian inference, estimating parameters \(\theta\) from data. We now address prediction: given observations \(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n\), characterize the distribution of a future observation \(Y_{n+1}\). Prediction is arguably the primary goal of statistics. As Efron (2020) observed, most of the practical success of modern machine learning, random forests, gradient boosting, deep nets, is driven by prediction, not parameter estimation.

The standard Bayesian predictive distribution integrates over the posterior: \[ p(y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, Z_{1:n}) = \int p(y_{n+1} \mid \theta, x_{n+1}) \, \pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n}) \, d\theta. \tag{11.1}\] This requires specifying a likelihood \(p(y \mid \theta,x)\) and a prior \(\pi(\theta)\), then computing the integral, either analytically or via MCMC. In high-dimensional settings, both specification and computation are challenging.

This chapter develops an alternative. Instead of densities and integrals, we learn the predictive quantile function directly: \[ Y_{n+1} = Q(\tau, \, x_{n+1}, \, S(Z_{1:n})), \quad \tau \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1), \] where \(S\) is a summary of the observed data. The noise outsourcing theorem (Chapter 4) guarantees that such a map exists; the de Finetti–Ressel representation guarantees that, under exchangeability, the implicit prior and likelihood needed for Eq. 11.1 also exist, we just never need to compute them.

We contrast this approach with conformal prediction (CP), the leading frequentist method for distribution-free prediction. Our central theoretical result is that CP and Bayesian prediction are fundamentally different: they disagree on four structural properties, and the set of distributions where they agree is topologically negligible. CP belongs to the Fisher–Dempster–Hill fiducial tradition, not to the Bayesian tradition.

The chapter is organized as follows. Section 11.1 sets up the prediction problem. Section 11.2 introduces conformal prediction and its coverage guarantee. Section 11.3 develops the four separations between CP and Bayes. Section 11.4 presents the generative Bayesian prediction framework via quantile transport. Section 11.5 establishes minimax convergence rates. Section 11.6 connects to prediction-powered inference. Section 11.8 gives an empirical comparison.

11.1 The Prediction Problem

We observe data \(Z_i = (X_i, Y_i)\) for \(i = 1, \ldots, n\), where \(X_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\) are covariates and \(Y_i \in \mathbb{R}\) is a scalar response. Given a new covariate \(x_{n+1}\), the goal is to characterize the full predictive distribution of \(Y_{n+1}\). A point forecast \(\hat{y}_{n+1}\) is not enough, we need uncertainty quantification.

Two paradigms provide prediction sets \(C(x_{n+1})\) satisfying a coverage guarantee of the form \(P(Y_{n+1} \in C(x_{n+1})) \geq 1 - \alpha\). The frequentist (conformal) approach constructs \(C\) so that the coverage guarantee holds marginally over both the training data and the test point, under the sole assumption that \((Z_1, \ldots, Z_n, Z_{n+1})\) are exchangeable; no model is assumed and the guarantee is distribution-free and finite-sample valid. The Bayesian (posterior predictive) approach specifies a likelihood \(p(y \mid \theta,x)\) and prior \(\pi(\theta)\), computes the posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n})\), and integrates: \[ p(y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, Z_{1:n}) = \int p(y_{n+1} \mid \theta, x_{n+1}) \, \pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n}) \, d\theta. \] The resulting \(1-\alpha\) credible set has conditional coverage: given the data \(Z_{1:n}\), the coverage holds pointwise for each realization of the training set.

These two paradigms look superficially similar, both produce prediction intervals, but they differ in important structural ways.

Prediction vs. estimation. Efron (2020) gives a clean illustration of the distinction. Consider the normal model \((y_1, \ldots, y_n \mid \theta) \sim N(\theta, 1)\). For estimating \(\theta\), the sample mean \(\bar{y}\) is 57% more efficient than the sample median \(\tilde{y}\): \[ \frac{\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{y} - \theta)^2]}{\mathbb{E}[(\bar{y} - \theta)^2]} = \frac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.57. \] But for predicting a new \(y_{n+1}\), the ratio is \[ \frac{\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{y} - y_{n+1})^2]}{\mathbb{E}[(\bar{y} - y_{n+1})^2]} = \frac{\pi/(2n) + 1}{1/n + 1} \approx 1.02 \quad \text{(for } n = 25\text{)}, \] and it approaches 1 as \(n \to \infty\). The mean still wins, but barely, because most of the prediction error comes from the irreducible noise of \(y_{n+1}\) itself. Prediction is fundamentally different from estimation: the estimator matters less, and the distributional shape matters more.

11.2 Conformal Prediction

Conformal prediction, introduced by Vovk, Gammerman, and Shafer (2005), constructs prediction sets with finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantees. We describe the split conformal method, which is the most widely used variant.

Setup. Split the data into a training set \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}\) (indices \(1, \ldots, m\)) and a calibration set \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}\) (indices \(m+1, \ldots, n\)), where \(n_{\text{cal}} = n - m\).

Algorithm. Split conformal prediction proceeds in four steps:

  1. Fit: Train any regression model \(\hat{\mu}\) on \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}\) to produce point predictions \(\hat{\mu}(x)\).
  2. Score: Compute nonconformity scores on the calibration set: \[ R_i = |Y_i - \hat{\mu}(X_i)|, \quad i = m+1, \ldots, n. \]
  3. Quantile: Let \(\hat{q}\) be the \(\lceil (1-\alpha)(n_{\text{cal}} + 1) \rceil / n_{\text{cal}}\) empirical quantile of the scores \(\{R_{m+1}, \ldots, R_n\}\). If \(\lceil (1-\alpha)(n_{\text{cal}}+1) \rceil > n_{\text{cal}}\), set the threshold to \(+\infty\) (the coverage guarantee is then trivially satisfied).
  4. Predict: The prediction set for \(Y_{n+1}\) at covariate \(x_{n+1}\) is \[ C(x_{n+1}) = \{y : |y - \hat{\mu}(x_{n+1})| \leq \hat{q}\} = [\hat{\mu}(x_{n+1}) - \hat{q}, \; \hat{\mu}(x_{n+1}) + \hat{q}]. \tag{11.2}\]
TipTheorem 11.1: Conformal Coverage Guarantee

Let \(\hat{\mu}\) be any regression model trained on \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}\) only (not on the calibration or test data). If \((Z_1, \ldots, Z_n, Z_{n+1})\) are exchangeable, then for any distribution \(P\), \[ P\!\left(Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})\right) \geq 1 - \alpha, \tag{11.3}\] where \(C(x_{n+1})\) is the conformal prediction set in Eq. 11.2 and \(\hat{q}\) is computed from the calibration residuals \(\{R_i : i \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}\}\).

Proof sketch. Because \(\hat{\mu}\) is trained on \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}\) and not on the calibration or test points, it is a fixed (deterministic) function when we condition on \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}\). The calibration residuals \(\{R_i = |Y_i - \hat{\mu}(X_i)| : i \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}\}\) and the test residual \(R_{n+1} = |Y_{n+1} - \hat{\mu}(X_{n+1})|\) are therefore exchangeable (they inherit exchangeability from the original sequence \((Z_i)\)). Among \(n_{\text{cal}} + 1\) exchangeable random variables, the probability that any one of them exceeds the \((1-\alpha)\)-quantile of the others is at most \(\alpha\). This is a finite-sample result requiring no parametric assumptions on \(P\) beyond exchangeability. \(\square\)

Strengths. The guarantee in Eq. 11.3 holds for any model \(\hat{\mu}\), even a terrible one, and for any distribution \(P\). It is finite-sample (not asymptotic), and it requires no parametric assumptions: we get valid uncertainty quantification regardless of model misspecification.

Limitations. The coverage is marginal, averaged over both the calibration data and the test point. It says nothing about coverage conditional on a particular value of \(X_{n+1}\) or a particular realization of the training data. The prediction band in Eq. 11.2 has constant width \(2\hat{q}\) regardless of \(x\), it does not adapt to heteroscedasticity. (We discuss the adaptive variant in the conformalized quantile regression procedure below.)

11.2.1 Conformalized Quantile Regression

Romano, Patterson, and Candès (2019) proposed conformalized quantile regression (CQR), which combines the adaptivity of quantile regression with the coverage guarantee of conformal prediction. The idea is to use a quantile neural network as the base model, then apply conformal calibration:

  1. Train a quantile network to predict \(\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x)\) and \(\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)\) on \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}\).
  2. Compute conformity scores on \(\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}\): \[ R_i = \max\!\left(\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(X_i) - Y_i, \; Y_i - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(X_i)\right). \]
  3. Let \(\hat{Q}\) be the \((1-\alpha)(1 + 1/n_{\text{cal}})\) empirical quantile of these scores.
  4. The prediction interval is \[ C(x_{n+1}) = \left[\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x_{n+1}) - \hat{Q}, \; \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x_{n+1}) + \hat{Q}\right]. \tag{11.4}\]

The interval width \(\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x) - \hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) + 2\hat{Q}\) now varies with \(x\), inheriting the adaptive width properties of quantile regression. The conformal correction \(\hat{Q}\) ensures that the finite-sample coverage guarantee still holds.

Let us implement split conformal prediction on a simple heteroscedastic regression problem.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

np.random.seed(42)

# Heteroscedastic data: Y = sin(2*pi*x) + (0.3 + 0.7*x^2) * eps
n = 1000
X = np.random.uniform(0, 1, n)
sigma_x = 0.1 + 0.5 * X**2
Y = np.sin(2 * np.pi * X) + sigma_x * np.random.randn(n)

# Split: train / calibration / test
X_tr, X_rest, Y_tr, Y_rest = train_test_split(
    X, Y, test_size=0.5, random_state=0)
X_cal, X_test, Y_cal, Y_test = train_test_split(
    X_rest, Y_rest, test_size=0.5, random_state=0)

# --- Split Conformal ---
model = GradientBoostingRegressor(n_estimators=200, max_depth=3,
                                   random_state=0)
model.fit(X_tr.reshape(-1, 1), Y_tr)
resid_cal = np.abs(Y_cal - model.predict(X_cal.reshape(-1, 1)))
alpha = 0.10
# Correct finite-sample conformal quantile (Romano et al. 2019)
n_cal = len(resid_cal)
q_idx = int(np.ceil((1 - alpha) * (n_cal + 1))) - 1
q_idx = min(q_idx, n_cal - 1)  # handle edge case
q_hat = np.sort(resid_cal)[q_idx]

# --- CQR: train quantile models ---
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor as GBR
model_lo = GBR(n_estimators=200, max_depth=3, loss='quantile',
               alpha=alpha/2, random_state=0)
model_hi = GBR(n_estimators=200, max_depth=3, loss='quantile',
               alpha=1-alpha/2, random_state=0)
model_lo.fit(X_tr.reshape(-1, 1), Y_tr)
model_hi.fit(X_tr.reshape(-1, 1), Y_tr)

lo_cal = model_lo.predict(X_cal.reshape(-1, 1))
hi_cal = model_hi.predict(X_cal.reshape(-1, 1))
scores_cqr = np.maximum(lo_cal - Y_cal, Y_cal - hi_cal)
# Correct finite-sample conformal quantile (Romano et al. 2019)
n_cal_cqr = len(scores_cqr)
q_idx_cqr = int(np.ceil((1 - alpha) * (n_cal_cqr + 1))) - 1
q_idx_cqr = min(q_idx_cqr, n_cal_cqr - 1)  # handle edge case
Q_cqr = np.sort(scores_cqr)[q_idx_cqr]

# Plot
x_grid = np.linspace(0, 1, 300)
y_hat = model.predict(x_grid.reshape(-1, 1))
lo_grid = model_lo.predict(x_grid.reshape(-1, 1)) - Q_cqr
hi_grid = model_hi.predict(x_grid.reshape(-1, 1)) + Q_cqr

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax.scatter(X_test, Y_test, s=8, alpha=0.25, color='gray', label='Test data')
ax.fill_between(x_grid, y_hat - q_hat, y_hat + q_hat,
                alpha=0.2, color='steelblue', label='Conformal (constant width)')
ax.fill_between(x_grid, lo_grid, hi_grid,
                alpha=0.25, color='orange', label='CQR (adaptive width)')
ax.plot(x_grid, y_hat, 'k-', lw=1.5, label='$\\hat{\\mu}(x)$')
ax.set_xlabel('$x$'); ax.set_ylabel('$y$')
ax.legend(fontsize=8, loc='upper left')
ax.set_title(f'90% prediction intervals (n={n})')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

In Figure 11.1, the split conformal band (blue) has constant width everywhere, it cannot adapt to the heteroscedasticity. The CQR band (orange) widens on the right where the noise variance is larger and narrows on the left where it is smaller, all while maintaining the finite-sample coverage guarantee. Both methods have valid marginal coverage; CQR simply distributes the coverage more efficiently.

11.3 Conformal \(\neq\) Bayes: Four Separations

Is conformal prediction Bayesian? The coverage guarantee Eq. 11.3 looks like a probability statement about \(Y_{n+1}\), which might suggest a Bayesian interpretation. The answer is no. We now develop four structural separations showing that conformal prediction belongs to the fiducial tradition of Fisher, Dempster, and Hill (1968), not to the Bayesian tradition.

Many applied researchers use conformal prediction and Bayesian prediction interchangeably, reasoning that both produce prediction intervals and both invoke exchangeability. The four results below show that this conflation is a mistake. The two frameworks answer fundamentally different questions, conformal prediction asks “will my coverage be correct on average over future data?” while Bayesian prediction asks “what do I believe about this particular future observation given everything I have seen?” Understanding which question you are answering is essential for interpreting your prediction intervals and for deciding when to use which approach.

To understand the fiducial roots of conformal prediction, we begin with the Hill–Dempster \(A_{(n)}\) rule (Hill 1968). Given observations \(X_{(1)} < X_{(2)} < \cdots < X_{(n)}\) (the order statistics), the \(A_{(n)}\) rule assigns predictive probability \[ P_{A_{(n)}}(Y_{n+1} \in (X_{(i)}, X_{(i+1)})) = \frac{1}{n+1}, \quad i = 0, 1, \ldots, n, \tag{11.5}\] where \(X_{(0)} = -\infty\) and \(X_{(n+1)} = \infty\). That is, the next observation is equally likely to fall in any of the \(n+1\) intervals formed by the order statistics.

This rule is distribution-free and produces valid rank calibration: the rank of \(Y_{n+1}\) among \((X_1, \ldots, X_n, Y_{n+1})\) is uniformly distributed on \(\{1, 2, \ldots, n+1\}\) under exchangeability. Conformal prediction is precisely the modern, algorithmic descendant of \(A_{(n)}\), both exploit the uniform distribution of ranks under exchangeability.

The question is whether \(A_{(n)}\) can be given a Bayesian interpretation. The answer, as we now show, is negative in four distinct ways.

Separation 1: Non-extensionality. A Bayesian predictive distribution is extensional: it depends only on the conditional distribution \(P(Y \mid X)\), not on the marginal \(P(X)\). Formally, if two data-generating processes \(P\) and \(P'\) share the same conditional \(P(Y \mid X) = P'(Y \mid X)\) but differ in their marginal distributions \(P(X) \neq P'(X)\), then the Bayesian posterior predictive at \(x_{n+1}\) is the same under both.

Conformal prediction violates this property. The calibration quantile \(\hat{q}\) depends on the residuals \(\{R_i = |Y_i - \hat{\mu}(X_i)|\}\) evaluated at the calibration points. Changing the distribution of \(X\) (while keeping \(P(Y \mid X)\) fixed) changes which residuals are observed, which changes \(\hat{q}\), which changes the prediction set. In other words, conformal prediction sets for \(Y_{n+1}\) at a fixed \(x_{n+1}\) can change even when the conditional model \(P(Y \mid X = x_{n+1})\) is unchanged.

Example. Consider \(Y \mid X = x \sim N(0, 1)\) (the conditional model is the same for all \(x\)). If \(X \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\), the calibration points are spread evenly over \([0,1]\). If instead \(X\) is concentrated near 0, the calibration points cluster near 0 and the residuals reflect the model’s behavior near 0 only. For a nonlinear model \(\hat{\mu}\), these residual distributions can differ, leading to different conformal prediction sets at the same test point \(x_{n+1} = 0.5\).

A Bayesian would never change their prediction about \(Y_{n+1}\) based on the marginal distribution of the covariates, this follows directly from the factorization \(P(Y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, Z_{1:n}) \propto \int p(y_{n+1} \mid \theta, x_{n+1}) \pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n}) \, d\theta\).

In plain language, Separation 1 says that conformal prediction cares about where the training data came from in the covariate space, not just about the relationship between covariates and responses. If we change the distribution of house sizes in our training set (while keeping the price-given-size relationship identical), the conformal prediction interval for a specific house changes, but the Bayesian predictive distribution does not. This makes conformal prediction sensitive to sampling design in a way that Bayesian prediction is not.

Separation 2: Nonconglomerability. Conglomerability is a coherence property of probability. A probability measure \(P\) is conglomerable with respect to a partition \(\{B_j\}\) if \[ P(A \mid B_j) \geq \alpha \quad \text{for all } j \quad \Longrightarrow \quad P(A) \geq \alpha. \] That is, if an event has high conditional probability given every element of a partition, then it has high unconditional probability.

All \(\sigma\)-additive probability measures are conglomerable (\(\sigma\)-additivity implies conglomerability). Bayesian predictive distributions, being proper \(\sigma\)-additive measures, are automatically conglomerable.

The conformal coverage guarantee, by contrast, is marginal: \[ P(Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})) \geq 1 - \alpha. \] This averages over \(X_{n+1}\). The conditional coverage \(P(Y_{n+1} \in C(x) \mid X_{n+1} = x)\) can be much lower than \(1 - \alpha\) for some values of \(x\) and much higher for others. The marginal guarantee hides conditional miscoverage. Any attempt to interpret conformal’s rank calibration as a finitely additive probability (as in the \(A_{(n)}\) framework) produces a nonconglomerable measure, one that admits a Dutch book (sure loss) in the decision-theoretic sense of de Finetti.

The practical consequence of Separation 2 is immediate. Suppose we are predicting hospital readmission risk. Conformal prediction might achieve 90% marginal coverage across all patients, yet have only 70% coverage for elderly patients with multiple comorbidities and 98% coverage for healthy young adults. The marginal guarantee tells us nothing about coverage within any subgroup. A Bayesian predictive distribution, by contrast, provides valid conditional coverage for each patient profile (assuming the model is correctly specified). When decisions are made at the individual level, which patient to monitor, which loan to approve, the conditional guarantee is what matters, and conformal prediction cannot provide it without additional assumptions.

Separation 3: No \(\sigma\)-additive extension. A natural question is whether we can “fix” the conformal prediction framework by finding a proper (countably additive) probability measure on \(\mathbb{R}^\infty\) that reproduces the \(A_{(n)}\) rank calibration at every sample size.

The answer is no. The following result shows that the \(A_{(n)}\) calibration is inherently finitely additive:

TipProposition 11.1: No \(\sigma\)-Additive Extension

There exists no countably additive exchangeable probability measure on \(\mathbb{R}^\infty\) that, for every \(n\), assigns equal probability \(1/(n+1)\) to the rank of \(Y_{n+1}\) among \((X_1, \ldots, X_n, Y_{n+1})\) being each of \(\{1, 2, \ldots, n+1\}\).

Proof sketch. By de Finetti’s theorem, any \(\sigma\)-additive exchangeable measure on \(\mathbb{R}^\infty\) is a mixture of i.i.d. sequences. The \(A_{(n)}\) rule demands that, for every \(n\), the rank of \(Y_{n+1}\) among \((Z_1, \ldots, Z_n, Y_{n+1})\) is uniform on \(\{1, \ldots, n+1\}\) when conditioning on \(\sigma(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n)\) (the \(\sigma\)-algebra generated by the training data alone, excluding \(Y_{n+1}\)). For any i.i.d. continuous distribution \(F\) and any realization \(z_{1:n}\) with order statistics \(z_{(1)} \le \cdots \le z_{(n)}\), the conditional probability is \(P(\text{rank}(Y_{n+1}) = k \mid Z_{1:n} = z_{1:n}) = F(z_{(k)}) - F(z_{(k-1)})\) (with \(z_{(0)} = -\infty\), \(z_{(n+1)} = +\infty\)). This equals \(1/(n+1)\) only when the \(z_j\) happen to be exact \(F\)-quantiles, which fails for \(F\)-almost-all realizations. No de Finetti mixture can satisfy this \(\sigma(Z_{1:n})\)-conditional uniformity for all \(n\), establishing that no \(\sigma\)-additive exchangeable measure meets the \(A_{(n)}\) requirement. \(\square\)

This says that the conformal calibration property cannot arise from any Bayesian model, not from a well-chosen prior, not from a hierarchical model, not from a nonparametric Bayesian procedure. The calibration is a property of the procedure, not of any belief. There is no prior that would produce the exact rank-uniform calibration that conformal prediction achieves. Conformal prediction rests on finite additivity and the combinatorics of exchangeable ranks; Bayesian prediction rests on \(\sigma\)-additivity and the de Finetti mixture representation. These are genuinely different probability theories, and neither subsumes the other.

Separation 4: Meagre intersection. The previous separations show specific ways in which CP and Bayes differ. The fourth separation is topological and says that the two frameworks agree only on a negligible set.

Consider the space of all predictive kernels \(K : \mathcal{X}^n \times \mathcal{X} \to \Delta(\mathbb{R})\), where \(\Delta(\mathbb{R})\) is the set of probability measures on \(\mathbb{R}\). Let \(\mathcal{B}\) denote the set of predictive kernels arising from some Bayesian model (prior + likelihood), and let \(\mathcal{C}\) denote the set arising from some conformal procedure with valid coverage.

Under appropriate topologies (e.g., total variation on the predictive measures, uniform convergence over \(\mathcal{X}\)), one can show that the intersection \(\mathcal{B} \cap \mathcal{C}\) is Baire meagre (a countable union of nowhere-dense sets); the argument follows from the rigidity of the exchangeable rank condition and the smoothness of Bayesian updating (see the discussion in Vovk, Gammerman, and Shafer 2005, Ch. 8). Topologically, this means the set of predictive distributions that are both Bayesian and conformally calibrated is negligible, a zero-measure-like concept in a topological setting.

The meagre intersection result is the strongest of the four separations because it is a genericity claim, not just an existence claim: the two frameworks disagree almost everywhere. The few cases where they agree, such as the uniform distribution on a finite set, are mathematical curiosities, not representative of real prediction problems.

Practical implication. If you want marginal coverage, use conformal prediction. If you want a coherent belief state for sequential updating, decision-making, and conditional inference, use Bayesian prediction. Trying to get both from a single framework is generically impossible.

The four separations place conformal prediction squarely in the Fisher–Dempster–Hill fiducial tradition. Fisher (1930) introduced fiducial inference as a way to make probability statements about parameters without a prior. Dempster (1968) gave a probabilistic interpretation in the nonparametric setting, and Hill (1968) extended it to the predictive \(A_{(n)}\) framework.

Hannig et al. (2016) showed that generalized fiducial inference carries an implicit prior, an empirical Jeffreys prior based on observed information. Specifically, if there exists a reparametrization \(\phi = u(\theta)\) such that the normalized likelihood factors as a location family, the quantity \(|du/d\theta|\) plays the role of this implicit prior. But this implicit prior is finitely additive, not \(\sigma\)-additive, and the resulting predictive distribution inherits the nonconglomerability of Separation 2.

The GBC framework we develop in the next section is explicitly Bayesian: it produces \(\sigma\)-additive predictive distributions that are conglomerable, extensional, and composable for sequential updating. This does not mean that conformal prediction is useless, far from it. As we saw in Section 11.2.1, conformal calibration can be applied as a post-processing step on top of any prediction method, including GBC. Conformal calibration provides a frequentist safety net (marginal coverage) but does not, by itself, give you a Bayesian predictive distribution. The two are complementary tools: GBC provides the probabilistic model, and conformal calibration provides the distribution-free coverage guarantee.

11.4 Generative Bayesian Prediction via Quantile Transport

We now develop the generative approach to Bayesian prediction. We bypass the density-based integral in Eq. 11.1 and learn the predictive quantile function directly. The theoretical foundation rests on two pillars: the de Finetti–Ressel representation theorem, which guarantees that an implicit prior exists, and the noise outsourcing theorem, which provides the quantile transport map.

11.4.1 Exchangeability and the de Finetti–Ressel Representation

Exchangeability is the assumption that the order of observations does not matter for prediction. More precisely, a sequence \((Z_1, Z_2, \ldots)\) is exchangeable if its joint distribution is invariant under finite permutations of the indices: for every \(n\) and every permutation \(\sigma\) of \(\{1, \ldots, n\}\), \[ (Z_1, \ldots, Z_n) \stackrel{d}{=} (Z_{\sigma(1)}, \ldots, Z_{\sigma(n)}). \] This is a weaker assumption than independence, exchangeable random variables can be correlated, but it captures a powerful structural insight: if we have no reason to believe that observation \(i\) is fundamentally different from observation \(j\), then permuting the labels should not change our joint beliefs.

Why is exchangeability the right assumption for prediction? Consider predicting tomorrow’s stock return given the last \(n\) returns. We believe that the returns are generated by some common mechanism (the market), but we do not know what that mechanism is. If we had no additional information (such as time trends or regime changes), we would have no reason to distinguish the first return from the last. Exchangeability formalizes this symmetry. It says that all the information in the data relevant to prediction is captured by symmetric functions of the observations, counts, order statistics, sample moments, rather than by which observation came first.

By de Finetti’s representation theorem, there exists a random distribution \(\tilde{F}\) such that, conditionally on \(\tilde{F}\), the \(Z_i\) are i.i.d. from \(\tilde{F}\). The distribution of \(\tilde{F}\) is the implicit prior, we can act as if there exists a parameter \(\theta\), a likelihood \(p(z \mid \theta)\), and a prior \(\pi(\theta)\). This is the connection between exchangeability and Bayesian inference: you do not need to start with a prior. If you believe your data is exchangeable, de Finetti’s theorem tells you that a prior must exist, whether or not you can write it down. The prior is not a subjective choice imposed from outside; it is a mathematical consequence of the symmetry assumption.

The connection to prediction is immediate. For a Bayesian, the predictive distribution of \(Z_{n+1}\) given \(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n\) is obtained by integrating over the mixing measure: \(p(Z_{n+1} \mid Z_{1:n}) = \int p(Z_{n+1} \mid \tilde{F}) \, d\Pi(\tilde{F} \mid Z_{1:n})\), where \(\Pi(\cdot \mid Z_{1:n})\) is the posterior over mixing measures. The crucial point is that this predictive distribution depends on the data only through the sufficient statistic for the mixing measure. In the parametric case, this is the classical sufficient statistic; in the nonparametric case, it is the empirical distribution itself.

The critical result for prediction is predictive sufficiency: \[ p(Y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, Z_1, \ldots, Z_n) = p(Y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, S(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n)), \tag{11.6}\] where \(S(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n)\) is a predictive sufficient statistic that reduces the dimensionality of the conditioning set from \(n\) observations to a fixed-dimensional summary.

Ressel (1985) proved a refinement of de Finetti’s theorem. Under measurability conditions on \((\beta, t)\) (specifically, that \(t\) is a measurable map into a locally compact abelian semigroup with a separating dual), if the joint distribution of an exchangeable sequence factorizes as \[ P(Z_1 = z_1, \ldots, Z_n = z_n) = \prod_{j=1}^n \beta(z_j) \, \varphi\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^n t(z_j)\right) \] for mappings \(\beta\) and \(\varphi\), then the sequence is a unique mixture of i.i.d. sequences from a generalized exponential family determined by \((\beta, t)\). The sufficient statistic is \(\sum_{j=1}^n t(z_j)\), and the mixing measure on the dual semigroup is the implicit prior. Depending on the form of \(t\), the representation characterizes mixtures of specific parametric families: normal (with \(t(z) = (z, z^2)\)), Poisson (with \(t(z) = z\)), gamma, and so on. See Ressel (1985) for the precise semigroup conditions and Fortini and Petrone (2025) for a modern treatment.

Ressel’s theorem runs the logic of Bayesian inference in reverse. In standard Bayesian analysis, we choose a parametric family (say, normal) and then derive the sufficient statistic (\(\bar{y}\) and \(s^2\)). Ressel’s theorem says that if we observe a particular form of predictive sufficiency in the data, if the predictive distribution depends on the data only through a particular type of summary, then the underlying mixing measure must correspond to a specific parametric family. The sufficient statistic determines the model, not the other way around. This is why our framework can be “prior-free” in the operational sense: the form of the summary \(S\) implicitly selects the class of mixing distributions, and the quantile transport map \(Q\) encodes the resulting predictive distribution without ever explicitly specifying a prior or likelihood.

Fortini and Petrone (2025) provide a comprehensive review of these predictive characterizations, formalizing the principle that Bayesian predictive rules are probabilistic learning rules: the predictive distribution \(P_n\) converges almost surely to the directing random measure \(\tilde{F}\), simultaneously with the empirical distribution. The statistical model is the limit of the predictive rule, one need not specify it in advance. Fortini and Petrone (2017) extend this program to Markov exchangeable processes.

11.4.2 From Predictive Sufficiency to Quantile Transport

Under exchangeability and predictive sufficiency through \(S\), there exists a parameter \(\theta\), a prior \(\pi(\theta)\), and a likelihood \(p(z \mid \theta)\) such that \[ p(Y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, Z_{1:n}) = \int p(Y_{n+1} \mid \theta, x_{n+1}) \, \pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n}) \, d\theta. \] One could proceed via Bayes’ theorem: compute the posterior \(\pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n})\) and integrate. But this requires specifying \(p\) and \(\pi\) explicitly and computing the integral.

Our approach bypasses all of this. The noise outsourcing theorem (Theorem 4.1 from Chapter 4) guarantees that there exists a measurable function \(Q\) such that \[ Y_{n+1} = Q\!\left(\tau, \; x_{n+1}, \; S(Z_{1:n})\right), \quad \tau \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1), \tag{11.7}\] where \(Q\) is the conditional quantile function of \(Y_{n+1}\) given the covariate and the sufficient summary. We learn \(Q\) via a quantile neural network from simulated data.

The method is likelihood-free and prior-free in the operational sense: we never specify \(p(z \mid \theta)\) or \(\pi(\theta)\). All we need is the ability to simulate sequences \((Z_1, \ldots, Z_n, Z_{n+1})\) from a forward generative model, from which we extract training tuples \((x_{n+1}, S(Z_{1:n}), Y_{n+1})\). The de Finetti–Ressel theory guarantees that an implicit prior and likelihood exist and could in principle be recovered from the learned quantile map, but we need not do so.

The generative Bayesian prediction algorithm follows the GBC template (Algorithm 10.2 in Section 10.4), adapted for the predictive setting:

  1. Simulate. Generate \(m\) exchangeable sequences \((Z_1^{(j)}, \ldots, Z_n^{(j)}, Z_{n+1}^{(j)})\) for \(j = 1, \ldots, m\) from a forward model. Extract training tuples \((x_{n+1}^{(j)}, s^{(j)}, y_{n+1}^{(j)})\) where \(s^{(j)} = S(Z_{1:n}^{(j)})\).

  2. Summarize. Compute a low-dimensional summary \(s = f(x_{n+1}, Z_{1:n}) \in \mathbb{R}^k\) where \(k\) is the effective dimension. The summary can be hand-crafted (moments, quantiles, autocorrelations) or learned (via a neural network trained to approximate the posterior mean, as in Jiang et al. (2017)).

  3. Train. Learn \(\hat{Q}(\tau, s)\) by minimizing the integrated pinball loss over the training tuples: \[ \hat{Q} = \arg\min_{Q \in \mathcal{Q}} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \mathbb{E}_\tau\!\left[\rho_\tau\!\left(y_{n+1}^{(j)} - Q(\tau, s^{(j)})\right)\right], \] where \(\rho_\tau\) is the pinball loss (Eq. 3.2) and \(\mathcal{Q}\) is the class of quantile neural networks.

  4. Predict. At test time, given observed data \(z_{\text{obs}} = (z_1, \ldots, z_n)\) and a new covariate \(x_{n+1}\):

    • Compute the summary: \(s_{\text{obs}} = S(z_{\text{obs}})\).
    • Draw \(\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_B \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\).
    • Return predictive samples \(\{\hat{Q}(\tau_b, s_{\text{obs}})\}_{b=1}^B\).

At prediction time, each sample requires only a forward pass through the neural network, constant-time computation that contrasts with MCMC or kernel methods.

The algorithm has a clear division of labor between offline and online computation. The offline phase (steps 1–3) is expensive, generating \(m\) training sequences and training a neural network can take hours for complex simulators. But this cost is paid once. The online phase (step 4) is cheap: given new data, computing the summary \(s_{\text{obs}}\) and evaluating the network takes milliseconds. This amortization is the same principle behind simulation-based inference (Cranmer, Brehmer, and Louppe 2020) and neural posterior estimation, but applied to the predictive distribution rather than the parameter posterior. For applications where predictions must be made in real time, financial trading, autonomous driving, clinical decision support, the amortized cost structure is essential.

11.4.3 Normal-Normal Learning and Wang Distortions

To make the framework concrete, consider the normal-normal conjugate model from Section 10.6. The prior-to-posterior update is a Wang distortion (Eq. 10.5): the posterior CDF is obtained by composing the prior CDF with a data-dependent distortion function \(g\). In the predictive setting, the same distortion applies, but the predictive variance includes an additional \(\sigma^2\) term (the observation noise), so the predictive distribution is \(N(\mu_*, \sigma^2 + \sigma_*^2)\) rather than \(N(\mu_*, \sigma_*^2)\).

The GBC framework learns this distortion from simulated data, without ever being told the parametric form.

11.5 Minimax Rates and Compositional Structure

How many training samples \(m\) does the generative approach need? The answer depends on the effective dimension \(k\) of the summary statistic, not the full input dimension \(d\).

We begin with the baseline: classical kernel methods. These estimators are well understood and minimax optimal for smooth functions, but they pay a heavy price as the input dimension grows.

Consider a \(\beta\)-Holder smooth conditional quantile function \(Q_\tau(x)\) with \(x \in \mathbb{R}^d\). The classic Nadaraya–Watson kernel estimator achieves the minimax optimal rate: \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[|Q_\tau(x) - \hat{Q}_\tau(x)|^2\right] = O\!\left(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta + d)}\right). \tag{11.8}\] The optimal bandwidth is \(h^* \propto m^{-1/(2\beta+d)}\), balancing bias \(O(h^{2\beta})\) and variance \(O((mh^d)^{-1})\).

As \(d\) grows, this rate deteriorates rapidly. For \(\beta = 2\) and \(d = 20\), the rate is \(O(m^{-0.17})\), one would need millions of samples for even modest accuracy. This is the curse of dimensionality: kernel methods must smooth over the full \(d\)-dimensional input space.

Suppose the true quantile function admits a compositional structure \[ Q(\tau, z) = g(\tau, f(z)), \] where \(f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^k\) is a summary map with \(k \ll n\), and \(g : [0,1] \times \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}\). This arises naturally when sufficient statistics or learned embeddings of the history capture the relevant information for prediction.

The following result shows that neural networks can exploit this compositional structure to achieve rates that depend on \(k\) rather than \(d\). The layered architecture of a deep network can learn the summary map \(f\) and the quantile map \(g\) separately, each in its own low-dimensional space, rather than approximating the full composition in the high-dimensional input space.

TipProposition 11.2: Minimax Rate for Generative Quantile Prediction

Let \(f \in \mathcal{H}^\beta(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{R}^k)\) and \(g \in \mathcal{H}^\beta([0,1] \times \mathbb{R}^k, \mathbb{R})\) be \(\beta\)-Holder smooth maps (\(\beta > 0\)) so that the target quantile function factorizes as \(Q(\tau, z) = g(\tau, f(z))\). Let \(\hat{Q}\) be a ReLU network estimator with depth \(L = O(\log m)\) layers and width \(W = O(m / \log m)\) weights, trained on \(m\) i.i.d. tuples \((Z_i, \tau_i, Q(\tau_i, Z_i))\) drawn from \(P_Z \otimes \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)\). Then, with the expectation over the training sample, \[ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_0^1 \int |\hat{Q}(\tau, s) - Q(\tau, s)|^2 \, dP_S(s) \, d\tau\right] = O\!\left(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta + k + 1)}\right), \tag{11.9}\] where \(P_S\) is the distribution of \(S = f(Z)\) and \(k = \dim(S)\). This rate is minimax optimal over the compositional function class \(\{g \circ f : f \in \mathcal{H}^\beta(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{R}^k),\, g \in \mathcal{H}^\beta([0,1] \times \mathbb{R}^k, \mathbb{R})\}\).

The result follows from the work of Schmidt-Hieber (2020) and Schmidt-Hieber and Zamolodtchikov (2024). Each layer of the ReLU network can approximate one component of the composition, so the approximation complexity depends on the dimension \(k\) of the intermediate representation, not the full input dimension \(d\).

The practical message: invest in finding good summary statistics. The convergence rate depends on \(k\), not on \(d\), so a well-chosen low-dimensional summary \(S\) can make the difference between a method that needs thousands of training samples and one that needs millions. In the extreme case where \(k = 1\) (the entire history is compressed into a single number), the rate is \(O(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+2)})\), which for \(\beta = 2\) gives \(O(m^{-2/3})\), close to the parametric rate. Of course, compressing to \(k = 1\) may lose important information, so the choice of \(k\) involves a bias-variance tradeoff that we discuss below.

Why composition matters. ReLU networks exploit compositional structure through their layered architecture. Each layer learns a component of the composition, so the approximation complexity depends on \(k\) rather than \(d\). Kernel methods, by contrast, treat the input as a flat vector in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) and cannot exploit such structure. The dimension gap between \(k\) and \(d\) determines the practical advantage of neural networks over kernel methods.

To make this concrete, consider a prediction problem with \(d = 20\) input lags, smoothness \(\beta = 2\), and \(k = 3\) summary statistics (mean, variance, last value):

The neural network is substantially more sample-efficient. At \(m = 1000\) training samples, the neural network’s \(L^2\) error is roughly 8 times smaller than the kernel’s. This gap widens as \(d\) increases relative to \(k\).

The convergence rate depends on \(k\), so choosing a good summary \(S\) is essential. The simplest option is to use hand-crafted summaries, moments, quantiles, or autocorrelations of the data, which are interpretable but may miss relevant features. When the process has short memory, a fixed window of the last \(k\) observations is often effective. For more demanding problems, one can train a neural network \(\hat{S}(Z_{1:n}) \approx \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid Z_{1:n}]\) to learn the summary jointly with the quantile predictor, as proposed by Jiang et al. (2017); the posterior mean is Bayes-optimal as a sufficient statistic.

The choice of \(k\) involves a bias–variance tradeoff: small \(k\) yields fast convergence but risks information loss, while large \(k\) preserves information at the cost of slower rates. In practice, cross-validation on the pinball loss provides a principled way to select \(k\): for each candidate summary dimension, we train the quantile network, evaluate the out-of-sample pinball loss, and choose the \(k\) that minimizes it. If the selected \(k\) is much smaller than \(d\), this confirms that the problem has low intrinsic dimensionality and the neural network approach will be advantageous.

What Proposition 11.2 does not say is also important. It does not say that neural networks are always better than kernel methods. When \(k = d\) (no low-dimensional structure exists), the kernel rate \(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)}\) is actually slightly better than the neural network rate \(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d+1)}\), because the neural network must also handle the additional \(\tau\) input. The advantage of neural networks is conditional on the existence of compositional structure, and the magnitude of the advantage is proportional to the gap between \(d\) and \(k\). Before reaching for a deep network, ask whether your prediction problem has a natural low-dimensional summary. If it does, the theory predicts, and our simulation confirms, that neural networks will be substantially more efficient.

11.6 Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI)

A distinct but related approach to leveraging machine learning for valid prediction is prediction-powered inference (PPI) of Angelopoulos et al. (2023). PPI addresses a setting where we have a small labeled dataset \((X_i, Y_i)_{i=1}^n\) with both features and responses, alongside a large unlabeled dataset \((\tilde{X}_i)_{i=1}^N\) with \(N \gg n\) where we can evaluate a pre-trained predictor \(f(\tilde{X}_i)\) but do not observe \(Y\).

The goal is to estimate a population parameter \(\theta^* = \mathbb{E}[Y]\) (or more generally, the solution to an estimating equation) with valid confidence intervals.

The PPI estimator combines the ML predictions on the large dataset with a bias correction from the small labeled dataset: \[ \hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(\tilde{X}_i)}_{\hat{\theta}^f} \; - \; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(f(X_i) - Y_i\big)}_{\hat{\Delta}}. \tag{11.10}\]

The first term \(\hat{\theta}^f\) is the ML-based estimate using all \(N\) unlabeled points. The second term \(\hat{\Delta}\) corrects for the bias of \(f\) using the labeled data. The estimator has three key properties. First, it is unbiased: \(\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}}] = \theta^*\) regardless of the quality of \(f\). Second, when \(f\) is a good predictor \(\hat{\Delta} \approx 0\) and \(\hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}} \approx \hat{\theta}^f\), with variance reduced by a factor of roughly \(n/N\). Third, if \(f\) is a poor predictor, \(\hat{\Delta}\) corrects the bias and we pay only the cost of estimating the bias from \(n\) labeled points.

Since \(\hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}}\) is a sum of two independent terms (one from the unlabeled data, one from the labeled data), valid confidence intervals follow from standard CLT arguments: \[ \hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\hat{\sigma}_f^2}{N} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_\Delta^2}{n}}, \] where \(\hat{\sigma}_f^2\) and \(\hat{\sigma}_\Delta^2\) are the sample variances of the two components. See also Zrnic and Candès (2024) for a cross-fitted variant.

PPI and GBC share a common philosophy: both use ML predictions to improve statistical inference while maintaining validity guarantees. Concretely, PPI uses ML predictions as a control variate for estimation, achieving valid inference through bias correction, while GBC uses neural networks to learn the full predictive distribution, achieving valid prediction through quantile transport. Both approaches recognize that black-box ML predictions contain useful information but are not, by themselves, statistically valid. PPI corrects the bias of point predictions; GBC calibrates quantile predictions through the pinball loss and (optionally) conformal post-processing. The approaches are complementary: one could use a GBC quantile network as the predictor \(f\) within the PPI framework.

11.6.1 PPI Worked Example: House Price Prediction

To make PPI concrete, we work through a synthetic house price example. Suppose we want to estimate the average house price in a city. We have a small labeled dataset of \(n = 500\) houses where both features (square footage, number of bedrooms, lot size) and sale prices are observed, and a large unlabeled dataset of \(N = 10{,}000\) houses where only features are available. A pre-trained gradient boosting model \(f\) can predict prices from features, but its predictions are systematically biased, perhaps it was trained on data from a different city or a different time period.

The classical approach uses only the \(n = 500\) labeled observations: \(\hat{\theta}_n = \bar{Y} = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i\). The PPI approach uses both datasets, applying the bias correction in Eq. 11.10. The key question is: how much does the unlabeled data help?

When the predictor is nearly unbiased, PPI produces intervals that are substantially narrower than the classical approach because it effectively uses the 10,000 unlabeled predictions. The variance reduction comes from using the predictor \(f\) as a control variate: the unlabeled predictions \(f(\tilde{X}_i)\) are correlated with the true prices, and this correlation reduces the effective variance of the estimator.

11.7 When GBC Prediction Excels: A Heteroscedastic AR(1) Example

Before the broader comparison study, a single clean example makes the main point concrete. Consider an AR(1) process with state-dependent variance: \[ Y_t = 0.8\, Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(0,\; 0.1 + 0.4 Y_{t-1}^2\bigr). \tag{11.11}\]

The conditional distribution \(Y_t \mid Y_{t-1}\) is Gaussian with state-dependent variance. A homoscedastic predictor (one that assumes constant variance) will produce prediction intervals that are too narrow in high-variance states (say \(|Y_{t-1}| \approx 2\)) and too wide in low-variance states (\(Y_{t-1} \approx 0\)). GBC, which learns the full conditional quantile function without any distributional assumption, adapts automatically.

11.8 Empirical: Neural vs Kernel Prediction

We illustrate the theoretical rate advantages with a simulation study comparing kernel and neural network quantile estimators on an AR(2) process with heteroscedastic errors: \[ X_t = 0.5 X_{t-1} - 0.3 X_{t-2} + \epsilon_t, \quad \epsilon_t \sim N(0, \sigma_t^2), \] where \(\sigma_t = (0.5 + 0.3 X_{t-1}^2 + 0.2 X_{t-2}^2)^{1/2}\). The conditional quantile function depends on \((X_{t-1}, X_{t-2})\) only, so the intrinsic dimension is 2, but we provide \(d = 20\) lags as input to test how each method handles the extraneous dimensions.

Setup. We generate \(m = 1000\) training sequences of length 50 and use the last \(d = 20\) lags as history. The neural network receives three summary statistics (\(k = 3\)): sample mean, standard deviation, and last value. The kernel method uses a Nadaraya–Watson estimator with Gaussian kernel on the full \(d = 20\) lag vector. Both methods estimate quantile levels \(\tau \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9\}\) jointly.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

np.random.seed(42)

def generate_ar2_data(n_sequences, seq_length, n_lags=20):
    """Generate AR(2) heteroscedastic data."""
    X_all, Y_all, S_all = [], [], []
    for _ in range(n_sequences):
        x = np.zeros(seq_length + n_lags + 2)
        x[:2] = np.random.randn(2)
        for t in range(2, len(x)):
            sig = np.sqrt(0.5 + 0.3 * x[t-1]**2 + 0.2 * x[t-2]**2)
            x[t] = 0.5 * x[t-1] - 0.3 * x[t-2] + sig * np.random.randn()
        # Last n_lags values as features, next value as target
        lags = x[-(n_lags+1):-1]
        target = x[-1]
        # Summary statistics
        summary = np.array([lags.mean(), lags.std(), lags[-1]])
        X_all.append(lags)
        Y_all.append(target)
        S_all.append(summary)
    return np.array(X_all), np.array(Y_all), np.array(S_all)

# Generate data
X_tr, Y_tr, S_tr = generate_ar2_data(300, 50)
X_te, Y_te, S_te = generate_ar2_data(100, 50)

tau_levels = np.array([0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9])

# --- Kernel method (Nadaraya-Watson on full d=20 lags) ---
def kernel_quantile(X_tr, Y_tr, X_te, tau, bandwidth=None):
    """Nadaraya-Watson kernel quantile estimator."""
    n, d = X_tr.shape
    if bandwidth is None:
        bandwidth = 1.06 * X_tr.std(axis=0).mean() * n ** (-1.0 / (d + 4))  # Silverman
    preds = []
    for x in X_te:
        dists = np.sum((X_tr - x)**2, axis=1)
        weights = np.exp(-dists / (2 * bandwidth**2))
        weights /= weights.sum() + 1e-10
        # Weighted quantile
        sorted_idx = np.argsort(Y_tr)
        cum_w = np.cumsum(weights[sorted_idx])
        idx = np.searchsorted(cum_w, tau)
        idx = min(idx, len(Y_tr) - 1)
        preds.append(Y_tr[sorted_idx[idx]])
    return np.array(preds)

# --- Neural network (simple 3-layer MLP on k=3 summaries) ---
# We use a simple numpy implementation for reproducibility
class SimpleQNN:
    def __init__(self, input_dim, hidden_dims=[128, 128, 64], lr=0.005):
        self.layers = []
        dims = [input_dim + 1] + hidden_dims + [1]  # +1 for tau input
        for i in range(len(dims) - 1):
            W = np.random.randn(dims[i], dims[i+1]) * np.sqrt(2.0 / dims[i])
            b = np.zeros(dims[i+1])
            self.layers.append((W, b))
        self.lr = lr

    def forward(self, x, tau_val):
        """Forward pass with tau concatenated to input."""
        h = np.concatenate([x, np.full((x.shape[0], 1), tau_val)], axis=1)
        self.activations = [h]
        for i, (W, b) in enumerate(self.layers):
            h = h @ W + b
            if i < len(self.layers) - 1:
                h = np.maximum(h, 0)  # ReLU
            self.activations.append(h)
        return h.ravel()

    def train(self, X, Y, epochs=200, batch_size=128):
        n = X.shape[0]
        for epoch in range(epochs):
            perm = np.random.permutation(n)
            for start in range(0, n, batch_size):
                idx = perm[start:start+batch_size]
                tau_val = np.random.uniform(0.05, 0.95)
                pred = self.forward(X[idx], tau_val)
                residual = Y[idx] - pred
                # Pinball loss gradient
                grad = np.where(residual >= 0, -tau_val, -(tau_val - 1))
                # Full backpropagation through all layers
                delta = grad.reshape(-1, 1)
                for i in range(len(self.layers) - 1, -1, -1):
                    h_in = self.activations[i]
                    W, b = self.layers[i]
                    dW = h_in.T @ delta / delta.shape[0]
                    db = delta.mean(axis=0)
                    self.layers[i] = (W - self.lr * dW, b - self.lr * db)
                    if i > 0:
                        delta = delta @ W.T
                        delta = delta * (self.activations[i] > 0)  # ReLU gradient

    def predict(self, X, tau_val):
        return self.forward(X, tau_val)

# Train QNN

qnn.train(S_tr, Y_tr, epochs=100)

In Figure 11.5, the neural network (coral) achieves lower pinball loss than the kernel method (blue) across all quantile levels, and its coverage tracks the ideal diagonal more closely. The kernel method, operating on the full \(d = 20\) lag vector, suffers from the curse of dimensionality: it must smooth over a 20-dimensional space, effectively averaging over irrelevant dimensions. The neural network, operating on \(k = 3\) summary statistics, exploits the low-dimensional compositional structure of the quantile function.

The theoretical rates from Proposition 11.2 predict this outcome: the neural network converges at rate \(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+k+1)} = m^{-0.50}\) (with \(\beta = 2\), \(k = 3\)), while the kernel converges at rate \(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)} = m^{-0.17}\) (with \(d = 20\)). The practical advantage tracks the theoretical gap.

One caveat in this comparison: the kernel method operates on the raw 20-dimensional lag vector while the neural network operates on 3 hand-crafted summary statistics. Part of the kernel’s disadvantage reflects operating in a higher-dimensional space rather than a deficiency of the kernel method itself. A fairer comparison would give the kernel the same 3 summary statistics as input. In that case the kernel rate would improve to \(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+k)} = m^{-4/7} \approx m^{-0.57}\), which is slightly better than the neural network rate of \(m^{-0.50}\) (the neural network pays for the additional \(\tau\) input). The neural network’s real advantage is its ability to learn the summary jointly with the quantile function when hand-crafted summaries are not available.

# Generative Bayesian Prediction {#sec-prediction}

> "*Prediction is very difficult, especially about the future.*" -- attributed to Niels Bohr

The previous chapter developed GBC as a tool for Bayesian *inference*, estimating parameters $\theta$ from data. We now address *prediction*: given observations $Z_1, \ldots, Z_n$, characterize the distribution of a future observation $Y_{n+1}$. Prediction is arguably the primary goal of statistics. As @efron2020prediction observed, most of the practical success of modern machine learning, random forests, gradient boosting, deep nets, is driven by prediction, not parameter estimation.

The standard Bayesian predictive distribution integrates over the posterior:
$$
p(y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, Z_{1:n}) = \int p(y_{n+1} \mid \theta, x_{n+1}) \, \pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n}) \, d\theta.
$$ {#eq-pred-bayes-predictive}
This requires specifying a likelihood $p(y \mid \theta,x)$ and a prior $\pi(\theta)$, then computing the integral, either analytically or via MCMC. In high-dimensional settings, both specification and computation are challenging.

This chapter develops an alternative. Instead of densities and integrals, we learn the predictive *quantile function* directly:
$$
Y_{n+1} = Q(\tau, \, x_{n+1}, \, S(Z_{1:n})), \quad \tau \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1),
$$
where $S$ is a summary of the observed data. The noise outsourcing theorem (@sec-noise-outsourcing) guarantees that such a map exists; the de Finetti--Ressel representation guarantees that, under exchangeability, the implicit prior and likelihood needed for @eq-pred-bayes-predictive also exist, we just never need to compute them.

We contrast this approach with conformal prediction (CP), the leading frequentist method for distribution-free prediction. Our central theoretical result is that CP and Bayesian prediction are fundamentally different: they disagree on four structural properties, and the set of distributions where they agree is topologically negligible. CP belongs to the Fisher--Dempster--Hill fiducial tradition, not to the Bayesian tradition.

## The Prediction Problem {#sec-pred-problem}

We observe data $Z_i = (X_i, Y_i)$ for $i = 1, \ldots, n$, where $X_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ are covariates and $Y_i \in \mathbb{R}$ is a scalar response. Given a new covariate $x_{n+1}$, the goal is to characterize the full predictive distribution of $Y_{n+1}$. A *point forecast* $\hat{y}_{n+1}$ is not enough, we need uncertainty quantification.

Two paradigms provide prediction sets $C(x_{n+1})$ satisfying a coverage guarantee of the form $P(Y_{n+1} \in C(x_{n+1})) \geq 1 - \alpha$. The *frequentist (conformal)* approach constructs $C$ so that the coverage guarantee holds marginally over both the training data and the test point, under the sole assumption that $(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n, Z_{n+1})$ are exchangeable; no model is assumed and the guarantee is distribution-free and finite-sample valid. The *Bayesian (posterior predictive)* approach specifies a likelihood $p(y \mid \theta,x)$ and prior $\pi(\theta)$, computes the posterior $\pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n})$, and integrates:
$$
p(y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, Z_{1:n}) = \int p(y_{n+1} \mid \theta, x_{n+1}) \, \pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n}) \, d\theta.
$$
The resulting $1-\alpha$ credible set has *conditional* coverage: given the data $Z_{1:n}$, the coverage holds pointwise for each realization of the training set.

These two paradigms look superficially similar, both produce prediction intervals, but they differ in important structural ways.

*Prediction vs. estimation.* @efron2020prediction gives a clean illustration of the distinction. Consider the normal model $(y_1, \ldots, y_n \mid \theta) \sim N(\theta, 1)$. For estimating $\theta$, the sample mean $\bar{y}$ is 57\% more efficient than the sample median $\tilde{y}$:
$$
\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{y} - \theta)^2]}{\mathbb{E}[(\bar{y} - \theta)^2]} = \frac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.57.
$$
But for predicting a new $y_{n+1}$, the ratio is
$$
\frac{\mathbb{E}[(\tilde{y} - y_{n+1})^2]}{\mathbb{E}[(\bar{y} - y_{n+1})^2]} = \frac{\pi/(2n) + 1}{1/n + 1} \approx 1.02 \quad \text{(for } n = 25\text{)},
$$
and it approaches 1 as $n \to \infty$. The mean still wins, but barely, because most of the prediction error comes from the irreducible noise of $y_{n+1}$ itself. Prediction is fundamentally different from estimation: the estimator matters less, and the distributional shape matters more.


## Conformal Prediction {#sec-pred-conformal}

Conformal prediction, introduced by @vovk2005algorithmic, constructs prediction sets with finite-sample, distribution-free coverage guarantees. We describe the *split conformal* method, which is the most widely used variant.

*Setup.* Split the data into a *training set* $\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}$ (indices $1, \ldots, m$) and a *calibration set* $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}$ (indices $m+1, \ldots, n$), where $n_{\text{cal}} = n - m$.

*Algorithm.* Split conformal prediction proceeds in four steps:

1. *Fit:* Train any regression model $\hat{\mu}$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}$ to produce point predictions $\hat{\mu}(x)$.
2. *Score:* Compute nonconformity scores on the calibration set:
$$
R_i = |Y_i - \hat{\mu}(X_i)|, \quad i = m+1, \ldots, n.
$$
3. *Quantile:* Let $\hat{q}$ be the $\lceil (1-\alpha)(n_{\text{cal}} + 1) \rceil / n_{\text{cal}}$ empirical quantile of the scores $\{R_{m+1}, \ldots, R_n\}$. If $\lceil (1-\alpha)(n_{\text{cal}}+1) \rceil > n_{\text{cal}}$, set the threshold to $+\infty$ (the coverage guarantee is then trivially satisfied).
4. *Predict:* The prediction set for $Y_{n+1}$ at covariate $x_{n+1}$ is
$$
C(x_{n+1}) = \{y : |y - \hat{\mu}(x_{n+1})| \leq \hat{q}\} = [\hat{\mu}(x_{n+1}) - \hat{q}, \; \hat{\mu}(x_{n+1}) + \hat{q}].
$$ {#eq-pred-cp-set}

::: {.callout-tip appearance="simple" title="Theorem 11.1: Conformal Coverage Guarantee"}
Let $\hat{\mu}$ be any regression model trained on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}$ only (not on the calibration or test data). If $(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n, Z_{n+1})$ are exchangeable, then for any distribution $P$,
$$
P\!\left(Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})\right) \geq 1 - \alpha,
$$ {#eq-pred-cp-coverage}
where $C(x_{n+1})$ is the conformal prediction set in @eq-pred-cp-set and $\hat{q}$ is computed from the calibration residuals $\{R_i : i \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}\}$.
:::

*Proof sketch.* Because $\hat{\mu}$ is trained on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}$ and not on the calibration or test points, it is a fixed (deterministic) function when we condition on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}$. The calibration residuals $\{R_i = |Y_i - \hat{\mu}(X_i)| : i \in \mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}\}$ and the test residual $R_{n+1} = |Y_{n+1} - \hat{\mu}(X_{n+1})|$ are therefore exchangeable (they inherit exchangeability from the original sequence $(Z_i)$). Among $n_{\text{cal}} + 1$ exchangeable random variables, the probability that any one of them exceeds the $(1-\alpha)$-quantile of the others is at most $\alpha$. This is a finite-sample result requiring no parametric assumptions on $P$ beyond exchangeability. $\square$

*Strengths.* The guarantee in @eq-pred-cp-coverage holds for *any* model $\hat{\mu}$, even a terrible one, and for *any* distribution $P$. It is finite-sample (not asymptotic), and it requires no parametric assumptions: we get valid uncertainty quantification regardless of model misspecification.

*Limitations.* The coverage is *marginal*, averaged over both the calibration data and the test point. It says nothing about coverage conditional on a particular value of $X_{n+1}$ or a particular realization of the training data. The prediction band in @eq-pred-cp-set has constant width $2\hat{q}$ regardless of $x$, it does not adapt to heteroscedasticity. (We discuss the adaptive variant in the conformalized quantile regression procedure below.)

### Conformalized Quantile Regression {#sec-pred-cqr}

@romano2019conformalized proposed *conformalized quantile regression* (CQR), which combines the adaptivity of quantile regression with the coverage guarantee of conformal prediction. The idea is to use a quantile neural network as the base model, then apply conformal calibration:

1. Train a quantile network to predict $\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x)$ and $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x)$ on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{tr}}$.
2. Compute conformity scores on $\mathcal{D}_{\text{cal}}$:
$$
R_i = \max\!\left(\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(X_i) - Y_i, \; Y_i - \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(X_i)\right).
$$
3. Let $\hat{Q}$ be the $(1-\alpha)(1 + 1/n_{\text{cal}})$ empirical quantile of these scores.
4. The prediction interval is
$$
C(x_{n+1}) = \left[\hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x_{n+1}) - \hat{Q}, \; \hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x_{n+1}) + \hat{Q}\right].
$$ {#eq-pred-cqr}

The interval width $\hat{q}_{1-\alpha/2}(x) - \hat{q}_{\alpha/2}(x) + 2\hat{Q}$ now varies with $x$, inheriting the adaptive width properties of quantile regression. The conformal correction $\hat{Q}$ ensures that the finite-sample coverage guarantee still holds.

Let us implement split conformal prediction on a simple heteroscedastic regression problem.

## Conformal $\neq$ Bayes: Four Separations {#sec-pred-cp-not-bayes}

Is conformal prediction Bayesian? The coverage guarantee @eq-pred-cp-coverage *looks* like a probability statement about $Y_{n+1}$, which might suggest a Bayesian interpretation. The answer is no. We now develop four structural separations showing that conformal prediction belongs to the fiducial tradition of Fisher, Dempster, and @hill1968posterior, not to the Bayesian tradition.

Many applied researchers use conformal prediction and Bayesian prediction interchangeably, reasoning that both produce prediction intervals and both invoke exchangeability. The four results below show that this conflation is a mistake. The two frameworks answer fundamentally different questions, conformal prediction asks "will my coverage be correct on average over future data?" while Bayesian prediction asks "what do I believe about this particular future observation given everything I have seen?" Understanding which question you are answering is essential for interpreting your prediction intervals and for deciding when to use which approach.

To understand the fiducial roots of conformal prediction, we begin with the Hill--Dempster $A_{(n)}$ rule [@hill1968posterior]. Given observations $X_{(1)} < X_{(2)} < \cdots < X_{(n)}$ (the order statistics), the $A_{(n)}$ rule assigns predictive probability
$$
P_{A_{(n)}}(Y_{n+1} \in (X_{(i)}, X_{(i+1)})) = \frac{1}{n+1}, \quad i = 0, 1, \ldots, n,
$$ {#eq-pred-an}
where $X_{(0)} = -\infty$ and $X_{(n+1)} = \infty$. That is, the next observation is equally likely to fall in any of the $n+1$ intervals formed by the order statistics.

This rule is distribution-free and produces valid rank calibration: the rank of $Y_{n+1}$ among $(X_1, \ldots, X_n, Y_{n+1})$ is uniformly distributed on $\{1, 2, \ldots, n+1\}$ under exchangeability. Conformal prediction is precisely the modern, algorithmic descendant of $A_{(n)}$, both exploit the uniform distribution of ranks under exchangeability.

The question is whether $A_{(n)}$ can be given a Bayesian interpretation. The answer, as we now show, is negative in four distinct ways.

**Separation 1: Non-extensionality.** A Bayesian predictive distribution is *extensional*: it depends only on the conditional distribution $P(Y \mid X)$, not on the marginal $P(X)$. Formally, if two data-generating processes $P$ and $P'$ share the same conditional $P(Y \mid X) = P'(Y \mid X)$ but differ in their marginal distributions $P(X) \neq P'(X)$, then the Bayesian posterior predictive at $x_{n+1}$ is the same under both.

Conformal prediction violates this property. The calibration quantile $\hat{q}$ depends on the residuals $\{R_i = |Y_i - \hat{\mu}(X_i)|\}$ evaluated at the calibration points. Changing the distribution of $X$ (while keeping $P(Y \mid X)$ fixed) changes which residuals are observed, which changes $\hat{q}$, which changes the prediction set. In other words, conformal prediction sets for $Y_{n+1}$ at a fixed $x_{n+1}$ can change even when the conditional model $P(Y \mid X = x_{n+1})$ is unchanged.

*Example.* Consider $Y \mid X = x \sim N(0, 1)$ (the conditional model is the same for all $x$). If $X \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$, the calibration points are spread evenly over $[0,1]$. If instead $X$ is concentrated near 0, the calibration points cluster near 0 and the residuals reflect the model's behavior near 0 only. For a nonlinear model $\hat{\mu}$, these residual distributions can differ, leading to different conformal prediction sets at the same test point $x_{n+1} = 0.5$.

A Bayesian would never change their prediction about $Y_{n+1}$ based on the marginal distribution of the covariates, this follows directly from the factorization $P(Y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, Z_{1:n}) \propto \int p(y_{n+1} \mid \theta, x_{n+1}) \pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n}) \, d\theta$.

In plain language, Separation 1 says that conformal prediction cares about *where the training data came from* in the covariate space, not just about the relationship between covariates and responses. If we change the distribution of house sizes in our training set (while keeping the price-given-size relationship identical), the conformal prediction interval for a specific house changes, but the Bayesian predictive distribution does not. This makes conformal prediction sensitive to sampling design in a way that Bayesian prediction is not.

**Separation 2: Nonconglomerability.** Conglomerability is a coherence property of probability. A probability measure $P$ is *conglomerable* with respect to a partition $\{B_j\}$ if
$$
P(A \mid B_j) \geq \alpha \quad \text{for all } j \quad \Longrightarrow \quad P(A) \geq \alpha.
$$
That is, if an event has high conditional probability given every element of a partition, then it has high unconditional probability.

All $\sigma$-additive probability measures are conglomerable ($\sigma$-additivity implies conglomerability). Bayesian predictive distributions, being proper $\sigma$-additive measures, are automatically conglomerable.

The conformal coverage guarantee, by contrast, is *marginal*:
$$
P(Y_{n+1} \in C(X_{n+1})) \geq 1 - \alpha.
$$
This averages over $X_{n+1}$. The conditional coverage $P(Y_{n+1} \in C(x) \mid X_{n+1} = x)$ can be much lower than $1 - \alpha$ for some values of $x$ and much higher for others. The marginal guarantee hides conditional miscoverage. Any attempt to interpret conformal's rank calibration as a finitely additive probability (as in the $A_{(n)}$ framework) produces a nonconglomerable measure, one that admits a Dutch book (sure loss) in the decision-theoretic sense of de Finetti.

The practical consequence of Separation 2 is immediate. Suppose we are predicting hospital readmission risk. Conformal prediction might achieve 90% marginal coverage across all patients, yet have only 70% coverage for elderly patients with multiple comorbidities and 98% coverage for healthy young adults. The marginal guarantee tells us nothing about coverage within any subgroup. A Bayesian predictive distribution, by contrast, provides valid conditional coverage for each patient profile (assuming the model is correctly specified). When decisions are made at the individual level, which patient to monitor, which loan to approve, the conditional guarantee is what matters, and conformal prediction cannot provide it without additional assumptions.

**Separation 3: No $\sigma$-additive extension.** A natural question is whether we can "fix" the conformal prediction framework by finding a proper (countably additive) probability measure on $\mathbb{R}^\infty$ that reproduces the $A_{(n)}$ rank calibration at every sample size.

The answer is no. The following result shows that the $A_{(n)}$ calibration is inherently finitely additive:

::: {.callout-tip appearance="simple" title="Proposition 11.1: No $\\sigma$-Additive Extension"}
There exists no countably additive exchangeable probability measure on $\mathbb{R}^\infty$ that, for every $n$, assigns equal probability $1/(n+1)$ to the rank of $Y_{n+1}$ among $(X_1, \ldots, X_n, Y_{n+1})$ being each of $\{1, 2, \ldots, n+1\}$.
:::

*Proof sketch.* By de Finetti's theorem, any $\sigma$-additive exchangeable measure on $\mathbb{R}^\infty$ is a mixture of i.i.d. sequences. The $A_{(n)}$ rule demands that, for every $n$, the rank of $Y_{n+1}$ among $(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n, Y_{n+1})$ is uniform on $\{1, \ldots, n+1\}$ when conditioning on $\sigma(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n)$ (the $\sigma$-algebra generated by the training data alone, excluding $Y_{n+1}$). For any i.i.d. continuous distribution $F$ and any realization $z_{1:n}$ with order statistics $z_{(1)} \le \cdots \le z_{(n)}$, the conditional probability is $P(\text{rank}(Y_{n+1}) = k \mid Z_{1:n} = z_{1:n}) = F(z_{(k)}) - F(z_{(k-1)})$ (with $z_{(0)} = -\infty$, $z_{(n+1)} = +\infty$). This equals $1/(n+1)$ only when the $z_j$ happen to be exact $F$-quantiles, which fails for $F$-almost-all realizations. No de Finetti mixture can satisfy this $\sigma(Z_{1:n})$-conditional uniformity for all $n$, establishing that no $\sigma$-additive exchangeable measure meets the $A_{(n)}$ requirement. $\square$

This says that the conformal calibration property cannot arise from any Bayesian model, not from a well-chosen prior, not from a hierarchical model, not from a nonparametric Bayesian procedure. The calibration is a property of the *procedure*, not of any *belief*. There is no prior that would produce the exact rank-uniform calibration that conformal prediction achieves. Conformal prediction rests on finite additivity and the combinatorics of exchangeable ranks; Bayesian prediction rests on $\sigma$-additivity and the de Finetti mixture representation. These are genuinely different probability theories, and neither subsumes the other.

**Separation 4: Meagre intersection.** The previous separations show specific ways in which CP and Bayes differ. The fourth separation is topological and says that the two frameworks agree only on a negligible set.

Consider the space of all predictive kernels $K : \mathcal{X}^n \times \mathcal{X} \to \Delta(\mathbb{R})$, where $\Delta(\mathbb{R})$ is the set of probability measures on $\mathbb{R}$. Let $\mathcal{B}$ denote the set of predictive kernels arising from some Bayesian model (prior + likelihood), and let $\mathcal{C}$ denote the set arising from some conformal procedure with valid coverage.

Under appropriate topologies (e.g., total variation on the predictive measures, uniform convergence over $\mathcal{X}$), one can show that the intersection $\mathcal{B} \cap \mathcal{C}$ is *Baire meagre* (a countable union of nowhere-dense sets); the argument follows from the rigidity of the exchangeable rank condition and the smoothness of Bayesian updating [see the discussion in @vovk2005algorithmic, Ch. 8]. Topologically, this means the set of predictive distributions that are *both* Bayesian and conformally calibrated is negligible, a zero-measure-like concept in a topological setting.

The meagre intersection result is the strongest of the four separations because it is a genericity claim, not just an existence claim: the two frameworks disagree almost everywhere. The few cases where they agree, such as the uniform distribution on a finite set, are mathematical curiosities, not representative of real prediction problems.

*Practical implication.* If you want marginal coverage, use conformal prediction. If you want a coherent belief state for sequential updating, decision-making, and conditional inference, use Bayesian prediction. Trying to get both from a single framework is generically impossible.

The four separations place conformal prediction squarely in the Fisher--Dempster--Hill *fiducial* tradition. Fisher (1930) introduced fiducial inference as a way to make probability statements about parameters without a prior. Dempster (1968) gave a probabilistic interpretation in the nonparametric setting, and @hill1968posterior extended it to the predictive $A_{(n)}$ framework.

@hannig2016generalized showed that generalized fiducial inference carries an *implicit prior*, an empirical Jeffreys prior based on observed information. Specifically, if there exists a reparametrization $\phi = u(\theta)$ such that the normalized likelihood factors as a location family, the quantity $|du/d\theta|$ plays the role of this implicit prior. But this implicit prior is finitely additive, not $\sigma$-additive, and the resulting predictive distribution inherits the nonconglomerability of Separation 2.

The GBC framework we develop in the next section is explicitly Bayesian: it produces $\sigma$-additive predictive distributions that are conglomerable, extensional, and composable for sequential updating. This does not mean that conformal prediction is useless, far from it. As we saw in @sec-pred-cqr, conformal calibration can be applied as a post-processing step on top of any prediction method, including GBC. Conformal calibration provides a *frequentist safety net* (marginal coverage) but does not, by itself, give you a Bayesian predictive distribution. The two are complementary tools: GBC provides the probabilistic model, and conformal calibration provides the distribution-free coverage guarantee.


## Generative Bayesian Prediction via Quantile Transport {#sec-pred-generative}

We now develop the generative approach to Bayesian prediction. We bypass the density-based integral in @eq-pred-bayes-predictive and learn the predictive quantile function directly. The theoretical foundation rests on two pillars: the de Finetti--Ressel representation theorem, which guarantees that an implicit prior exists, and the noise outsourcing theorem, which provides the quantile transport map.

### Exchangeability and the de Finetti--Ressel Representation {#sec-pred-definetti}

Exchangeability is the assumption that the order of observations does not matter for prediction. More precisely, a sequence $(Z_1, Z_2, \ldots)$ is *exchangeable* if its joint distribution is invariant under finite permutations of the indices: for every $n$ and every permutation $\sigma$ of $\{1, \ldots, n\}$,
$$
(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n) \stackrel{d}{=} (Z_{\sigma(1)}, \ldots, Z_{\sigma(n)}).
$$
This is a weaker assumption than independence, exchangeable random variables can be correlated, but it captures a powerful structural insight: if we have no reason to believe that observation $i$ is fundamentally different from observation $j$, then permuting the labels should not change our joint beliefs.

Why is exchangeability the right assumption for prediction? Consider predicting tomorrow's stock return given the last $n$ returns. We believe that the returns are generated by some common mechanism (the market), but we do not know what that mechanism is. If we had no additional information (such as time trends or regime changes), we would have no reason to distinguish the first return from the last. Exchangeability formalizes this symmetry. It says that all the information in the data relevant to prediction is captured by *symmetric functions* of the observations, counts, order statistics, sample moments, rather than by which observation came first.

By de Finetti's representation theorem, there exists a random distribution $\tilde{F}$ such that, conditionally on $\tilde{F}$, the $Z_i$ are i.i.d. from $\tilde{F}$. The distribution of $\tilde{F}$ is the *implicit prior*, we can act as if there exists a parameter $\theta$, a likelihood $p(z \mid \theta)$, and a prior $\pi(\theta)$. This is the connection between exchangeability and Bayesian inference: you do not need to start with a prior. If you believe your data is exchangeable, de Finetti's theorem tells you that a prior *must exist*, whether or not you can write it down. The prior is not a subjective choice imposed from outside; it is a mathematical consequence of the symmetry assumption.

The connection to prediction is immediate. For a Bayesian, the predictive distribution of $Z_{n+1}$ given $Z_1, \ldots, Z_n$ is obtained by integrating over the mixing measure: $p(Z_{n+1} \mid Z_{1:n}) = \int p(Z_{n+1} \mid \tilde{F}) \, d\Pi(\tilde{F} \mid Z_{1:n})$, where $\Pi(\cdot \mid Z_{1:n})$ is the posterior over mixing measures. The crucial point is that this predictive distribution depends on the data only through the *sufficient statistic* for the mixing measure. In the parametric case, this is the classical sufficient statistic; in the nonparametric case, it is the empirical distribution itself.

The critical result for prediction is *predictive sufficiency*:
$$
p(Y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, Z_1, \ldots, Z_n) = p(Y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, S(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n)),
$$ {#eq-pred-suff}
where $S(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n)$ is a predictive sufficient statistic that reduces the dimensionality of the conditioning set from $n$ observations to a fixed-dimensional summary.

@ressel1985finettitype proved a refinement of de Finetti's theorem. Under measurability conditions on $(\beta, t)$ (specifically, that $t$ is a measurable map into a locally compact abelian semigroup with a separating dual), if the joint distribution of an exchangeable sequence factorizes as
$$
P(Z_1 = z_1, \ldots, Z_n = z_n) = \prod_{j=1}^n \beta(z_j) \, \varphi\!\left(\sum_{j=1}^n t(z_j)\right)
$$
for mappings $\beta$ and $\varphi$, then the sequence is a unique mixture of i.i.d. sequences from a *generalized exponential family* determined by $(\beta, t)$. The sufficient statistic is $\sum_{j=1}^n t(z_j)$, and the mixing measure on the dual semigroup is the implicit prior. Depending on the form of $t$, the representation characterizes mixtures of specific parametric families: normal (with $t(z) = (z, z^2)$), Poisson (with $t(z) = z$), gamma, and so on. See @ressel1985finettitype for the precise semigroup conditions and @fortini2025exchangeability for a modern treatment.

Ressel's theorem runs the logic of Bayesian inference in reverse. In standard Bayesian analysis, we *choose* a parametric family (say, normal) and then derive the sufficient statistic ($\bar{y}$ and $s^2$). Ressel's theorem says that if we observe a particular *form* of predictive sufficiency in the data, if the predictive distribution depends on the data only through a particular type of summary, then the underlying mixing measure *must* correspond to a specific parametric family. The sufficient statistic determines the model, not the other way around. This is why our framework can be "prior-free" in the operational sense: the form of the summary $S$ implicitly selects the class of mixing distributions, and the quantile transport map $Q$ encodes the resulting predictive distribution without ever explicitly specifying a prior or likelihood.

@fortini2025exchangeability provide a comprehensive review of these predictive characterizations, formalizing the principle that Bayesian predictive rules are probabilistic *learning rules*: the predictive distribution $P_n$ converges almost surely to the directing random measure $\tilde{F}$, simultaneously with the empirical distribution. The statistical model is the *limit of the predictive rule*, one need not specify it in advance. @fortini2017predictive extend this program to Markov exchangeable processes.

### From Predictive Sufficiency to Quantile Transport {#sec-pred-transport}

Under exchangeability and predictive sufficiency through $S$, there *exists* a parameter $\theta$, a prior $\pi(\theta)$, and a likelihood $p(z \mid \theta)$ such that
$$
p(Y_{n+1} \mid x_{n+1}, Z_{1:n}) = \int p(Y_{n+1} \mid \theta, x_{n+1}) \, \pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n}) \, d\theta.
$$
One could proceed via Bayes' theorem: compute the posterior $\pi(\theta \mid Z_{1:n})$ and integrate. But this requires specifying $p$ and $\pi$ explicitly and computing the integral.

Our approach bypasses all of this. The noise outsourcing theorem (Theorem 4.1 from @sec-noise-outsourcing) guarantees that there exists a measurable function $Q$ such that
$$
Y_{n+1} = Q\!\left(\tau, \; x_{n+1}, \; S(Z_{1:n})\right), \quad \tau \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1),
$$ {#eq-pred-transport}
where $Q$ is the conditional quantile function of $Y_{n+1}$ given the covariate and the sufficient summary. We learn $Q$ via a quantile neural network from simulated data.

The method is *likelihood-free* and *prior-free* in the operational sense: we never specify $p(z \mid \theta)$ or $\pi(\theta)$. All we need is the ability to *simulate* sequences $(Z_1, \ldots, Z_n, Z_{n+1})$ from a forward generative model, from which we extract training tuples $(x_{n+1}, S(Z_{1:n}), Y_{n+1})$. The de Finetti--Ressel theory guarantees that an implicit prior and likelihood *exist* and could in principle be recovered from the learned quantile map, but we need not do so.

The generative Bayesian prediction algorithm follows the GBC template (Algorithm 10.2 in @sec-bi-algorithm), adapted for the predictive setting:

1. *Simulate.* Generate $m$ exchangeable sequences $(Z_1^{(j)}, \ldots, Z_n^{(j)}, Z_{n+1}^{(j)})$ for $j = 1, \ldots, m$ from a forward model. Extract training tuples $(x_{n+1}^{(j)}, s^{(j)}, y_{n+1}^{(j)})$ where $s^{(j)} = S(Z_{1:n}^{(j)})$.

2. *Summarize.* Compute a low-dimensional summary $s = f(x_{n+1}, Z_{1:n}) \in \mathbb{R}^k$ where $k$ is the effective dimension. The summary can be hand-crafted (moments, quantiles, autocorrelations) or learned (via a neural network trained to approximate the posterior mean, as in @jiang2017learning).

3. *Train.* Learn $\hat{Q}(\tau, s)$ by minimizing the integrated pinball loss over the training tuples:
$$
\hat{Q} = \arg\min_{Q \in \mathcal{Q}} \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \mathbb{E}_\tau\!\left[\rho_\tau\!\left(y_{n+1}^{(j)} - Q(\tau, s^{(j)})\right)\right],
$$
where $\rho_\tau$ is the pinball loss (@eq-check-loss) and $\mathcal{Q}$ is the class of quantile neural networks.

4. *Predict.* At test time, given observed data $z_{\text{obs}} = (z_1, \ldots, z_n)$ and a new covariate $x_{n+1}$:
   - Compute the summary: $s_{\text{obs}} = S(z_{\text{obs}})$.
   - Draw $\tau_1, \ldots, \tau_B \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$.
   - Return predictive samples $\{\hat{Q}(\tau_b, s_{\text{obs}})\}_{b=1}^B$.

At prediction time, each sample requires only a forward pass through the neural network, constant-time computation that contrasts with MCMC or kernel methods.

The algorithm has a clear division of labor between offline and online computation. The offline phase (steps 1--3) is expensive, generating $m$ training sequences and training a neural network can take hours for complex simulators. But this cost is paid once. The online phase (step 4) is cheap: given new data, computing the summary $s_{\text{obs}}$ and evaluating the network takes milliseconds. This amortization is the same principle behind simulation-based inference [@cranmer2020frontier] and neural posterior estimation, but applied to the predictive distribution rather than the parameter posterior. For applications where predictions must be made in real time, financial trading, autonomous driving, clinical decision support, the amortized cost structure is essential.

### Normal-Normal Learning and Wang Distortions {#sec-pred-wang}

To make the framework concrete, consider the normal-normal conjugate model from @sec-bi-normal. The prior-to-posterior update is a Wang distortion (@eq-wang-distortion): the posterior CDF is obtained by composing the prior CDF with a data-dependent distortion function $g$. In the predictive setting, the same distortion applies, but the predictive variance includes an additional $\sigma^2$ term (the observation noise), so the predictive distribution is $N(\mu_*, \sigma^2 + \sigma_*^2)$ rather than $N(\mu_*, \sigma_*^2)$.

The GBC framework learns this distortion from simulated data, without ever being told the parametric form.

## Minimax Rates and Compositional Structure {#sec-pred-minimax}

How many training samples $m$ does the generative approach need? The answer depends on the *effective dimension* $k$ of the summary statistic, not the full input dimension $d$.

We begin with the baseline: classical kernel methods. These estimators are well understood and minimax optimal for smooth functions, but they pay a heavy price as the input dimension grows.

Consider a $\beta$-Holder smooth conditional quantile function $Q_\tau(x)$ with $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The classic Nadaraya--Watson kernel estimator achieves the minimax optimal rate:
$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[|Q_\tau(x) - \hat{Q}_\tau(x)|^2\right] = O\!\left(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta + d)}\right).
$$ {#eq-pred-kernel-rate}
The optimal bandwidth is $h^* \propto m^{-1/(2\beta+d)}$, balancing bias $O(h^{2\beta})$ and variance $O((mh^d)^{-1})$.

As $d$ grows, this rate deteriorates rapidly. For $\beta = 2$ and $d = 20$, the rate is $O(m^{-0.17})$, one would need millions of samples for even modest accuracy. This is the curse of dimensionality: kernel methods must smooth over the full $d$-dimensional input space.

Suppose the true quantile function admits a compositional structure
$$
Q(\tau, z) = g(\tau, f(z)),
$$
where $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^k$ is a summary map with $k \ll n$, and $g : [0,1] \times \mathbb{R}^k \to \mathbb{R}$. This arises naturally when sufficient statistics or learned embeddings of the history capture the relevant information for prediction.

The following result shows that neural networks can exploit this compositional structure to achieve rates that depend on $k$ rather than $d$. The layered architecture of a deep network can learn the summary map $f$ and the quantile map $g$ separately, each in its own low-dimensional space, rather than approximating the full composition in the high-dimensional input space.

::: {.callout-tip appearance="simple" title="Proposition 11.2: Minimax Rate for Generative Quantile Prediction"}
Let $f \in \mathcal{H}^\beta(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{R}^k)$ and $g \in \mathcal{H}^\beta([0,1] \times \mathbb{R}^k, \mathbb{R})$ be $\beta$-Holder smooth maps ($\beta > 0$) so that the target quantile function factorizes as $Q(\tau, z) = g(\tau, f(z))$. Let $\hat{Q}$ be a ReLU network estimator with depth $L = O(\log m)$ layers and width $W = O(m / \log m)$ weights, trained on $m$ i.i.d. tuples $(Z_i, \tau_i, Q(\tau_i, Z_i))$ drawn from $P_Z \otimes \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$. Then, with the expectation over the training sample,
$$
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_0^1 \int |\hat{Q}(\tau, s) - Q(\tau, s)|^2 \, dP_S(s) \, d\tau\right] = O\!\left(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta + k + 1)}\right),
$$ {#eq-pred-minimax}
where $P_S$ is the distribution of $S = f(Z)$ and $k = \dim(S)$. This rate is minimax optimal over the compositional function class $\{g \circ f : f \in \mathcal{H}^\beta(\mathbb{R}^n, \mathbb{R}^k),\, g \in \mathcal{H}^\beta([0,1] \times \mathbb{R}^k, \mathbb{R})\}$.
:::

The result follows from the work of @schmidt-hieber2020nonparametric and @schmidt-hieber2024generative. Each layer of the ReLU network can approximate one component of the composition, so the approximation complexity depends on the dimension $k$ of the intermediate representation, not the full input dimension $d$.

The practical message: invest in finding good summary statistics. The convergence rate depends on $k$, not on $d$, so a well-chosen low-dimensional summary $S$ can make the difference between a method that needs thousands of training samples and one that needs millions. In the extreme case where $k = 1$ (the entire history is compressed into a single number), the rate is $O(m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+2)})$, which for $\beta = 2$ gives $O(m^{-2/3})$, close to the parametric rate. Of course, compressing to $k = 1$ may lose important information, so the choice of $k$ involves a bias-variance tradeoff that we discuss below.

*Why composition matters.* ReLU networks exploit compositional structure through their layered architecture. Each layer learns a component of the composition, so the approximation complexity depends on $k$ rather than $d$. Kernel methods, by contrast, treat the input as a flat vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$ and cannot exploit such structure. The dimension gap between $k$ and $d$ determines the practical advantage of neural networks over kernel methods.

To make this concrete, consider a prediction problem with $d = 20$ input lags, smoothness $\beta = 2$, and $k = 3$ summary statistics (mean, variance, last value):

| Method | Effective dimension | Rate | Value at $m = 1000$ |
|-----|----------|---|------------|
| Kernel ($d = 20$) | 20 | $m^{-4/24} = m^{-0.17}$ | 0.24 |
| Neural net ($k = 3$) | 3 | $m^{-4/8} = m^{-0.50}$ | 0.03 |

: Convergence rates for kernel vs. neural network quantile estimators. {#tbl-pred-rates}

The neural network is substantially more sample-efficient. At $m = 1000$ training samples, the neural network's $L^2$ error is roughly 8 times smaller than the kernel's. This gap widens as $d$ increases relative to $k$.

The convergence rate depends on $k$, so choosing a good summary $S$ is essential. The simplest option is to use hand-crafted summaries, moments, quantiles, or autocorrelations of the data, which are interpretable but may miss relevant features. When the process has short memory, a fixed window of the last $k$ observations is often effective. For more demanding problems, one can train a neural network $\hat{S}(Z_{1:n}) \approx \mathbb{E}[\theta \mid Z_{1:n}]$ to learn the summary jointly with the quantile predictor, as proposed by @jiang2017learning; the posterior mean is Bayes-optimal as a sufficient statistic.

The choice of $k$ involves a bias--variance tradeoff: small $k$ yields fast convergence but risks information loss, while large $k$ preserves information at the cost of slower rates. In practice, cross-validation on the pinball loss provides a principled way to select $k$: for each candidate summary dimension, we train the quantile network, evaluate the out-of-sample pinball loss, and choose the $k$ that minimizes it. If the selected $k$ is much smaller than $d$, this confirms that the problem has low intrinsic dimensionality and the neural network approach will be advantageous.

What Proposition 11.2 does *not* say is also important. It does not say that neural networks are always better than kernel methods. When $k = d$ (no low-dimensional structure exists), the kernel rate $m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)}$ is actually slightly better than the neural network rate $m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d+1)}$, because the neural network must also handle the additional $\tau$ input. The advantage of neural networks is conditional on the existence of compositional structure, and the magnitude of the advantage is proportional to the gap between $d$ and $k$. Before reaching for a deep network, ask whether your prediction problem has a natural low-dimensional summary. If it does, the theory predicts, and our simulation confirms, that neural networks will be substantially more efficient.


## Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI) {#sec-pred-ppi}

A distinct but related approach to leveraging machine learning for valid prediction is *prediction-powered inference* (PPI) of @angelopoulos2023predictionpowered. PPI addresses a setting where we have a small labeled dataset $(X_i, Y_i)_{i=1}^n$ with both features and responses, alongside a large unlabeled dataset $(\tilde{X}_i)_{i=1}^N$ with $N \gg n$ where we can evaluate a pre-trained predictor $f(\tilde{X}_i)$ but do not observe $Y$.

The goal is to estimate a population parameter $\theta^* = \mathbb{E}[Y]$ (or more generally, the solution to an estimating equation) with valid confidence intervals.

The PPI estimator combines the ML predictions on the large dataset with a bias correction from the small labeled dataset:
$$
\hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(\tilde{X}_i)}_{\hat{\theta}^f} \; - \; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(f(X_i) - Y_i\big)}_{\hat{\Delta}}.
$$ {#eq-pred-ppi}

The first term $\hat{\theta}^f$ is the ML-based estimate using all $N$ unlabeled points. The second term $\hat{\Delta}$ corrects for the bias of $f$ using the labeled data. The estimator has three key properties. First, it is unbiased: $\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}}] = \theta^*$ regardless of the quality of $f$. Second, when $f$ is a good predictor $\hat{\Delta} \approx 0$ and $\hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}} \approx \hat{\theta}^f$, with variance reduced by a factor of roughly $n/N$. Third, if $f$ is a poor predictor, $\hat{\Delta}$ corrects the bias and we pay only the cost of estimating the bias from $n$ labeled points.

Since $\hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}}$ is a sum of two independent terms (one from the unlabeled data, one from the labeled data), valid confidence intervals follow from standard CLT arguments:
$$
\hat{\theta}^{\text{PP}} \pm z_{1-\alpha/2} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{\hat{\sigma}_f^2}{N} + \frac{\hat{\sigma}_\Delta^2}{n}},
$$
where $\hat{\sigma}_f^2$ and $\hat{\sigma}_\Delta^2$ are the sample variances of the two components. See also @zrnic2024crosspredictionpowered for a cross-fitted variant.

PPI and GBC share a common philosophy: both use ML predictions to improve statistical inference while maintaining validity guarantees. Concretely, PPI uses ML predictions as a *control variate* for estimation, achieving valid inference through bias correction, while GBC uses neural networks to learn the *full predictive distribution*, achieving valid prediction through quantile transport. Both approaches recognize that black-box ML predictions contain useful information but are not, by themselves, statistically valid. PPI corrects the bias of point predictions; GBC calibrates quantile predictions through the pinball loss and (optionally) conformal post-processing. The approaches are complementary: one could use a GBC quantile network as the predictor $f$ within the PPI framework.

### PPI Worked Example: House Price Prediction {#sec-pred-ppi-example}

To make PPI concrete, we work through a synthetic house price example. Suppose we want to estimate the average house price in a city. We have a small labeled dataset of $n = 500$ houses where both features (square footage, number of bedrooms, lot size) and sale prices are observed, and a large unlabeled dataset of $N = 10{,}000$ houses where only features are available. A pre-trained gradient boosting model $f$ can predict prices from features, but its predictions are systematically biased, perhaps it was trained on data from a different city or a different time period.

The classical approach uses only the $n = 500$ labeled observations: $\hat{\theta}_n = \bar{Y} = n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n Y_i$. The PPI approach uses both datasets, applying the bias correction in @eq-pred-ppi. The key question is: how much does the unlabeled data help?

## When GBC Prediction Excels: A Heteroscedastic AR(1) Example {#sec-pred-success}

Before the broader comparison study, a single clean example makes the main point concrete. Consider an AR(1) process with state-dependent variance:
$$
Y_t = 0.8\, Y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}\!\bigl(0,\; 0.1 + 0.4 Y_{t-1}^2\bigr).
$$ {#eq-pred-ar1-hetero}

The conditional distribution $Y_t \mid Y_{t-1}$ is Gaussian with state-dependent variance. A homoscedastic predictor (one that assumes constant variance) will produce prediction intervals that are too narrow in high-variance states (say $|Y_{t-1}| \approx 2$) and too wide in low-variance states ($Y_{t-1} \approx 0$). GBC, which learns the full conditional quantile function without any distributional assumption, adapts automatically.

```python
        np.random.seed(seed)
    y = np.zeros(T + 1)
    for t in range(1, T + 1):
        sig = np.sqrt(0.1 + 0.4 * y[t-1]**2)

xm_p, xs_p = X_pred.mean(), X_pred.std()
ym_p, ys_p = Y_pred.mean(), Y_pred.std()
Xn_p = (X_pred - xm_p) / xs_p
Yn_p = (Y_pred - ym_p) / ys_p

class SmallIQN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, xdim=1, hdim=64, M=32):
        super().__init__()
        self.M = M
        self.fc_tau = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(M, hdim), nn.ReLU())
        self.fc_x = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(xdim, hdim), nn.ReLU())
        self.head = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(hdim, hdim), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(hdim, 1))
    def forward(self, x, tau):
        i = torch.arange(self.M, dtype=torch.float32)
        h_tau = self.fc_tau(torch.cos(i * torch.pi * tau))
        h_x = self.fc_x(x)
        h = h_x * h_tau.unsqueeze(0) if h_tau.dim() == 1 else h_x * h_tau
        return self.head(h)

model_p = SmallIQN()
opt_p = torch.optim.Adam(model_p.parameters(), lr=3e-4)
for _ in range(600):
    idx = torch.randperm(T_train)[:256]
    x_b, y_b = Xn_p[idx], Yn_p[idx]
    tau = torch.rand(256, 1)
    r = y_b - model_p(x_b, tau)
    loss = torch.mean(torch.maximum(tau * r, (tau - 1) * r))
    opt_p.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt_p.step()

# Test on a fresh trajectory of length 200
model_p.eval()
T_test = 200
y_test = ar1_simulate(T_test + 1, seed=77)
x_test_vals = y_test[:-1]
y_test_vals = y_test[1:]

lo_band, hi_band = [], []
with torch.no_grad():
    for xv in x_test_vals:
        z = torch.tensor([[(xv - xm_p.item()) / xs_p.item()]], dtype=torch.float32)
        lo = model_p(z, torch.tensor([[0.10]])).item() * ys_p.item() + ym_p.item()
        hi = model_p(z, torch.tensor([[0.90]])).item() * ys_p.item() + ym_p.item()
        lo_band.append(lo); hi_band.append(hi)

lo_band, hi_band = np.array(lo_band), np.array(hi_band)
coverage = np.mean((y_test_vals >= lo_band) & (y_test_vals <= hi_band))

t_idx = np.arange(T_test)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(11, 4))
ax.fill_between(t_idx, lo_band, hi_band, alpha=0.3, color='steelblue',
                label=f'10%--90% GBC band (coverage {coverage:.1%})')
ax.scatter(t_idx, y_test_vals, s=4, color='k', alpha=0.5, label='Observed $Y_t$')
ax.set_xlabel('Time step $t$'); ax.set_ylabel('$Y_t$')
ax.legend(fontsize=9); ax.set_title('GBC prediction intervals — AR(1) with state-dependent variance')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
```

The 80% band achieves approximately 80% empirical coverage (@fig-pred-ar1-success). More importantly, the band adapts to the local variance: it is wide when $|Y_{t-1}|$ is large and narrow when $Y_{t-1} \approx 0$, as the model requires. A homoscedastic interval ($\pm 1.28\hat{\sigma}$ for a fixed $\hat{\sigma}$) would be systematically miscalibrated in both directions simultaneously.

This is the setting where GBC prediction excels: a process with well-defined conditional quantile function structure, enough training data, and moderate signal-to-noise ratio. Chapter 14 documents the setting where GBC fails (high signal-to-noise, slowly varying mean dominating the variance), and the diagnostics that distinguish the two regimes before deployment.


## Empirical: Neural vs Kernel Prediction {#sec-pred-empirical}

We illustrate the theoretical rate advantages with a simulation study comparing kernel and neural network quantile estimators on an AR(2) process with heteroscedastic errors:
$$
X_t = 0.5 X_{t-1} - 0.3 X_{t-2} + \epsilon_t, \quad \epsilon_t \sim N(0, \sigma_t^2),
$$
where $\sigma_t = (0.5 + 0.3 X_{t-1}^2 + 0.2 X_{t-2}^2)^{1/2}$. The conditional quantile function depends on $(X_{t-1}, X_{t-2})$ only, so the intrinsic dimension is 2, but we provide $d = 20$ lags as input to test how each method handles the extraneous dimensions.

*Setup.* We generate $m = 1000$ training sequences of length 50 and use the last $d = 20$ lags as history. The neural network receives three summary statistics ($k = 3$): sample mean, standard deviation, and last value. The kernel method uses a Nadaraya--Watson estimator with Gaussian kernel on the full $d = 20$ lag vector. Both methods estimate quantile levels $\tau \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9\}$ jointly.

```python
#| fig-cap: "Comparison of local kernel ($d=20$) and ReLU network ($k=3$) quantile estimators on the AR(2) heteroscedastic process. Left: pinball loss at each quantile level. Right: empirical coverage vs. nominal level; the dashed line indicates ideal calibration."
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

np.random.seed(42)

def generate_ar2_data(n_sequences, seq_length, n_lags=20):
    """Generate AR(2) heteroscedastic data."""
    X_all, Y_all, S_all = [], [], []
    for _ in range(n_sequences):
        x = np.zeros(seq_length + n_lags + 2)
        x[:2] = np.random.randn(2)
        for t in range(2, len(x)):
            sig = np.sqrt(0.5 + 0.3 * x[t-1]**2 + 0.2 * x[t-2]**2)
            x[t] = 0.5 * x[t-1] - 0.3 * x[t-2] + sig * np.random.randn()
        # Last n_lags values as features, next value as target
        lags = x[-(n_lags+1):-1]
        target = x[-1]
        # Summary statistics
        summary = np.array([lags.mean(), lags.std(), lags[-1]])
        X_all.append(lags)
        Y_all.append(target)
        S_all.append(summary)
    return np.array(X_all), np.array(Y_all), np.array(S_all)

# Generate data
X_tr, Y_tr, S_tr = generate_ar2_data(300, 50)
X_te, Y_te, S_te = generate_ar2_data(100, 50)

tau_levels = np.array([0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9])

# --- Kernel method (Nadaraya-Watson on full d=20 lags) ---
def kernel_quantile(X_tr, Y_tr, X_te, tau, bandwidth=None):
    """Nadaraya-Watson kernel quantile estimator."""
    n, d = X_tr.shape
    if bandwidth is None:
        bandwidth = 1.06 * X_tr.std(axis=0).mean() * n ** (-1.0 / (d + 4))  # Silverman
    preds = []
    for x in X_te:
        dists = np.sum((X_tr - x)**2, axis=1)
        weights = np.exp(-dists / (2 * bandwidth**2))
        weights /= weights.sum() + 1e-10
        # Weighted quantile
        sorted_idx = np.argsort(Y_tr)
        cum_w = np.cumsum(weights[sorted_idx])
        idx = np.searchsorted(cum_w, tau)
        idx = min(idx, len(Y_tr) - 1)
        preds.append(Y_tr[sorted_idx[idx]])
    return np.array(preds)

# --- Neural network (simple 3-layer MLP on k=3 summaries) ---
# We use a simple numpy implementation for reproducibility
class SimpleQNN:
    def __init__(self, input_dim, hidden_dims=[128, 128, 64], lr=0.005):
        self.layers = []
        dims = [input_dim + 1] + hidden_dims + [1]  # +1 for tau input
        for i in range(len(dims) - 1):
            W = np.random.randn(dims[i], dims[i+1]) * np.sqrt(2.0 / dims[i])
            b = np.zeros(dims[i+1])
            self.layers.append((W, b))
        self.lr = lr

    def forward(self, x, tau_val):
        """Forward pass with tau concatenated to input."""
        h = np.concatenate([x, np.full((x.shape[0], 1), tau_val)], axis=1)
        self.activations = [h]
        for i, (W, b) in enumerate(self.layers):
            h = h @ W + b
            if i < len(self.layers) - 1:
                h = np.maximum(h, 0)  # ReLU
            self.activations.append(h)
        return h.ravel()

    def train(self, X, Y, epochs=200, batch_size=128):
        n = X.shape[0]
        for epoch in range(epochs):
            perm = np.random.permutation(n)
            for start in range(0, n, batch_size):
                idx = perm[start:start+batch_size]
                tau_val = np.random.uniform(0.05, 0.95)
                pred = self.forward(X[idx], tau_val)
                residual = Y[idx] - pred
                # Pinball loss gradient
                grad = np.where(residual >= 0, -tau_val, -(tau_val - 1))
                # Full backpropagation through all layers
                delta = grad.reshape(-1, 1)
                for i in range(len(self.layers) - 1, -1, -1):
                    h_in = self.activations[i]
                    W, b = self.layers[i]
                    dW = h_in.T @ delta / delta.shape[0]
                    db = delta.mean(axis=0)
                    self.layers[i] = (W - self.lr * dW, b - self.lr * db)
                    if i > 0:
                        delta = delta @ W.T
                        delta = delta * (self.activations[i] > 0)  # ReLU gradient

    def predict(self, X, tau_val):
        return self.forward(X, tau_val)

# Train QNN
qnn = SimpleQNN(input_dim=3, hidden_dims=[128, 128, 64])
qnn.train(S_tr, Y_tr, epochs=100)

# Evaluate
pinball_kernel = {tau: [] for tau in tau_levels}

    pinball_kernel[tau] = loss_k.mean()

    # NN predictions
```

In @fig-pred-simulation, the neural network (coral) achieves lower pinball loss than the kernel method (blue) across all quantile levels, and its coverage tracks the ideal diagonal more closely. The kernel method, operating on the full $d = 20$ lag vector, suffers from the curse of dimensionality: it must smooth over a 20-dimensional space, effectively averaging over irrelevant dimensions. The neural network, operating on $k = 3$ summary statistics, exploits the low-dimensional compositional structure of the quantile function.

The theoretical rates from Proposition 11.2 predict this outcome: the neural network converges at rate $m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+k+1)} = m^{-0.50}$ (with $\beta = 2$, $k = 3$), while the kernel converges at rate $m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+d)} = m^{-0.17}$ (with $d = 20$). The practical advantage tracks the theoretical gap.

One caveat in this comparison: the kernel method operates on the raw 20-dimensional
lag vector while the neural network operates on
3 hand-crafted summary statistics. Part of the kernel's
disadvantage reflects operating in a higher-dimensional
space rather than a deficiency of the kernel method
itself. A fairer comparison would give the kernel
the same 3 summary statistics as input. In that case
the kernel rate would improve to
$m^{-2\beta/(2\beta+k)} = m^{-4/7} \approx m^{-0.57}$,
which is slightly better than the neural network rate
of $m^{-0.50}$ (the neural network pays for the
additional $\tau$ input). The neural network's real
advantage is its ability to *learn* the summary jointly
with the quantile function when hand-crafted summaries
are not available.
