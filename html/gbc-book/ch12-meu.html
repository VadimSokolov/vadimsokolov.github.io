“The idea of maximizing expected utility is the cornerstone of rational decision-making under uncertainty.” – Leonard Savage

The previous chapters developed the GBC machinery for posterior inference and prediction. We learned how to simulate from priors and likelihoods, train quantile neural networks, and construct posterior distributions—all without evaluating densities. But inference is rarely the final goal. We observe data to make decisions.

A doctor interprets diagnostic results to choose a treatment. A portfolio manager reads market data to allocate capital. An engineer analyzes sensor readings to set control parameters. In each case, the statistical model is a means to an end: optimal action under uncertainty.

The classical framework for optimal decision-making is maximum expected utility (MEU). Given a utility function \(U(d, \theta)\) defined over decisions \(d\) and unknown parameters \(\theta\), the Bayesian decision rule is \[ d^\star(y) = \arg\max_d \; \mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}[U(d, \theta)] = \arg\max_d \int U(d, \theta) \, p(\theta \mid y) \, d\theta. \] The standard approach computes the posterior \(p(\theta \mid y)\) first and then evaluates the integral by Monte Carlo. This two-step procedure is wasteful. Monte Carlo requires many samples in regions of high posterior probability, but utility functions often place high weight on tail events—rare but consequential outcomes like portfolio crashes or treatment failures. The expected utility integral and the posterior integral have different structures, and solving one does not efficiently solve the other.

This chapter shows that GBC provides a more direct path. A classical identity connects expected utility to quantile functions: \[ \mathbb{E}[U] = \int_0^1 F_U^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau. \tag{12.1}\] The expected utility is the area under the quantile function of the utility random variable. If we can learn the quantile function of the utility distribution, we can compute expected utility by simple numerical integration—no density evaluation required.

We develop this idea in several steps. Section 12.1 establishes the quantile identity and its connection to Lorenz curves and the Gini coefficient. Section 12.2 introduces Yaari’s dual theory, which replaces outcome distortion with probability distortion. Section 12.3 shows that Bayesian updating itself can be viewed as a Wang distortion, connecting prior-to-posterior quantile maps to risk measures from finance. Section 12.4 develops the data augmentation trick that enables efficient computation. Section 12.5 presents the complete GenBayes-MEU algorithm. Section 12.6 applies the method to portfolio optimization. Section 12.7 walks through a pharmaceutical go/no-go decision. Section 12.8 connects CVaR and Wang distortions to insurance pricing. Section 12.9 discusses further applications in economics and risk management.

12.1 Expected Utility and Quantiles

We begin with the identity that makes density-free expected utility computation possible. The connection runs through two classical objects from economics: the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient.

Let \(U\) be a non-negative random variable with CDF \(F_U\) and finite mean \(\mu = \mathbb{E}[U]\). The quantile function \(F_U^{-1}(\tau) = \inf\{u : F_U(u) \geq \tau\}\) provides a complete description of the distribution of \(U\). The following identity expresses the expectation as an integral over quantiles.

Instead of integrating the random variable against its density—which requires knowing the density—we integrate the quantile function over the unit interval. The quantile function is a non-decreasing rearrangement of the random variable, and its integral over \([0,1]\) recovers the mean. For decision problems, this means we can compute expected utility by evaluating quantiles at a grid of probability levels and averaging. No likelihood, no normalizing constant, no density estimation.

TipProposition 12.1: Quantile Representation of Expectations

For any random variable \(U\) with \(\mathbb{E}[|U|] < \infty\), \[ \mathbb{E}[U] = \int_0^1 F_U^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau. \tag{12.2}\]

Proof. Apply the change of variable \(\tau = F_U(u)\), so \(d\tau = f_U(u) \, du\) and \(u = F_U^{-1}(\tau)\): \[ \int_0^1 F_U^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} u \, f_U(u) \, du = \mathbb{E}[U]. \] For the non-negative case, an alternative proof uses the survival function \(S_U(u) = 1 - F_U(u) = P(U > u)\) and the well-known identity \(\mathbb{E}[U] = \int_0^\infty S_U(u) \, du\). \(\square\)

This identity is sometimes called the quantile representation of expectations. The mean of a distribution equals the area under its quantile function. Since the quantile function is non-decreasing, the trapezoidal rule is particularly accurate: the integrand has no oscillations, no sign changes, and bounded curvature for well-behaved distributions.

For decision problems, the identity takes the form \[ \mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}[U(d, \theta)] = \int_0^1 F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau, \tag{12.3}\] where \(F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau)\) is the quantile function of the random utility \(U_d = U(d, \theta)\) with \(\theta \sim p(\theta \mid y)\). Rather than computing the posterior density and then integrating the utility against it, we learn the quantile function of the utility distribution directly.

The quantile function of the utility can be learned from simulated data. We do not need to derive the distribution of \(U(d, \theta)\) analytically. We simply simulate many \((\theta, y)\) pairs from the prior-predictive, compute the corresponding utilities, sort them, and the sorted values approximate the quantile function at equally spaced probability levels.

The Lorenz curve. The Lorenz curve (Lorenz 1905), originally introduced to measure income inequality, provides a normalized version of the quantile identity. For a non-negative random variable \(U\) with mean \(\mu > 0\), the Lorenz curve is \[ \mathcal{L}(p) = \frac{1}{\mu} \int_0^p F_U^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau, \quad p \in [0, 1]. \tag{12.4}\]

The Lorenz curve maps the cumulative share of the population (\(p\)) to the cumulative share of the total (\(\mathcal{L}(p)\)). If \(U\) represents income, then \(\mathcal{L}(0.5) = 0.3\) means the bottom 50% of earners receive 30% of total income.

The Lorenz curve has several key properties. It satisfies \(\mathcal{L}(0) = 0\) and \(\mathcal{L}(1) = 1\), and it is convex because the quantile function is non-decreasing, so the integrand is non-decreasing. When \(U\) is constant (no uncertainty), \(\mathcal{L}(p) = p\), which gives the line of perfect equality. Finally, the area under \(\mathcal{L}\) relates directly to \(\mathbb{E}[U]\): since \(\mu \cdot \mathcal{L}(1) = \int_0^1 F_U^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau = \mu\), the identity is self-consistent.

The Gini coefficient. The Gini coefficient (Gini 1912) measures the deviation of the Lorenz curve from the line of equality: \[ G = 2 \int_0^1 \left(p - \mathcal{L}(p)\right) dp = 1 - 2 \int_0^1 \mathcal{L}(p) \, dp. \tag{12.5}\]

The Gini coefficient satisfies \(0 \leq G \leq 1\), where \(G = 0\) corresponds to perfect equality (all outcomes identical) and \(G = 1\) to maximal inequality. In the context of utility distributions, the Gini coefficient measures how “spread out” the utility random variable is. A decision with low Gini coefficient has more predictable utility—less risk.

Numerical computation. The quantile identity Eq. 12.2 converts expected utility computation into numerical integration of a monotone function on \([0, 1]\). Yakowitz (1978) showed that the trapezoidal rule applied to quantile functions achieves an \(O(N^{-4})\) mean-squared error rate—far better than the \(O(N^{-1})\) rate of standard Monte Carlo.

The gain is substantial. To achieve four-digit accuracy, standard Monte Carlo requires roughly \(10^8\) samples (\(N^{-1/2} \approx 10^{-4}\)), whereas the quantile trapezoidal rule needs only about \(N \approx 32\) samples (\(N^{-2} \approx 10^{-3}\), or more precisely, the constant in the \(O(N^{-4})\) bound is small for smooth quantile functions). In practice, we use \(K = 100\) to \(500\) quantile evaluations, which provides far more accuracy than millions of Monte Carlo draws.

TipProposition 12.2: Trapezoidal Rule for Quantile Expectations (Yakowitz, 1978)

Let \(Q(\tau)\) be a quantile function with continuous second derivative on \([0, 1]\), and let \(U_{(1)} \leq U_{(2)} \leq \cdots \leq U_{(N)}\) be the order statistics of \(N\) independent uniform samples on \([0, 1]\), with \(U_{(0)} = 0\) and \(U_{(N+1)} = 1\). Then the trapezoidal estimator \[ \hat{\mu}_N = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=0}^{N} \left[Q(U_{(i)}) + Q(U_{(i+1)})\right] (U_{(i+1)} - U_{(i)}) \] satisfies \(\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\mu}_N - \mu)^2] \leq M / N^4\) for some constant \(M\).

The proof uses the classical error bound for the trapezoidal rule on each subinterval, together with moment bounds on the spacings of uniform order statistics. The key observation is that \(\sum_{i} Z_i^3\), where \(Z_i = U_{(i)} - U_{(i-1)}\) are the spacings, has moments that decay as \(N^{-4}\) in the squared sum. Specifically, one shows that \(|\hat{\mu}_N - \mu| \leq \frac{C}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{N+1} Z_i^3\) where \(C\) bounds \(|Q''|\). Taking expectations and using the moment formulas for uniform spacings—\(\mathbb{E}[Z_i^6] = 6! \, n!/(n+6)!\) and \(\mathbb{E}[Z_i^3 Z_j^3] = (3!)^2 \, n!/(n+6)!\) for \(i \neq j\)—yields the \(O(N^{-4})\) bound.

In practice, we approximate the integral with a deterministic grid: \[ \mathbb{E}[U] \approx \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} F_U^{-1}\left(\frac{k - 0.5}{K}\right). \tag{12.6}\] This is simply the average of \(K\) equally spaced quantiles. For a smooth quantile function, \(K = 100\) typically suffices for four digits of accuracy.

Let us verify this with a simple example.

12.2 Yaari’s Dual Theory

The classical expected utility framework, due to Neumann and Morgenstern (1947) and axiomatized by Savage (1954), evaluates risky prospects by distorting outcomes through a utility function while keeping probabilities unchanged: \[ V(X) = \mathbb{E}[u(X)] = \int_0^\infty u(x) \, dF_X(x). \] Risk aversion is encoded in the concavity of \(u\): a concave \(u\) assigns diminishing marginal utility to increasingly large payoffs.

Yaari (1987) proposed a dual theory that reverses this logic. Instead of distorting payoffs while preserving probabilities, Yaari distorts probabilities while preserving payoffs. The dual functional is \[ \widetilde{V}(X) = \int_0^\infty g(S_X(u)) \, du, \tag{12.7}\] where \(S_X(u) = 1 - F_X(u) = P(X > u)\) is the survival function and \(g : [0,1] \to [0,1]\) is a distortion function satisfying \(g(0) = 0\) and \(g(1) = 1\).

A distortion function \(g\) transforms probabilities. If \(g\) is convex, then \(g(p) \leq p\) for \(p\) near 0 and \(g(p) \geq p\) for \(p\) near 1—the agent overweights the probability of bad outcomes (low survival) and underweights the probability of good outcomes (high survival). This generates risk-averse behavior without any concavity in the utility function.

Conversely, if \(g\) is concave, the agent overweights the probability of good outcomes—exhibiting risk-seeking behavior.

The duality between outcome distortion and probability distortion is central to Yaari’s theory. Given a utility function \(u\) with inverse \(u^{-1}\), we can define the equivalent distortion function: \[ g(p) = S_X\left(u^{-1}\left(S_X^{-1}(p)\right)\right). \] Under this choice of \(g\), the dual functional \(\widetilde{V}(X)\) produces the same ranking of gambles as \(V(X) = \mathbb{E}[u(X)]\). This means we can always move between the two representations.

This duality has a consequence for computation. In the standard framework, evaluating \(\mathbb{E}[u(X)]\) requires integrating the nonlinear function \(u\) against the density \(f_X\). In the dual framework, evaluating \(\widetilde{V}(X)\) requires integrating the nonlinear function \(g\) against the survival function \(S_X\). The dual form is more natural for quantile-based methods because it operates directly on probabilities rather than on transformed payoffs.

Using the change of variable \(u = F_X^{-1}(\tau)\), the dual functional Eq. 12.7 can be rewritten as \[ \widetilde{V}(X) = \int_0^1 F_X^{-1}(\tau) \cdot g'(1 - \tau) \, d\tau. \] When \(g\) is differentiable, the weight \(g'(1 - \tau)\) acts as a probability weighting function. If \(g'\) is non-decreasing (i.e., \(g\) is convex), higher quantiles receive more weight—reflecting risk aversion.

The special case \(g(p) = p\) (the identity distortion) gives \[ \widetilde{V}(X) = \int_0^1 F_X^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau = \mathbb{E}[X], \] which recovers the quantile identity from Eq. 12.2. The identity distortion corresponds to risk neutrality.

The dual theory provides the conceptual bridge between expected utility and quantile methods. In the standard framework, we need the posterior density \(p(\theta \mid y)\) to compute \(\mathbb{E}[u(\theta)]\). In the dual framework, we need the posterior quantile function \(F_{\theta \mid y}^{-1}(\tau)\) to compute \(\int g(S_{\theta|y}(u)) \, du\). Since GBC naturally produces quantile functions (via quantile neural networks), the dual framework is more compatible with our computational machinery.

Moreover, different distortion functions \(g\) correspond to different attitudes toward risk. By training a single quantile neural network that learns \(F_{U \mid d,y}^{-1}(\tau)\), we can evaluate the expected utility under any distortion function by simply reweighting the quantile integral. This flexibility is valuable in applications where risk preferences are uncertain or context-dependent.

12.3 Wang Distortion and Bayesian Updating

A connection exists between Bayesian updating and probability distortion. Wang (1996) and Wang (2000) introduced a parametric family of distortion functions: \[ g(p) = \Phi\left(\lambda_1 \Phi^{-1}(p) + \lambda_0\right), \tag{12.8}\] where \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF and \(\lambda_1 > 0\), \(\lambda_0 \in \mathbb{R}\) are parameters. This family is closed under composition, maps \([0,1]\) to \([0,1]\), and includes the identity (\(\lambda_1 = 1\), \(\lambda_0 = 0\)) as a special case.

In the normal-normal conjugate model, Bayesian updating is exactly a Wang distortion: the posterior CDF is \(g\) composed with the prior CDF, where \(\lambda_1 = \sigma_0/\sigma_n\) and \(\lambda_0 = (\mu_0 - \mu_n)/\sigma_n\) (see the derivation in Section 10.6, Eq. 10.5), with \(\sigma_0\) the prior std, \(\mu_n\) and \(\sigma_n\) the posterior mean and std. The distortion steepens the CDF when data are informative (\(\lambda_1 > 1\)) and shifts it when the posterior mean differs from the prior mean (\(\lambda_0 \neq 0\)). The IQN learns this distortion from simulated data.

Let us illustrate the Wang distortion for a concrete normal-normal model.

Figure 12.2 illustrates the Wang distortion for a normal-normal model with diffuse prior \(N(0, 25)\) and data strongly favoring \(\theta \approx 3\). The center panel shows the distortion function \(g(p)\) that transforms the prior CDF to the posterior CDF. The departure from the diagonal (identity) reflects the information content of the data. In the normal-normal model, the Wang distortion has a closed form. In general, the distortion that maps prior to posterior quantile functions must be learned. This is precisely the role of the quantile neural network in GBC. The network learns the transformation \[ F_{\theta \mid y}^{-1}(\tau) = Q_\theta\!\left(g^{-1}(\tau; y)\right), \] where \(g^{-1}(\cdot; y)\) is the data-dependent inverse distortion. The Wang distortion provides a useful parametric special case, but the neural network can represent arbitrary distortions—multimodal posteriors, skewed updates, and non-conjugate models that no parametric distortion family can capture.

12.4 Data Augmentation and Conditional Sufficient Statistics

The computational trick that makes GBC-MEU practical is augmenting the training data with independent uniform random variables.

Parzen (2004) observed a fundamental identity: for any random variable \(\theta\) with CDF \(F_\theta\) and quantile function \(Q_\theta\), \[ \theta \stackrel{d}{=} Q_\theta(F_\theta(\theta)). \tag{12.9}\] This is a tautology—applying the CDF and then the quantile function is the identity (in distribution). But it has a computational consequence: any random variable can be represented as a composition of two functions: the CDF (which maps to the uniform scale) and the quantile function (which maps back to the original scale).

Moreover, for monotone transformations \(h\), the quantile function has a compositional property: \[ Q_{h(\theta)}(\tau) = h(Q_\theta(\tau)). \] This means that quantile functions compose under monotone transformations—the same compositional structure exploited by deep neural networks with monotone activation functions.

The compositional property is what connects quantile methods to deep learning architectures. A deep network with monotone activations is, in effect, a sequence of quantile transformations. Each layer takes the quantile function from the previous layer and applies a learned monotone map. The utility function sits naturally as the outermost layer of this composition: given the posterior quantile \(Q_{\theta \mid y}(\tau)\), the utility quantile is \(Q_{U \mid d,y}(\tau) = U(d, Q_{\theta \mid y}(\tau))\) whenever \(U(d, \cdot)\) is monotone in \(\theta\).

The GBC framework exploits the identity Eq. 12.9 by introducing independent uniform random variables \(u^{(i)} \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\). Given simulated pairs \((\theta^{(i)}, y^{(i)})\) from the prior-predictive distribution, we construct the augmented training dataset \[ \{(\theta^{(i)}, y^{(i)}, u^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N. \] The noise outsourcing theorem (see Chapter 4) guarantees the existence of a function \(H\) such that \[ \theta \mid y \stackrel{d}{=} H(S(y), u), \] where \(S(y)\) is a sufficient statistic (or summary statistic performing dimensionality reduction) and \(u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\). The function \(H\) is precisely the posterior quantile function: \(H(S(y), \tau) = F_{\theta \mid y}^{-1}(\tau)\).

In many statistical models, the data \(y = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)\) is high-dimensional, but the posterior depends on \(y\) only through a low-dimensional sufficient statistic \(S(y)\). For the normal mean \(S(y) = \bar{y}\); for the normal variance \(S(y) = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2\); for the exponential rate and the binomial both reduce to \(S(y) = \sum y_i\).

When a sufficient statistic is available, the augmented representation becomes \[ \theta \mid y \stackrel{d}{=} H(S(y), u), \quad u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1). \] This dramatically reduces the dimensionality of the learning problem. The quantile neural network takes inputs \((S(y), \tau)\) rather than \((y_1, \ldots, y_n, \tau)\).

For models without known sufficient statistics, we can learn \(S\) using a deep neural network. Polson, Ruggeri, and Sokolov (2024) show that the summary statistic can be learned independently of the quantile map: a simple \(\ell_2\) regression of \(\theta\) on \(y\) provides a good approximation to the posterior mean, which often serves as a near-sufficient summary.

The augmentation extends to utility computations. Given the utility function \(U(d, \theta)\) and the posterior quantile map \(H(S(y), u)\), the distributional utility is \[ U_d \stackrel{d}{=} U(d, H(S(y), u)), \quad u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1). \] This is a composition: the utility function sits on top of the posterior quantile map. In neural network terms, the utility function acts as the first layer of the architecture, transforming the posterior parameter into a utility value. As Polson, Ruggeri, and Sokolov (2024) observe, rather than learning the posterior and then computing utility as a post-processing step, we embed the utility transformation directly into the network. The resulting architecture learns the utility quantile function end-to-end, avoiding the accumulation of errors from a two-stage procedure.

The augmented training dataset for MEU is then \[ \{(U_d^{(i)}, S(y^{(i)}), u^{(i)}, d^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N, \] where \(U_d^{(i)} = U(d^{(i)}, \theta^{(i)})\). The quantile neural network learns \[ F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau) \approx H_{\text{MEU}}(S(y), \tau, d), \] and the expected utility is computed via the trapezoidal rule: \[ \mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}[U(d, \theta)] \approx \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K H_{\text{MEU}}\!\left(S(y), \frac{k - 0.5}{K}, d\right). \]

12.5 The GenBayes-MEU Algorithm

We now assemble the pieces into a complete algorithm. Following the GBC template (Algorithm 10.2), but adapted for utility estimation:

ImportantAlgorithm: GenBayes-MEU

Input: Prior \(\pi(\theta)\), forward model \(y \sim p(y \mid \theta)\) or \(y = f(\theta)\), utility function \(U(d, \theta)\), decision space \(\mathcal{D}\).

Step 1: Simulate training data. Generate \(N\) triples from the prior-predictive: \[ \theta^{(i)} \sim \pi(\theta), \quad y^{(i)} \sim p(y \mid \theta^{(i)}) \;\text{or}\; y^{(i)} = f(\theta^{(i)}), \quad i = 1, \ldots, N. \]

Step 2: Compute utilities. For each decision \(d\) on a grid \(\{d_1, \ldots, d_M\} \subset \mathcal{D}\): \[ U^{(i)}_j = U(d_j, \theta^{(i)}), \quad i = 1, \ldots, N, \;\; j = 1, \ldots, M. \]

Step 3: Augment with uniform draws. Sample \(\tau^{(i)} \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)\) independently.

Step 4: Train quantile neural network. Learn \(H_\phi(S(y), \tau, d) \approx F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau)\) by minimizing the pinball loss (Eq. 3.2): \[ \mathcal{L}(\phi) = \frac{1}{NM} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^M \rho_{\tau^{(i)}}\!\left(U^{(i)}_j - H_\phi(S(y^{(i)}), \tau^{(i)}, d_j)\right). \]

Step 5: Evaluate expected utility. For observed data \(y_{\text{obs}}\) and each decision \(d\): \[ \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[U \mid d, y_{\text{obs}}] = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K H_\phi\!\left(S(y_{\text{obs}}), \frac{k - 0.5}{K}, d\right). \]

Step 6: Optimize. Find \(d^\star(y_{\text{obs}}) = \arg\max_d \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[U \mid d, y_{\text{obs}}]\).

Output: Optimal decision \(d^\star(y_{\text{obs}})\) and the full utility quantile function \(\tau \mapsto H_\phi(S(y_{\text{obs}}), \tau, d)\) for any \(d\).

Amortization. The expensive computation (Steps 1–4) is performed once during training. At test time (Steps 5–6), evaluating the expected utility for a new dataset \(y_{\text{obs}}\) requires only forward passes through the network—no new simulations, no MCMC, no density evaluations.

Decision as input. By including the decision \(d\) as an input to the network, a single trained model handles the entire optimization problem. We do not need to retrain for each candidate decision. For continuous decision spaces, the network interpolates smoothly between training decisions.

Full distributional output. The network learns the entire quantile function \(F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau)\), not just a point estimate of expected utility. From the learned quantile function we can immediately extract the Value-at-Risk \(\text{VaR}_\alpha = F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\alpha)\), the expected shortfall \(\text{ES}_\alpha = \frac{1}{\alpha} \int_0^\alpha F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau\) (the average utility in the worst \(\alpha\) fraction of scenarios), or any distorted expected utility \(\int_0^1 F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau) \cdot g'(1-\tau) \, d\tau\) for a chosen distortion function \(g\).

No density evaluation. At no point do we evaluate a likelihood, compute a normalizing constant, or estimate a density.

12.6 Portfolio Learning

We apply the GenBayes-MEU framework to portfolio allocation under parameter uncertainty. This example, from Polson, Ruggeri, and Sokolov (2024), demonstrates the method on a problem where the analytical solution is known, allowing us to verify accuracy.

Consider an investor allocating wealth between a risky asset and a risk-free asset. The portfolio return is \[ W(\omega) = (1 - \omega) r_f + \omega R, \quad R \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2), \tag{12.10}\] where \(\omega \in (0, 1)\) is the fraction allocated to the risky asset, \(r_f\) is the risk-free rate, and \(R\) is the random return on the risky asset.

The investor has exponential (CARA) utility: \[ U(W) = -e^{-\gamma W}, \tag{12.11}\] where \(\gamma > 0\) is the coefficient of absolute risk aversion. The CARA (constant absolute risk aversion) utility has the convenient property that the optimal portfolio weight does not depend on the investor’s wealth level. The negative exponential ensures that \(U\) is increasing and concave: more wealth is always preferred, but with diminishing marginal utility. The parameter \(\gamma\) controls how rapidly the marginal utility diminishes—a large \(\gamma\) corresponds to a highly risk-averse investor who strongly penalizes downside outcomes.

Since \(W(\omega) \sim N((1-\omega)r_f + \omega\mu, \; \omega^2 \sigma^2)\), the expected utility has a closed form using the moment-generating function of the normal: \[ \mathbb{E}[U(\omega)] = -\exp\!\left\{-\gamma\left[(1-\omega)r_f + \omega\mu\right] + \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \omega^2 \sigma^2\right\}. \] Maximizing over \(\omega\) yields the fractional Kelly criterion (Kelly 1956): \[ \omega^\star = \frac{\mu - r_f}{\gamma \sigma^2}. \tag{12.12}\] This is the Merton optimal portfolio weight (Merton 1969). When \(\gamma = 1\), it reduces to the full Kelly criterion. The formula has an intuitive interpretation: the optimal allocation to the risky asset increases with the risk premium \(\mu - r_f\) (a higher expected excess return justifies more exposure), decreases with risk aversion \(\gamma\) (a more cautious investor takes less risk), and decreases with return variance \(\sigma^2\) (a more volatile asset warrants smaller position sizes).

Now we solve the same problem using the quantile approach. The idea is to bypass the closed-form derivation entirely and instead:

  1. Simulate portfolio returns \(W^{(i)} \sim N((1-\omega^{(i)})r_f + \omega^{(i)}\mu, \; (\omega^{(i)})^2 \sigma^2)\)
  2. Compute utilities \(Z^{(i)} = -\exp(-\gamma W^{(i)})\)
  3. For each \(\omega\), compute the expected utility as \(\mathbb{E}[Z_\omega] = \int_0^1 F_{Z_\omega}^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau\)
  4. Optimize over \(\omega\)

Since the utility is a monotone transformation of \(W\), and \(W\) is normally distributed for each fixed \(\omega\), we can compute the quantile function of \(Z_\omega\) analytically: \[ F_{Z_\omega}^{-1}(\tau) = -\exp\!\left(-\gamma\!\left[(1-\omega)r_f + \omega\mu + \omega\sigma\Phi^{-1}(\tau)\right]\right). \] But we do not need this formula—the quantile function can be estimated from simulated utility values by simply sorting them.

Adding parameter uncertainty. The GBC approach shows its strength when we add parameter uncertainty. Suppose the expected return \(\mu\) is unknown with prior \(\mu \sim N(\mu_0, \sigma_0^2)\). The analytical solution now involves integrating over both the return randomness and the parameter uncertainty—a nested integral that is cumbersome to compute in closed form.

With the quantile approach, the solution is straightforward. No additional mathematical derivation is needed—the simulation automatically marginalizes over parameter uncertainty.

Multi-asset extension. The framework extends directly to multiple assets. With \(p\) risky assets and weights \(\omega = (\omega_1, \ldots, \omega_p)\) summing to at most 1, the portfolio return is \[ W(\omega) = \left(1 - \sum_j \omega_j\right) r_f + \sum_j \omega_j R_j, \quad R \sim N_p(\mu, \Sigma). \] The decision space is now multi-dimensional, and the quantile neural network takes \((\omega, S(y), \tau)\) as input. The grid search in Step 6 of the algorithm becomes a multi-dimensional optimization, but the core quantile machinery is unchanged. For moderate-dimensional portfolios (say, 5–20 assets), a random search or evolutionary optimizer over the weight simplex is effective. For larger portfolios, gradient-based optimization through the differentiable quantile network is preferable.

Consider a concrete three-asset example. An investor allocates wealth across a risk-free bond (\(r_f = 0.03\)), a stock index (\(\mu_1 = 0.10\), \(\sigma_1 = 0.20\)), a corporate bond fund (\(\mu_2 = 0.06\), \(\sigma_2 = 0.08\)), and an emerging market fund (\(\mu_3 = 0.14\), \(\sigma_3 = 0.30\)), with correlation matrix capturing the fact that stocks and emerging markets are positively correlated while bonds provide diversification. The portfolio return is \(W(\omega) = (1 - \omega_1 - \omega_2 - \omega_3)r_f + \omega_1 R_1 + \omega_2 R_2 + \omega_3 R_3\), and the GBC-MEU algorithm searches over the weight simplex to find the allocation that maximizes expected CARA utility. The key advantage over classical mean-variance optimization is that the GBC approach naturally handles non-normal return distributions, parameter uncertainty in \(\mu\) and \(\Sigma\), and non-quadratic utility functions—all within the same quantile integration framework.

12.7 Drug Development Decision

Pharmaceutical development decisions involve large stakes, deep uncertainty, and utility functions that differ substantially from simple expected profit maximization.

Consider a pharmaceutical company that has completed a Phase II clinical trial and must decide whether to proceed to Phase III. The Phase III trial is the final and most expensive step before regulatory approval. The company faces a binary decision: \(d \in \{\text{go}, \text{stop}\}\). If the company stops (\(d = \text{stop}\)), the payoff is zero—no further costs, no revenue. If the company proceeds (\(d = \text{go}\)), it incurs a trial cost \(C\) and faces an uncertain outcome. Let \(\theta\) denote the true treatment effect (e.g., the difference in response rates between treatment and placebo). If the Phase III trial succeeds—meaning the treatment effect exceeds a regulatory threshold \(\theta_{\min}\) and achieves statistical significance—the company earns revenue \(R\) from marketing the drug. If the trial fails, the company loses the trial cost with no offsetting revenue.

The probability of Phase III success depends on \(\theta\) through both the treatment effect magnitude and the statistical power. For a trial of sample size \(n_{\text{III}}\), the success probability given \(\theta\) is approximately \[ P(\text{success} \mid \theta) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\theta - \theta_{\min}}{\sigma_{\text{III}} / \sqrt{n_{\text{III}}}} - z_{1-\alpha/2}\right), \] where \(\sigma_{\text{III}}\) is the outcome standard deviation and \(z_{1-\alpha/2}\) is the critical value for the hypothesis test. The expected utility of proceeding is \[ \mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}[U(\text{go}, \theta)] = \mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}\!\left[u\!\left(R \cdot P(\text{success} \mid \theta) - C\right)\right], \] where \(u\) is the company’s utility function (typically concave, reflecting risk aversion over multi-billion-dollar investments) and \(y\) represents the Phase II data. The company should proceed whenever \(\mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}[U(\text{go}, \theta)] > u(0)\).

The GBC approach handles this problem by simulating from the prior-predictive distribution of Phase II outcomes and treatment effects, computing the utility for each simulation, and learning the utility quantile function. The quantile representation is particularly valuable here because the utility distribution is highly skewed: most of the probability mass is concentrated near \(u(-C)\) (trial failure) or \(u(R - C)\) (trial success), with the exact balance depending on \(\theta\).

12.8 Insurance Pricing via Wang Distortion

The connection between distortion functions and risk measures has a direct application in insurance pricing. Wang (2000) showed that coherent insurance premiums can be represented as distorted expectations of losses, and that the Wang distortion provides a principled one-parameter family for pricing.

Let \(L\) denote a random loss with survival function \(S_L(x) = P(L > x)\). The insurance premium under a distortion function \(g\) is \[ \Pi_g = \int_0^\infty g(S_L(x)) \, dx. \tag{12.13}\]

Without distortion (\(g(p) = p\)), the premium equals \(\mathbb{E}[L]\)—the actuarially fair price. A convex distortion \(g\) inflates the survival probabilities: the insurer acts as if losses are more likely than they actually are, generating a risk loading. The more convex \(g\) is, the larger the loading.

The one-parameter Wang distortion uses \[ g_\lambda(p) = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(p) + \lambda), \quad \lambda > 0, \] where \(\lambda\) is the market price of risk. This is the special case of Eq. 12.8 with \(\lambda_1 = 1\) and \(\lambda_0 = \lambda\). The premium becomes \[ \Pi_\lambda = \int_0^\infty \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(S_L(x)) + \lambda) \, dx. \]

For a normally distributed loss \(L \sim N(\mu_L, \sigma_L^2)\), the Wang premium has a closed form: \(\Pi_\lambda = \mu_L + \lambda \sigma_L\). This is the well-known standard deviation principle in actuarial science, with loading proportional to the standard deviation of the loss. For non-normal losses, the Wang premium naturally adapts to the shape of the loss distribution, loading more heavily on losses with fat tails.

The connection to Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) from Chapter 6 is direct. CVaR at level \(\alpha\) is \[ \text{CVaR}_\alpha(L) = \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \int_\alpha^1 F_L^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau, \] which is the mean loss in the worst \((1-\alpha)\) fraction of scenarios. This can be written as a distorted expectation with the piecewise-linear distortion \(g(p) = \min(p/(1-\alpha), 1)\). The Wang distortion provides a smooth alternative to this piecewise-linear distortion, with the parameter \(\lambda\) controlling the degree of tail emphasis.

From a GBC perspective, computing the insurance premium Eq. 12.13 requires only the quantile function of the loss distribution. Given a trained IQN that outputs \(F_{L \mid \text{data}}^{-1}(\tau)\), the premium under any distortion \(g\) is \[ \Pi_g = \int_0^1 F_L^{-1}(\tau) \cdot g'(1 - \tau) \, d\tau \approx \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K F_L^{-1}\!\left(\frac{k-0.5}{K}\right) \cdot g'\!\left(1 - \frac{k-0.5}{K}\right). \] This is a weighted average of quantiles, where the weights come from the derivative of the distortion function. The computation is a simple post-processing step on the quantile network output—no retraining required. Changing the distortion (and hence the risk loading) requires only changing the weights, not relearning the loss distribution.

The practical workflow for an insurer using GBC is straightforward. Given historical loss data, the insurer trains an IQN to learn the loss quantile function \(F_{L \mid \text{covariates}}^{-1}(\tau)\), where covariates might include policyholder characteristics, geographic region, or coverage level. The premium for any new policy is then computed by evaluating the distorted quantile integral with the company’s chosen \(\lambda\). Changing the risk loading requires no retraining—only reweighting the quantile evaluations.

12.9 Further Economic Applications

The GenBayes-MEU framework applies broadly to economic decision problems where the objective involves an expectation over uncertain parameters. We briefly discuss two additional application areas.

Risk management and Value-at-Risk. Financial regulators require banks to compute Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall (ES) for their trading portfolios. These are quantiles and conditional quantile integrals of portfolio loss distributions. The GenBayes-MEU framework computes both as by-products of the quantile function: \[ \text{VaR}_\alpha = F_L^{-1}(1 - \alpha), \qquad \text{ES}_\alpha = \frac{1}{\alpha} \int_0^\alpha F_L^{-1}(1 - \tau) \, d\tau. \] This is equivalent to the standard form \(\text{CVaR}_\alpha = \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_\alpha^1 F_L^{-1}(\tau)\,d\tau\) via the substitution \(u = 1-\tau\). When the portfolio contains derivatives or complex structured products, the loss distribution \(F_L\) is intractable, but forward simulation is straightforward. The quantile neural network learns the loss quantile function from simulated scenarios, providing a fast lookup table for risk calculations. The amortization property is especially valuable here: the bank trains the network once on a large set of market scenarios, and then evaluates VaR and ES in real time for any portfolio composition.

Supply chain optimization. A retailer must choose order quantities \(d = (d_1, \ldots, d_p)\) for \(p\) products to maximize expected profit: \[ U(d, \theta) = \sum_{j=1}^p \left[r_j \min(d_j, \theta_j) - c_j d_j\right], \] where \(\theta_j\) is the uncertain demand for product \(j\), \(r_j\) is the revenue per unit sold, and \(c_j\) is the cost per unit ordered. The min function makes this a newsvendor problem. The optimal solution depends on the posterior distribution of demand given observed sales data. The GenBayes-MEU framework simulates demand scenarios, computes profits for each order quantity, and trains a quantile network to learn the full profit distribution. The optimal order quantity maximizes the expected utility integral.

These applications share a common structure: an uncertain parameter \(\theta\), a decision \(d\), and a utility function \(U(d, \theta)\). The GenBayes-MEU algorithm provides a unified, density-free approach to all of them. The same algorithmic template applies to any decision problem that can be simulated forward, regardless of the complexity of the model, the form of the utility function, or the shape of the posterior distribution.

# Maximum Expected Utility {#sec-meu}

> "*The idea of maximizing expected utility is the cornerstone of rational decision-making under uncertainty.*" -- Leonard Savage

The previous chapters developed the GBC machinery for posterior inference and prediction. We learned how to simulate from priors and likelihoods, train quantile neural networks, and construct posterior distributions---all without evaluating densities. But inference is rarely the final goal. We observe data to *make decisions*.

A doctor interprets diagnostic results to choose a treatment. A portfolio manager reads market data to allocate capital. An engineer analyzes sensor readings to set control parameters. In each case, the statistical model is a means to an end: optimal action under uncertainty.

The classical framework for optimal decision-making is *maximum expected utility* (MEU). Given a utility function $U(d, \theta)$ defined over decisions $d$ and unknown parameters $\theta$, the Bayesian decision rule is
$$
d^\star(y) = \arg\max_d \; \mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}[U(d, \theta)] = \arg\max_d \int U(d, \theta) \, p(\theta \mid y) \, d\theta.
$$
The standard approach computes the posterior $p(\theta \mid y)$ first and then evaluates the integral by Monte Carlo. This two-step procedure is wasteful. Monte Carlo requires many samples in regions of high posterior probability, but utility functions often place high weight on tail events---rare but consequential outcomes like portfolio crashes or treatment failures. The expected utility integral and the posterior integral have different structures, and solving one does not efficiently solve the other.

This chapter shows that GBC provides a more direct path. A classical identity connects expected utility to quantile functions:
$$
\mathbb{E}[U] = \int_0^1 F_U^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau.
$$ {#eq-meu-quantile-identity}
The expected utility is the area under the quantile function of the utility random variable. If we can learn the quantile function of the utility distribution, we can compute expected utility by simple numerical integration---no density evaluation required.

We develop this idea in several steps. @sec-meu-quantile-identity establishes the quantile identity and its connection to Lorenz curves and the Gini coefficient. @sec-meu-yaari introduces Yaari's dual theory, which replaces outcome distortion with probability distortion. @sec-meu-wang shows that Bayesian updating itself can be viewed as a Wang distortion, connecting prior-to-posterior quantile maps to risk measures from finance. @sec-meu-augmentation develops the data augmentation trick that enables efficient computation. @sec-meu-algorithm presents the complete GenBayes-MEU algorithm. @sec-meu-portfolio applies the method to portfolio optimization. @sec-meu-drug-development walks through a pharmaceutical go/no-go decision. @sec-meu-insurance connects CVaR and Wang distortions to insurance pricing. @sec-meu-economics discusses further applications in economics and risk management.


## Expected Utility and Quantiles {#sec-meu-quantile-identity}

We begin with the identity that makes density-free expected utility computation possible. The connection runs through two classical objects from economics: the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient.

Let $U$ be a non-negative random variable with CDF $F_U$ and finite mean $\mu = \mathbb{E}[U]$. The **quantile function** $F_U^{-1}(\tau) = \inf\{u : F_U(u) \geq \tau\}$ provides a complete description of the distribution of $U$. The following identity expresses the expectation as an integral over quantiles.

Instead of integrating the random variable against its density---which requires knowing the density---we integrate the quantile function over the unit interval. The quantile function is a non-decreasing rearrangement of the random variable, and its integral over $[0,1]$ recovers the mean. For decision problems, this means we can compute expected utility by evaluating quantiles at a grid of probability levels and averaging. No likelihood, no normalizing constant, no density estimation.

::: {.callout-tip appearance="simple" title="Proposition 12.1: Quantile Representation of Expectations"}
For any random variable $U$ with $\mathbb{E}[|U|] < \infty$,
$$
\mathbb{E}[U] = \int_0^1 F_U^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau.
$$ {#eq-quantile-expectation}
:::

*Proof.* Apply the change of variable $\tau = F_U(u)$, so $d\tau = f_U(u) \, du$ and $u = F_U^{-1}(\tau)$:
$$
\int_0^1 F_U^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} u \, f_U(u) \, du = \mathbb{E}[U].
$$
For the non-negative case, an alternative proof uses the survival function $S_U(u) = 1 - F_U(u) = P(U > u)$ and the well-known identity $\mathbb{E}[U] = \int_0^\infty S_U(u) \, du$. $\square$

This identity is sometimes called the *quantile representation of expectations*. The mean of a distribution equals the area under its quantile function. Since the quantile function is non-decreasing, the trapezoidal rule is particularly accurate: the integrand has no oscillations, no sign changes, and bounded curvature for well-behaved distributions.

For decision problems, the identity takes the form
$$
\mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}[U(d, \theta)] = \int_0^1 F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau,
$$ {#eq-meu-decision-quantile}
where $F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau)$ is the quantile function of the random utility $U_d = U(d, \theta)$ with $\theta \sim p(\theta \mid y)$. Rather than computing the posterior density and then integrating the utility against it, we learn the quantile function of the utility distribution directly.

The quantile function of the utility can be learned from simulated data. We do not need to derive the distribution of $U(d, \theta)$ analytically. We simply simulate many $(\theta, y)$ pairs from the prior-predictive, compute the corresponding utilities, sort them, and the sorted values approximate the quantile function at equally spaced probability levels.

*The Lorenz curve.* The *Lorenz curve* [@lorenz1905methods], originally introduced to measure income inequality, provides a normalized version of the quantile identity. For a non-negative random variable $U$ with mean $\mu > 0$, the Lorenz curve is
$$
\mathcal{L}(p) = \frac{1}{\mu} \int_0^p F_U^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau, \quad p \in [0, 1].
$$ {#eq-lorenz-curve}

The Lorenz curve maps the cumulative share of the population ($p$) to the cumulative share of the total ($\mathcal{L}(p)$). If $U$ represents income, then $\mathcal{L}(0.5) = 0.3$ means the bottom 50% of earners receive 30% of total income.

The Lorenz curve has several key properties. It satisfies $\mathcal{L}(0) = 0$ and $\mathcal{L}(1) = 1$, and it is convex because the quantile function is non-decreasing, so the integrand is non-decreasing. When $U$ is constant (no uncertainty), $\mathcal{L}(p) = p$, which gives the line of perfect equality. Finally, the area under $\mathcal{L}$ relates directly to $\mathbb{E}[U]$: since $\mu \cdot \mathcal{L}(1) = \int_0^1 F_U^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau = \mu$, the identity is self-consistent.

*The Gini coefficient.* The *Gini coefficient* [@gini1912variabilita] measures the deviation of the Lorenz curve from the line of equality:
$$
G = 2 \int_0^1 \left(p - \mathcal{L}(p)\right) dp = 1 - 2 \int_0^1 \mathcal{L}(p) \, dp.
$$ {#eq-gini}

The Gini coefficient satisfies $0 \leq G \leq 1$, where $G = 0$ corresponds to perfect equality (all outcomes identical) and $G = 1$ to maximal inequality. In the context of utility distributions, the Gini coefficient measures how "spread out" the utility random variable is. A decision with low Gini coefficient has more predictable utility---less risk.

*Numerical computation.* The quantile identity @eq-quantile-expectation converts expected utility computation into numerical integration of a monotone function on $[0, 1]$. @yakowitz1978weighted showed that the trapezoidal rule applied to quantile functions achieves an $O(N^{-4})$ mean-squared error rate---far better than the $O(N^{-1})$ rate of standard Monte Carlo.

The gain is substantial. To achieve four-digit accuracy, standard Monte Carlo requires roughly $10^8$ samples ($N^{-1/2} \approx 10^{-4}$), whereas the quantile trapezoidal rule needs only about $N \approx 32$ samples ($N^{-2} \approx 10^{-3}$, or more precisely, the constant in the $O(N^{-4})$ bound is small for smooth quantile functions). In practice, we use $K = 100$ to $500$ quantile evaluations, which provides far more accuracy than millions of Monte Carlo draws.

::: {.callout-tip appearance="simple" title="Proposition 12.2: Trapezoidal Rule for Quantile Expectations (Yakowitz, 1978)"}
Let $Q(\tau)$ be a quantile function with continuous second derivative on $[0, 1]$, and let $U_{(1)} \leq U_{(2)} \leq \cdots \leq U_{(N)}$ be the order statistics of $N$ independent uniform samples on $[0, 1]$, with $U_{(0)} = 0$ and $U_{(N+1)} = 1$. Then the trapezoidal estimator
$$
\hat{\mu}_N = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=0}^{N} \left[Q(U_{(i)}) + Q(U_{(i+1)})\right] (U_{(i+1)} - U_{(i)})
$$
satisfies $\mathbb{E}[(\hat{\mu}_N - \mu)^2] \leq M / N^4$ for some constant $M$.
:::

The proof uses the classical error bound for the trapezoidal rule on each subinterval, together with moment bounds on the spacings of uniform order statistics. The key observation is that $\sum_{i} Z_i^3$, where $Z_i = U_{(i)} - U_{(i-1)}$ are the spacings, has moments that decay as $N^{-4}$ in the squared sum. Specifically, one shows that $|\hat{\mu}_N - \mu| \leq \frac{C}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{N+1} Z_i^3$ where $C$ bounds $|Q''|$. Taking expectations and using the moment formulas for uniform spacings---$\mathbb{E}[Z_i^6] = 6! \, n!/(n+6)!$ and $\mathbb{E}[Z_i^3 Z_j^3] = (3!)^2 \, n!/(n+6)!$ for $i \neq j$---yields the $O(N^{-4})$ bound.

In practice, we approximate the integral with a deterministic grid:
$$
\mathbb{E}[U] \approx \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} F_U^{-1}\left(\frac{k - 0.5}{K}\right).
$$ {#eq-trapezoid-approx}
This is simply the average of $K$ equally spaced quantiles. For a smooth quantile function, $K = 100$ typically suffices for four digits of accuracy.

## Yaari's Dual Theory {#sec-meu-yaari}

The classical expected utility framework, due to @vonneumann1947theory and axiomatized by @savage1954foundations, evaluates risky prospects by distorting outcomes through a utility function while keeping probabilities unchanged:
$$
V(X) = \mathbb{E}[u(X)] = \int_0^\infty u(x) \, dF_X(x).
$$
Risk aversion is encoded in the concavity of $u$: a concave $u$ assigns diminishing marginal utility to increasingly large payoffs.

@yaari1987dual proposed a dual theory that reverses this logic. Instead of distorting payoffs while preserving probabilities, Yaari distorts probabilities while preserving payoffs. The dual functional is
$$
\widetilde{V}(X) = \int_0^\infty g(S_X(u)) \, du,
$$ {#eq-yaari-dual}
where $S_X(u) = 1 - F_X(u) = P(X > u)$ is the survival function and $g : [0,1] \to [0,1]$ is a *distortion function* satisfying $g(0) = 0$ and $g(1) = 1$.

A distortion function $g$ transforms probabilities. If $g$ is convex, then $g(p) \leq p$ for $p$ near 0 and $g(p) \geq p$ for $p$ near 1---the agent overweights the probability of bad outcomes (low survival) and underweights the probability of good outcomes (high survival). This generates risk-averse behavior without any concavity in the utility function.

Conversely, if $g$ is concave, the agent overweights the probability of good outcomes---exhibiting risk-seeking behavior.

The *duality* between outcome distortion and probability distortion is central to Yaari's theory. Given a utility function $u$ with inverse $u^{-1}$, we can define the equivalent distortion function:
$$
g(p) = S_X\left(u^{-1}\left(S_X^{-1}(p)\right)\right).
$$
Under this choice of $g$, the dual functional $\widetilde{V}(X)$ produces the same ranking of gambles as $V(X) = \mathbb{E}[u(X)]$. This means we can always move between the two representations.

This duality has a consequence for computation. In the standard framework, evaluating $\mathbb{E}[u(X)]$ requires integrating the nonlinear function $u$ against the density $f_X$. In the dual framework, evaluating $\widetilde{V}(X)$ requires integrating the nonlinear function $g$ against the survival function $S_X$. The dual form is more natural for quantile-based methods because it operates directly on probabilities rather than on transformed payoffs.

Using the change of variable $u = F_X^{-1}(\tau)$, the dual functional @eq-yaari-dual can be rewritten as
$$
\widetilde{V}(X) = \int_0^1 F_X^{-1}(\tau) \cdot g'(1 - \tau) \, d\tau.
$$
When $g$ is differentiable, the weight $g'(1 - \tau)$ acts as a probability weighting function. If $g'$ is non-decreasing (i.e., $g$ is convex), higher quantiles receive more weight---reflecting risk aversion.

The special case $g(p) = p$ (the identity distortion) gives
$$
\widetilde{V}(X) = \int_0^1 F_X^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau = \mathbb{E}[X],
$$
which recovers the quantile identity from @eq-quantile-expectation. The identity distortion corresponds to risk neutrality.

The dual theory provides the conceptual bridge between expected utility and quantile methods. In the standard framework, we need the posterior density $p(\theta \mid y)$ to compute $\mathbb{E}[u(\theta)]$. In the dual framework, we need the posterior quantile function $F_{\theta \mid y}^{-1}(\tau)$ to compute $\int g(S_{\theta|y}(u)) \, du$. Since GBC naturally produces quantile functions (via quantile neural networks), the dual framework is more compatible with our computational machinery.

Moreover, different distortion functions $g$ correspond to different attitudes toward risk. By training a single quantile neural network that learns $F_{U \mid d,y}^{-1}(\tau)$, we can evaluate the expected utility under *any* distortion function by simply reweighting the quantile integral. This flexibility is valuable in applications where risk preferences are uncertain or context-dependent.


## Wang Distortion and Bayesian Updating {#sec-meu-wang}

A connection exists between Bayesian updating and probability distortion. @wang1996premium and @wang2000class introduced a parametric family of distortion functions:
$$
g(p) = \Phi\left(\lambda_1 \Phi^{-1}(p) + \lambda_0\right),
$$ {#eq-wang-distortion-meu}
where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF and $\lambda_1 > 0$, $\lambda_0 \in \mathbb{R}$ are parameters. This family is closed under composition, maps $[0,1]$ to $[0,1]$, and includes the identity ($\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_0 = 0$) as a special case.

In the normal-normal conjugate model, Bayesian updating is exactly a Wang distortion: the posterior CDF is $g$ composed with the prior CDF, where $\lambda_1 = \sigma_0/\sigma_n$ and $\lambda_0 = (\mu_0 - \mu_n)/\sigma_n$ (see the derivation in @sec-bi-normal, @eq-wang-distortion), with $\sigma_0$ the prior std, $\mu_n$ and $\sigma_n$ the posterior mean and std. The distortion steepens the CDF when data are informative ($\lambda_1 > 1$) and shifts it when the posterior mean differs from the prior mean ($\lambda_0 \neq 0$). The IQN learns this distortion from simulated data.

Let us illustrate the Wang distortion for a concrete normal-normal model.

## Data Augmentation and Conditional Sufficient Statistics {#sec-meu-augmentation}

The computational trick that makes GBC-MEU practical is augmenting the training data with independent uniform random variables.

@parzen2004quantile observed a fundamental identity: for any random variable $\theta$ with CDF $F_\theta$ and quantile function $Q_\theta$,
$$
\theta \stackrel{d}{=} Q_\theta(F_\theta(\theta)).
$$ {#eq-parzen-identity}
This is a tautology---applying the CDF and then the quantile function is the identity (in distribution). But it has a computational consequence: any random variable can be represented as a *composition* of two functions: the CDF (which maps to the uniform scale) and the quantile function (which maps back to the original scale).

Moreover, for monotone transformations $h$, the quantile function has a compositional property:
$$
Q_{h(\theta)}(\tau) = h(Q_\theta(\tau)).
$$
This means that quantile functions compose under monotone transformations---the same compositional structure exploited by deep neural networks with monotone activation functions.

The compositional property is what connects quantile methods to deep learning architectures. A deep network with monotone activations is, in effect, a sequence of quantile transformations. Each layer takes the quantile function from the previous layer and applies a learned monotone map. The utility function sits naturally as the outermost layer of this composition: given the posterior quantile $Q_{\theta \mid y}(\tau)$, the utility quantile is $Q_{U \mid d,y}(\tau) = U(d, Q_{\theta \mid y}(\tau))$ whenever $U(d, \cdot)$ is monotone in $\theta$.

The GBC framework exploits the identity @eq-parzen-identity by introducing independent uniform random variables $u^{(i)} \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$. Given simulated pairs $(\theta^{(i)}, y^{(i)})$ from the prior-predictive distribution, we construct the augmented training dataset
$$
\{(\theta^{(i)}, y^{(i)}, u^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N.
$$
The noise outsourcing theorem (see @sec-noise-outsourcing) guarantees the existence of a function $H$ such that
$$
\theta \mid y \stackrel{d}{=} H(S(y), u),
$$
where $S(y)$ is a sufficient statistic (or summary statistic performing dimensionality reduction) and $u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$. The function $H$ is precisely the posterior quantile function: $H(S(y), \tau) = F_{\theta \mid y}^{-1}(\tau)$.

In many statistical models, the data $y = (y_1, \ldots, y_n)$ is high-dimensional, but the posterior depends on $y$ only through a low-dimensional sufficient statistic $S(y)$. For the normal mean $S(y) = \bar{y}$; for the normal variance $S(y) = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2$; for the exponential rate and the binomial both reduce to $S(y) = \sum y_i$.

When a sufficient statistic is available, the augmented representation becomes
$$
\theta \mid y \stackrel{d}{=} H(S(y), u), \quad u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1).
$$
This dramatically reduces the dimensionality of the learning problem. The quantile neural network takes inputs $(S(y), \tau)$ rather than $(y_1, \ldots, y_n, \tau)$.

For models without known sufficient statistics, we can learn $S$ using a deep neural network. @polson2024generative show that the summary statistic can be learned independently of the quantile map: a simple $\ell_2$ regression of $\theta$ on $y$ provides a good approximation to the posterior mean, which often serves as a near-sufficient summary.

The augmentation extends to utility computations. Given the utility function $U(d, \theta)$ and the posterior quantile map $H(S(y), u)$, the distributional utility is
$$
U_d \stackrel{d}{=} U(d, H(S(y), u)), \quad u \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1).
$$
This is a composition: the utility function sits on top of the posterior quantile map. In neural network terms, the utility function acts as the first layer of the architecture, transforming the posterior parameter into a utility value. As @polson2024generative observe, rather than learning the posterior and then computing utility as a post-processing step, we embed the utility transformation directly into the network. The resulting architecture learns the *utility quantile function* end-to-end, avoiding the accumulation of errors from a two-stage procedure.

The augmented training dataset for MEU is then
$$
\{(U_d^{(i)}, S(y^{(i)}), u^{(i)}, d^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^N,
$$
where $U_d^{(i)} = U(d^{(i)}, \theta^{(i)})$. The quantile neural network learns
$$
F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau) \approx H_{\text{MEU}}(S(y), \tau, d),
$$
and the expected utility is computed via the trapezoidal rule:
$$
\mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}[U(d, \theta)] \approx \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K H_{\text{MEU}}\!\left(S(y), \frac{k - 0.5}{K}, d\right).
$$


## The GenBayes-MEU Algorithm {#sec-meu-algorithm}

We now assemble the pieces into a complete algorithm. Following the GBC template (Algorithm 10.2), but adapted for utility estimation:

::: {.callout-important appearance="simple" title="Algorithm: GenBayes-MEU"}
*Input:* Prior $\pi(\theta)$, forward model $y \sim p(y \mid \theta)$ or $y = f(\theta)$, utility function $U(d, \theta)$, decision space $\mathcal{D}$.

*Step 1: Simulate training data.* Generate $N$ triples from the prior-predictive:
$$
\theta^{(i)} \sim \pi(\theta), \quad y^{(i)} \sim p(y \mid \theta^{(i)}) \;\text{or}\; y^{(i)} = f(\theta^{(i)}), \quad i = 1, \ldots, N.
$$

*Step 2: Compute utilities.* For each decision $d$ on a grid $\{d_1, \ldots, d_M\} \subset \mathcal{D}$:
$$
U^{(i)}_j = U(d_j, \theta^{(i)}), \quad i = 1, \ldots, N, \;\; j = 1, \ldots, M.
$$

*Step 3: Augment with uniform draws.* Sample $\tau^{(i)} \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ independently.

*Step 4: Train quantile neural network.* Learn $H_\phi(S(y), \tau, d) \approx F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau)$ by minimizing the pinball loss (@eq-check-loss):
$$
\mathcal{L}(\phi) = \frac{1}{NM} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^M \rho_{\tau^{(i)}}\!\left(U^{(i)}_j - H_\phi(S(y^{(i)}), \tau^{(i)}, d_j)\right).
$$

*Step 5: Evaluate expected utility.* For observed data $y_{\text{obs}}$ and each decision $d$:
$$
\widehat{\mathbb{E}}[U \mid d, y_{\text{obs}}] = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K H_\phi\!\left(S(y_{\text{obs}}), \frac{k - 0.5}{K}, d\right).
$$

*Step 6: Optimize.* Find $d^\star(y_{\text{obs}}) = \arg\max_d \widehat{\mathbb{E}}[U \mid d, y_{\text{obs}}]$.

*Output:* Optimal decision $d^\star(y_{\text{obs}})$ and the full utility quantile function $\tau \mapsto H_\phi(S(y_{\text{obs}}), \tau, d)$ for any $d$.
:::

*Amortization.* The expensive computation (Steps 1--4) is performed once during training. At test time (Steps 5--6), evaluating the expected utility for a new dataset $y_{\text{obs}}$ requires only forward passes through the network---no new simulations, no MCMC, no density evaluations.

*Decision as input.* By including the decision $d$ as an input to the network, a single trained model handles the entire optimization problem. We do not need to retrain for each candidate decision. For continuous decision spaces, the network interpolates smoothly between training decisions.

*Full distributional output.* The network learns the entire quantile function $F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau)$, not just a point estimate of expected utility. From the learned quantile function we can immediately extract the Value-at-Risk $\text{VaR}_\alpha = F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\alpha)$, the expected shortfall $\text{ES}_\alpha = \frac{1}{\alpha} \int_0^\alpha F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau$ (the average utility in the worst $\alpha$ fraction of scenarios), or any distorted expected utility $\int_0^1 F_{U \mid d, y}^{-1}(\tau) \cdot g'(1-\tau) \, d\tau$ for a chosen distortion function $g$.

*No density evaluation.* At no point do we evaluate a likelihood, compute a normalizing constant, or estimate a density.


## Portfolio Learning {#sec-meu-portfolio}

We apply the GenBayes-MEU framework to portfolio allocation under parameter uncertainty. This example, from @polson2024generative, demonstrates the method on a problem where the analytical solution is known, allowing us to verify accuracy.

Consider an investor allocating wealth between a risky asset and a risk-free asset. The portfolio return is
$$
W(\omega) = (1 - \omega) r_f + \omega R, \quad R \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2),
$$ {#eq-portfolio-return}
where $\omega \in (0, 1)$ is the fraction allocated to the risky asset, $r_f$ is the risk-free rate, and $R$ is the random return on the risky asset.

The investor has exponential (CARA) utility:
$$
U(W) = -e^{-\gamma W},
$$ {#eq-cara-utility}
where $\gamma > 0$ is the coefficient of absolute risk aversion. The CARA (constant absolute risk aversion) utility has the convenient property that the optimal portfolio weight does not depend on the investor's wealth level. The negative exponential ensures that $U$ is increasing and concave: more wealth is always preferred, but with diminishing marginal utility. The parameter $\gamma$ controls how rapidly the marginal utility diminishes---a large $\gamma$ corresponds to a highly risk-averse investor who strongly penalizes downside outcomes.

Since $W(\omega) \sim N((1-\omega)r_f + \omega\mu, \; \omega^2 \sigma^2)$, the expected utility has a closed form using the moment-generating function of the normal:
$$
\mathbb{E}[U(\omega)] = -\exp\!\left\{-\gamma\left[(1-\omega)r_f + \omega\mu\right] + \frac{1}{2}\gamma^2 \omega^2 \sigma^2\right\}.
$$
Maximizing over $\omega$ yields the *fractional Kelly criterion* [@kelly1956new]:
$$
\omega^\star = \frac{\mu - r_f}{\gamma \sigma^2}.
$$ {#eq-kelly}
This is the Merton optimal portfolio weight [@merton1969lifetime]. When $\gamma = 1$, it reduces to the full Kelly criterion. The formula has an intuitive interpretation: the optimal allocation to the risky asset increases with the risk premium $\mu - r_f$ (a higher expected excess return justifies more exposure), decreases with risk aversion $\gamma$ (a more cautious investor takes less risk), and decreases with return variance $\sigma^2$ (a more volatile asset warrants smaller position sizes).

Now we solve the same problem using the quantile approach. The idea is to bypass the closed-form derivation entirely and instead:

1. Simulate portfolio returns $W^{(i)} \sim N((1-\omega^{(i)})r_f + \omega^{(i)}\mu, \; (\omega^{(i)})^2 \sigma^2)$
2. Compute utilities $Z^{(i)} = -\exp(-\gamma W^{(i)})$
3. For each $\omega$, compute the expected utility as $\mathbb{E}[Z_\omega] = \int_0^1 F_{Z_\omega}^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau$
4. Optimize over $\omega$

Since the utility is a monotone transformation of $W$, and $W$ is normally distributed for each fixed $\omega$, we can compute the quantile function of $Z_\omega$ analytically:
$$
F_{Z_\omega}^{-1}(\tau) = -\exp\!\left(-\gamma\!\left[(1-\omega)r_f + \omega\mu + \omega\sigma\Phi^{-1}(\tau)\right]\right).
$$
But we do not need this formula---the quantile function can be estimated from simulated utility values by simply sorting them.

When the expected return is uncertain, the optimal weight shrinks---the investor becomes more conservative because parameter uncertainty adds risk beyond the inherent return volatility. This phenomenon, sometimes called *estimation risk* or the *Bayesian shrinkage effect*, is difficult to capture in a plug-in framework but arises naturally from the GBC approach.

**Multi-asset extension.** The framework extends directly to multiple assets. With $p$ risky assets and weights $\omega = (\omega_1, \ldots, \omega_p)$ summing to at most 1, the portfolio return is
$$
W(\omega) = \left(1 - \sum_j \omega_j\right) r_f + \sum_j \omega_j R_j, \quad R \sim N_p(\mu, \Sigma).
$$
The decision space is now multi-dimensional, and the quantile neural network takes $(\omega, S(y), \tau)$ as input. The grid search in Step 6 of the algorithm becomes a multi-dimensional optimization, but the core quantile machinery is unchanged. For moderate-dimensional portfolios (say, 5--20 assets), a random search or evolutionary optimizer over the weight simplex is effective. For larger portfolios, gradient-based optimization through the differentiable quantile network is preferable.

Consider a concrete three-asset example. An investor allocates wealth across a risk-free bond ($r_f = 0.03$), a stock index ($\mu_1 = 0.10$, $\sigma_1 = 0.20$), a corporate bond fund ($\mu_2 = 0.06$, $\sigma_2 = 0.08$), and an emerging market fund ($\mu_3 = 0.14$, $\sigma_3 = 0.30$), with correlation matrix capturing the fact that stocks and emerging markets are positively correlated while bonds provide diversification. The portfolio return is $W(\omega) = (1 - \omega_1 - \omega_2 - \omega_3)r_f + \omega_1 R_1 + \omega_2 R_2 + \omega_3 R_3$, and the GBC-MEU algorithm searches over the weight simplex to find the allocation that maximizes expected CARA utility. The key advantage over classical mean-variance optimization is that the GBC approach naturally handles non-normal return distributions, parameter uncertainty in $\mu$ and $\Sigma$, and non-quadratic utility functions---all within the same quantile integration framework.


## Drug Development Decision {#sec-meu-drug-development}

Pharmaceutical development decisions involve large stakes, deep uncertainty, and utility functions that differ substantially from simple expected profit maximization.

Consider a pharmaceutical company that has completed a Phase II clinical trial and must decide whether to proceed to Phase III. The Phase III trial is the final and most expensive step before regulatory approval. The company faces a binary decision: $d \in \{\text{go}, \text{stop}\}$. If the company stops ($d = \text{stop}$), the payoff is zero---no further costs, no revenue. If the company proceeds ($d = \text{go}$), it incurs a trial cost $C$ and faces an uncertain outcome. Let $\theta$ denote the true treatment effect (e.g., the difference in response rates between treatment and placebo). If the Phase III trial succeeds---meaning the treatment effect exceeds a regulatory threshold $\theta_{\min}$ and achieves statistical significance---the company earns revenue $R$ from marketing the drug. If the trial fails, the company loses the trial cost with no offsetting revenue.

The probability of Phase III success depends on $\theta$ through both the treatment effect magnitude and the statistical power. For a trial of sample size $n_{\text{III}}$, the success probability given $\theta$ is approximately
$$
P(\text{success} \mid \theta) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{\theta - \theta_{\min}}{\sigma_{\text{III}} / \sqrt{n_{\text{III}}}} - z_{1-\alpha/2}\right),
$$
where $\sigma_{\text{III}}$ is the outcome standard deviation and $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ is the critical value for the hypothesis test. The expected utility of proceeding is
$$
\mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}[U(\text{go}, \theta)] = \mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}\!\left[u\!\left(R \cdot P(\text{success} \mid \theta) - C\right)\right],
$$
where $u$ is the company's utility function (typically concave, reflecting risk aversion over multi-billion-dollar investments) and $y$ represents the Phase II data. The company should proceed whenever $\mathbb{E}_{\theta \mid y}[U(\text{go}, \theta)] > u(0)$.

The GBC approach handles this problem by simulating from the prior-predictive distribution of Phase II outcomes and treatment effects, computing the utility for each simulation, and learning the utility quantile function. The quantile representation is particularly valuable here because the utility distribution is highly skewed: most of the probability mass is concentrated near $u(-C)$ (trial failure) or $u(R - C)$ (trial success), with the exact balance depending on $\theta$.

## Insurance Pricing via Wang Distortion {#sec-meu-insurance}

The connection between distortion functions and risk measures has a direct application in insurance pricing. @wang2000class showed that coherent insurance premiums can be represented as distorted expectations of losses, and that the Wang distortion provides a principled one-parameter family for pricing.

Let $L$ denote a random loss with survival function $S_L(x) = P(L > x)$. The insurance premium under a distortion function $g$ is
$$
\Pi_g = \int_0^\infty g(S_L(x)) \, dx.
$$ {#eq-insurance-premium}

Without distortion ($g(p) = p$), the premium equals $\mathbb{E}[L]$---the actuarially fair price. A convex distortion $g$ inflates the survival probabilities: the insurer acts as if losses are more likely than they actually are, generating a risk loading. The more convex $g$ is, the larger the loading.

The one-parameter Wang distortion uses
$$
g_\lambda(p) = \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(p) + \lambda), \quad \lambda > 0,
$$
where $\lambda$ is the *market price of risk*. This is the special case of @eq-wang-distortion-meu with $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_0 = \lambda$. The premium becomes
$$
\Pi_\lambda = \int_0^\infty \Phi(\Phi^{-1}(S_L(x)) + \lambda) \, dx.
$$

For a normally distributed loss $L \sim N(\mu_L, \sigma_L^2)$, the Wang premium has a closed form: $\Pi_\lambda = \mu_L + \lambda \sigma_L$. This is the well-known *standard deviation principle* in actuarial science, with loading proportional to the standard deviation of the loss. For non-normal losses, the Wang premium naturally adapts to the shape of the loss distribution, loading more heavily on losses with fat tails.

The connection to Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR) from @sec-dist-rl is direct. CVaR at level $\alpha$ is
$$
\text{CVaR}_\alpha(L) = \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \int_\alpha^1 F_L^{-1}(\tau) \, d\tau,
$$
which is the mean loss in the worst $(1-\alpha)$ fraction of scenarios. This can be written as a distorted expectation with the piecewise-linear distortion $g(p) = \min(p/(1-\alpha), 1)$. The Wang distortion provides a smooth alternative to this piecewise-linear distortion, with the parameter $\lambda$ controlling the degree of tail emphasis.

From a GBC perspective, computing the insurance premium @eq-insurance-premium requires only the quantile function of the loss distribution. Given a trained IQN that outputs $F_{L \mid \text{data}}^{-1}(\tau)$, the premium under any distortion $g$ is
$$
\Pi_g = \int_0^1 F_L^{-1}(\tau) \cdot g'(1 - \tau) \, d\tau \approx \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K F_L^{-1}\!\left(\frac{k-0.5}{K}\right) \cdot g'\!\left(1 - \frac{k-0.5}{K}\right).
$$
This is a weighted average of quantiles, where the weights come from the derivative of the distortion function. The computation is a simple post-processing step on the quantile network output---no retraining required. Changing the distortion (and hence the risk loading) requires only changing the weights, not relearning the loss distribution.

## Further Economic Applications {#sec-meu-economics}

The GenBayes-MEU framework applies broadly to economic decision problems where the objective involves an expectation over uncertain parameters. We briefly discuss two additional application areas.

**Risk management and Value-at-Risk.** Financial regulators require banks to compute Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall (ES) for their trading portfolios. These are quantiles and conditional quantile integrals of portfolio loss distributions. The GenBayes-MEU framework computes both as by-products of the quantile function:
$$
\text{VaR}_\alpha = F_L^{-1}(1 - \alpha), \qquad \text{ES}_\alpha = \frac{1}{\alpha} \int_0^\alpha F_L^{-1}(1 - \tau) \, d\tau.
$$
This is equivalent to the standard form $\text{CVaR}_\alpha = \frac{1}{1-\alpha}\int_\alpha^1 F_L^{-1}(\tau)\,d\tau$ via the substitution $u = 1-\tau$.
When the portfolio contains derivatives or complex structured products, the loss distribution $F_L$ is intractable, but forward simulation is straightforward. The quantile neural network learns the loss quantile function from simulated scenarios, providing a fast lookup table for risk calculations. The amortization property is especially valuable here: the bank trains the network once on a large set of market scenarios, and then evaluates VaR and ES in real time for any portfolio composition.

**Supply chain optimization.** A retailer must choose order quantities $d = (d_1, \ldots, d_p)$ for $p$ products to maximize expected profit:
$$
U(d, \theta) = \sum_{j=1}^p \left[r_j \min(d_j, \theta_j) - c_j d_j\right],
$$
where $\theta_j$ is the uncertain demand for product $j$, $r_j$ is the revenue per unit sold, and $c_j$ is the cost per unit ordered. The min function makes this a newsvendor problem. The optimal solution depends on the posterior distribution of demand given observed sales data. The GenBayes-MEU framework simulates demand scenarios, computes profits for each order quantity, and trains a quantile network to learn the full profit distribution. The optimal order quantity maximizes the expected utility integral.

These applications share a common structure: an uncertain parameter $\theta$, a decision $d$, and a utility function $U(d, \theta)$. The GenBayes-MEU algorithm provides a unified, density-free approach to all of them. The same algorithmic template applies to any decision problem that can be simulated forward, regardless of the complexity of the model, the form of the utility function, or the shape of the posterior distribution.
