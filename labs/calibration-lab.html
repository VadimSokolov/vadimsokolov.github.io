Calibration Lab: When Can a Model Decide Alone?

Take a bank that predicts the probability p that a loan will default. Suppose a repaid loan earns 25 cents per dollar lent and a default loses the dollar. The expected profit per dollar is \(0.25(1 - p) - 1\cdot p = 0.25 - 1.25p\), which is positive when \(p < 0.25/1.25 = 1/5\). So the bank approves a loan when its predicted default probability is below 1/5. The rule takes p at its word: it assumes that among loans given p = 0.2, one in five defaults. Logistic regression always returns a number between 0 and 1, and p and 1 − p always add to 1, but that only makes p look like a probability. Whether it behaves like one is an empirical question called calibration, and it is not the same as accuracy. A model can rank borrowers well and still report default chances it has not earned, or report honest chances that tell us almost nothing. The same question comes up whenever a model reports a confidence, from a credit score to a language model that says it is 83% sure. This lab starts with the loan model and builds the idea step by step: what calibration means and how it is measured, why it differs from accuracy, how to read it from a plot without being fooled, and how a trustworthy probability becomes a decision.

Start here · a loan-default model

A number between 0 and 1 is not yet a probability

A logistic regression turns a score \(\beta^\top x\) into \(p = 1/(1 + e^{-\beta^\top x})\). The formula guarantees that \(0 < p < 1\) and that \(p\) and \(1 - p\) add to 1. Those are the rules a probability must obey, so it is natural to treat p as one. But the formula would produce numbers with those properties for any β, even a β fitted badly or to noise. Whether p = 0.2 means that one loan in five like this one defaults is a question about data, and the only way to answer it is to compare the predictions with what happened to loans the model did not see.

The cutoff rule depends on that answer. With a profit of g per dollar on a repaid loan and a loss of the dollar on a default, the expected profit is \(g(1 - p) - 1\cdot p = g - (1 + g)p\), positive when \(p < g/(1 + g)\). For \(g = 0.25\) this is \(0.25 - 1.25p\) and the cutoff is 1/5. Questions for students

  1. On the loans it was fitted to, every model looks calibrated, and the largest looks perfect. Why is that no evidence?

    Answer

    Maximum likelihood forces the fitted probabilities of a logistic regression with an intercept to average to the observed default rate on the training data (the score equation \(\sum_i (y_i - p_i) = 0\)), and a model with enough coefficients can push each loan to 0 or 1 by memorizing it. With 745 coefficients for 1,000 loans the model separates the training data completely. Calibration has to be checked on loans the model has not seen.

  2. The 20-variable model has the better AUC on new loans but the worse ECE. Which would you use, and what would you change?

    Answer

    It ranks borrowers better (AUC 0.78 against 0.69) and still makes more money at the 1/5 cutoff, but its probabilities are too extreme: among the loans it approves it predicts about 8% default and gets about 12%. Use its ranking and recalibrate its probabilities, or shrink the coefficients with a penalty, before setting the cutoff.

  3. Why does the interaction model lose money even though it approves the most loans?

    Answer

    It reports a default chance near zero for most applicants, so almost all clear the 1/5 cutoff. On new loans about one in five of those defaults, exactly the break-even rate, and the 25 cents earned on four loans is lost on the fifth. The rule was right; the p fed into it was not a probability.

Why this matters

Why care about calibration, and why Jev made noise

An accurate model that picks the right answer is enough when a person reads every answer. Once software acts on the answers, the probability becomes an input to a decision, and a wrong probability gives a wrong decision even when the answer is right.

Probabilities feed decisions

Whether to act alone or ask a human, buy insurance, order stock or treat a patient depends on the chance of being wrong times the cost of being wrong. The rule “automate when \((1 - p)\,e < h\)” is only right if p is. An overconfident 90% that is really 70% triples the errors the rule was set to tolerate (see the threshold section below).

Probabilities get combined

A calibrated probability can be multiplied by a payoff, fed into Bayes’ rule with other evidence, averaged with other forecasts, or summed into an expected count (“we expect 40 failures this week”). A score that only ranks can do none of this. Ranking is enough to sort a queue; calibration is what lets you price it.

Knowing when you do not know

Abstaining, asking a clarifying question or handing off to a person all need a confidence that drops when the model is out of its depth. The BMJ review counts poor calibration and metacognition among the main obstacles to clinical AI: models stayed confident when the correct answer was not on the list.

Why LLMs are usually not calibrated

A chat model writes text. Asked for a probability, it writes a number that sounds right, which is not the same thing. Its internal token probabilities are better, but instruction tuning makes them overconfident: in Dibia’s experiment on 10-option intent classification, ECE was 0.02 for the 7B base model and 0.10 for the instruct version.

What Jev promised

TypeSafe sells Jev as a decision model: give it a state and a list of options, and it returns a typed choice with a probability for each option, in 70 to 500 ms, at $0.042 per million input tokens. It says the probabilities are calibrated by a training method it calls Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions, and recommends thresholds on them to decide when software acts alone. Cheap, fast, always on the list of options, and probabilities you can set a threshold on: that is what made the noise.

What the evidence shows

TypeSafe has published no reliability diagram or ECE. Its one accuracy-by-confidence result is 27 of 30 correct above 0.9, a 95% interval of 74% to 97%. A third-party test on 662 prompt-injection messages (jev-sec-bench, reported by Dibia) found ECE 0.059. Hayashi’s hidden die drew 83% for a 1-in-6 guess. Calibration is a claim about a population of questions, so it has to be checked on yours.

Accuracy

Question: how often is the forecast on the right side?

\[ \text{call “yes” if } p > 0.5, \qquad \text{accuracy} = \frac{\text{number of correct calls}}{n} \]

Between 0 and 1, higher is better. With several options it is the share of times the top pick is right. It ignores how confident the forecast was: 51% and 99% count the same.

AUC

Question: does the forecaster rank events correctly?

\[ \text{AUC} = P\big(p_i > p_j \;\big|\; y_i = 1,\ y_j = 0\big), \quad \text{ties count } \tfrac12 \]

0.5 means no ranking, 1 means every event that happened was ranked above every one that did not. Any change that keeps the order, such as halving every forecast, leaves AUC unchanged, so AUC cannot detect miscalibration.

ECE

Question: do the numbers mean what they say?

\[ \text{ECE} = \sum_{g} \frac{n_g}{n}\,\big|\bar p_g - \bar y_g\big| \]

Group the forecasts by value (or into bins) g; \(n_g\) is the number of forecasts in group g, \(\bar p_g\) their average and \(\bar y_g\) the share of their events that happened. ECE = 0 is perfectly calibrated. It compares each group’s average forecast with the share of its events that happened, weighted by group size. It cannot detect a useless forecaster: 10% on every horse scores 0. With few events per bin it is noisy.

Brier score

Question: how far were the forecasts from what happened?

\[ \text{Brier} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (p_i - y_i)^2, \qquad y_i \in \{0, 1\} \]

0 is perfect, 0.25 is always saying 50%, lower is better. It is a proper scoring rule: a forecaster minimizes its expected Brier score by reporting its true probability. Log loss, −log of the probability given to what happened, works the same way and punishes confident misses harder.

REL, RES and UNC

Question: how much of the Brier score is bad numbers, and how much is real information?

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Brier} &= \text{REL} - \text{RES} + \text{UNC}\\ \text{REL} &= \sum_g \tfrac{n_g}{n}\,(\bar p_g - \bar y_g)^2\\ \text{RES} &= \sum_g \tfrac{n_g}{n}\,(\bar y_g - \bar y)^2\\ \text{UNC} &= \bar y\,(1 - \bar y) \end{aligned} \]

UNC depends only on the outcomes, so it is the same for every forecaster scored on the same events. REL is the squared version of ECE, lower is better. RES is how far the groups’ event rates spread from the overall rate \(\bar y\), higher is better. The split is exact when every forecast in a group has the same value, approximate with bins.

Which is which

Separation: accuracy, AUC, RES. Calibration: ECE, REL. Both: Brier, log loss.

A forecaster can be calibrated and useless (always the base rate: REL = 0, RES = 0), or miscalibrated and well ranked (overconfident: high AUC, high REL). Recalibration lowers REL and leaves the ranking, and so AUC, unchanged.

AUC measures discrimination: the chance that a randomly chosen right answer got higher confidence than a randomly chosen wrong one (0.5 is no ranking, 1 is perfect). It ignores the numbers themselves, so a model can rank answers well and still be badly calibrated, and a single increasing recalibration map leaves AUC unchanged. Here each half gets its own map, so the recalibrated AUC can move slightly. ECE is \(\sum_b (n_b/n)\,|\text{acc}_b - \text{conf}_b|\) over ten equal-width bins. Brier is the mean of \((c - y)^2\) and log loss the mean of −log of the probability given to what happened, where y = 1 if the answer was right. Both are proper scoring rules: the forecaster scores best by reporting the true chance. Recalibration leaves accuracy unchanged because it changes the reported number, never the chosen answer.

The map is fitted by maximum likelihood on one half of the questions and applied to the other (two-fold). Temperature: \(c' = \sigma(\operatorname{logit}(c)/T)\), one parameter, which undoes s but not b. Platt: \(c' = \sigma(\alpha \operatorname{logit}(c) + \beta)\), which undoes both. “Benchmark only” fits on answerable questions, as when a model is checked on an exam and then deployed on everything.

Questions for students

  1. What does a point on the diagonal tell you about a market? What does it not tell you?

    Answer

    That events priced near p happened about a fraction p of the time: the prices can be read as probabilities. It does not say how close the prices came to the outcome of each event. Calibration is a property of the pooled prices, accuracy of each forecast against its outcome.

  2. Rank the eight markets by accuracy using only the coloured curves. Can you?

    Answer

    No. All eight curves lie on the diagonal up to noise, yet the Brier scores differ several-fold. Market H prices every event at its base rate, the analogue of 10% on every horse, and is calibrated too: it shows as one point.

  3. Which part of each panel does carry information about accuracy?

    Answer

    The grey histogram. For calibrated forecasts the Brier score is UNC − RES: the base-rate uncertainty minus the resolution, and resolution is large when prices sit near 0 and 1 and come true there. Markets whose prices pile up at the ends are sharper and more accurate. Calibrated forecasts should be judged by their sharpness, “maximize sharpness subject to calibration” (Gneiting, Balabdaoui and Raftery, 2007).

  4. A market trades many times before it closes. Which price goes into the plot? Switch to “1 month before close” and compare.

    Answer

    The curves stay on the diagonal while the histograms move toward the middle and every Brier score gets worse. A day before an election, or minutes before a game ends, the price is close to 0 or 1 because the outcome is nearly known. Pooling trades from all times rewards a market for what it learned at the last minute. A claim about accuracy has to fix the horizon.

  5. What evidence would you need to say “very accurate”?

    Answer

    A proper score (Brier or log loss) at a stated horizon, compared with a benchmark: the base rate, polls, a statistical model or expert forecasts, on the same events. Plus the share of events that never reach a confident price, and how the markets were selected.

Harry Crane’s conclusion

“These are calibration plots, which says nothing about accuracy. These are nice pictures, but they don’t prove accuracy.” Calibration is necessary for prices to be read as probabilities. It is not evidence that they are close to the truth.

From a probability to an action

Set the automation threshold

Let a human review cost h and an automated error cost e. Acting alone on an answer with probability p of being right costs \((1 - p)\,e\) on average, so automate when \((1 - p)\,e < h\), that is when \(p > t^* = 1 - h/e\). The rule is only as good as p. A confidence is a frequency claim

Calibration is a property of many answers, not one. “80%” is checked by collecting the answers given 80% and counting how many were right.

  1. Accuracy and calibration are different questions

    Accuracy asks how often the chosen answer is right. Calibration asks whether the reported probability matches that rate among answers given the same probability. Dibia’s 7B instruct model reported 95% on average at 77 options and was right 64% of the time. Its choices were reasonable, its numbers were not.

  2. Ranking well is not the same as reporting well

    A model can be good at separating classes, putting its right answers above its wrong ones, and still be poorly calibrated. AUC measures the separation, the reliability diagram the numbers. In the “Instruct, 77 options” preset Platt scaling takes ECE from about 0.31 to 0.03 while AUC stays near 0.78, because a recalibration map never reorders answers. (The AUC moves slightly here only because each half of the data gets its own fitted map.) Answers reported at 95% may come true 70% of the time (overconfidence), or the reverse (underconfidence, set s < 1).

  3. The reliability diagram shows where the numbers lie

    Bars below the diagonal mean overconfidence: answers given 90% are right less often than 90%. ECE averages the gaps, weighted by how many answers fall in each bin. A model can be accurate and badly calibrated, or calibrated and useless.

  4. A fitted map repairs the numbers, not the choices

    Dividing every logit by one fitted T stretches or squeezes confidence without changing any choice. In Dibia’s experiment at 10 options it took a 7B instruct model’s ECE from 0.10 to 0.03 at the same 88.8% accuracy. A temperature cannot undo a confidence that is shifted up; Platt scaling adds a shift. Either needs labelled data from the task where the model will be used.

  5. Calibrated on the exam is not calibrated on the ward

    A model checked on questions that have an answer says nothing about questions that do not. The BMJ review reports that models gave highly confident answers when the correct option was absent (MetaMedQA), and lost 9 to 38 points of accuracy when the right answer was replaced by “None of the other answers”. Fit the temperature on the benchmark only, add unknowable questions, and the diagram breaks again.

  6. The threshold turns calibration into money

    With an error ten times as costly as a review, automate above t* = 0.90. If the reported 90% is really 70%, every automated batch carries three times the errors the rule assumed. Overconfidence by d moves the right threshold up by about d on the reported scale.

Takeaway for students. Before trusting a model’s probability to route decisions, collect labelled cases from the setting where it will run, including cases with no answer, and draw the reliability diagram. A number that is not calibrated there is a ranking, not a probability.

When the model overwrites the document

Hayashi gave Jev synthetic inventory reports that stated a 30% shortage risk. The choice mode returned a mean of 5.3%. With a rule “alert when shortage risk exceeds 10%”, the stated 30% fires the alert and the model’s 5.3% does not. A stated 45% came back as 6.6% and a stated 55% as 95.9%: ten points of input moved the output by about 89.

When a source already gives a probability, extract it; do not pass it through a classifier.

Clinical AI

Worth et al. list failures in uncertainty calibration and metacognition among the persistent weaknesses of frontier models, and the inability of models to calibrate their own confidence as a safety concern. In a randomised trial, physicians exposed to erroneous AI advice were 14 percentage points less accurate (95% CI −18.9 to −9.1) than controls. An overconfident number is most dangerous when a person defers to it.
