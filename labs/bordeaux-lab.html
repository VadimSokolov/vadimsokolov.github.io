The wine equation and what each coefficient means

The model is fitted to the 27 mature vintages from 1952 to 1980. Prices are skewed to the right and weather acts on them multiplicatively, so the response is log price. A coefficient \(\beta\) then means "one more unit multiplies the price by \(e^{\beta}\)", or about \(100\beta\) percent when \(\beta\) is small.

Out of sample

Does the equation predict vintages it has not seen?

In-sample fit flatters a model. Here each prediction comes from a fit that never saw the vintage being predicted, so the errors are honest. The 95% prediction intervals use the training fit's own residual spread.

Log-linear regression in four steps

Weather adds on the log scale and multiplies the price

Four weather numbers and the wine's age explain 83% of the variation in log mature prices. Summer heat and a dry harvest dominate. September temperature, the experts' favourite indicator, adds nothing once the whole summer is in the model.

  1. Model the log

    A good vintage is worth several times a bad one, so price differences are ratios. On the log scale the model in levels (\(R^2\) 0.75) becomes a better fitting additive model (\(R^2\) 0.83), and every coefficient reads as a percent effect.

  2. Read \(\beta\) as a multiplier

    Summer has \(\beta = 0.607\), so one more degree multiplies the price by \(e^{0.607} \approx 1.84\), an 84% rise. Harvest rain has \(\beta = -0.0037\) per mm, so 10 mm more cuts the price by about 4%. Each year of age adds about 2.5%, the return to cellaring.

  3. Predict on the log scale, then exponentiate

    The prediction interval is \(\hat y \pm t_{0.975,21} \cdot s_{\text{pred}}\) in logs. Exponentiating gives an interval for the price that is lopsided: wider above than below. \(e^{\hat y}\) is the median price; the mean is larger by \(e^{s^2/2} \approx 1.04\).

  4. Check it on vintages it has not seen

    The in-sample residual SE is 0.29. Leaving each vintage out in turn gives a prediction error of about 0.34 on the log scale. Fitting on early vintages and predicting later ones keeps most actual prices inside the 95% intervals.

Takeaway for students. A regression fitted to past vintages can price a new one from public weather data the day the harvest ends. The equation called 1986 the weakest vintage of the decade and 1989 and 1990 the classics. Out-of-range weather, like the 19 to 21 °C summers of 2017 to 2019, is where its forecasts deserve the least trust.

Ashenfelter vs the critics

Wine critics judge a young Bordeaux by tasting it from the barrel, months before it is bottled, and the market prices it on their verdicts. Orley Ashenfelter, an economist, argued that the weather already contains most of that information: hot summers ripen the grapes, a dry harvest concentrates them, and a wet winter feeds the vines early.

The critics did not welcome an equation that claimed to rate wine without tasting it. The data in this lab is the test of who was right.

What the numbers are

Price is an index of mature-wine auction prices with 1961 = 100. Summer is average growing-season temperature in °C, September is September's average temperature, harvest and winter rain are in mm, and age is years since the vintage. The 1981–1991 rows have weather and age but no price. The 2017–2019 weather values come from the case study's script.

All coefficients and statistics on this page are fitted live in your browser from these rows.

Where the equation can fail

The training summers run from 15.0 to 17.6 °C. A log-linear heat effect cannot continue forever: past some point heat and drought hurt quality. Predictions for 19 to 21 °C summers are extrapolations, so trust the ranking more than the magnitudes.

Prices also reflect fashion, scarcity and critics' scores, which the weather cannot see. That is what the residuals measure.

Try this in class

  1. Before showing anything, ask students to rank 1983, 1986 and 1990 from the weather alone, using the tooltips on the vintage chart. Then reveal the 1980s bar chart. (1990 ≈ 116, 1983 ≈ 70, 1986 ≈ 21 at age 31.)
  2. Load 1961 and raise summer temperature by exactly 1 °C. Ask the class to predict the new price before you move the slider. It should rise by \(e^{0.607} \approx 1.84\) times. Then add 100 mm of harvest rain and ask for the combined effect.
  3. Switch to "Drop September". Adjusted \(R^2\) rises from 0.79 to 0.80 and the other coefficients barely move. Ask why September looks significant on its own (\(t = 2.47\)) but not next to summer. Hint: the two temperatures correlate at 0.44.
  4. In the out-of-sample panel, slide the training cutoff from 1964 to 1976. Which cutoffs give the worst forecasts, and why do the intervals get wider when the training set is small?
  5. Load 2019 at age 31. The interval runs from about 150 to 1,400. Ask which part of the prediction interval formula makes it so wide, and whether the model should be trusted at 21 °C.
