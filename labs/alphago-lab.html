AlphaGo Lab: Move 37 and Move 78

In March 2016 in Seoul, AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol 4 to 1. Two moves from that match are still discussed. In Game 2, AlphaGo played a move its own policy network gave about 1 chance in 10,000 of a human playing, and it won. In Game 4, Lee played a move AlphaGo had given about the same 1 in 10,000, and AlphaGo lost. This lab runs the search rule AlphaGo used on a small game tree, so you can watch both happen and see where the line between them falls.

After Nick Polson's 41916 Week 8 notes on AlphaGo and Lee Sedol, and Silver et al., Mastering the game of Go with deep neural networks and tree search, Nature 529 (2016). For a network you can play against, see the Chess Lab.

Move 37: search finds what the prior missed

The policy network, trained on human games, gives each move a prior \(P(s,a)\). Move 37 had \(P \approx 10^{-4}\). Once the search did visit it, the value network scored it high, and the search played it.

Move 78: the prior hides what search needed

Lee's wedge also had \(P \approx 10^{-4}\) in AlphaGo's eyes, so its search almost never looked at it. AlphaGo kept reporting about 70% after its mistaken reply at move 79 and only saw the problem at move 87.

Same rule, two outcomes

Both come from one selection rule: \(a = \arg\max_a Q(s,a) + c\,P(s,a)\,\frac{\sqrt{N(s)}}{1 + N(s,a)}\). A move with a tiny prior gets its first look only after a large number of simulations. Whether that number fits in the budget decides which story you get.

Part ITwo moves nobody expected

The boards

Schematic local sketches after Nick's slides, not full game records: the stones around each move are shown, the rest of the board is not.

Game 2, 10 March 2016 · AlphaGo has Black

Move 37. A shoulder hit on the fifth line. The proverb says third-line stones take territory and fourth-line stones build influence; a fifth-line shoulder hit hands the opponent secure territory for a thin wall. Black gave up the side for a stone facing the open centre, where the game was later decided. Lee left the board and took about 15 minutes to reply. Commentator Michael Redmond first took it for a mistake; Fan Hui called it beautiful. Later engines such as KataGo agree with it.

Game 4, 13 March 2016 · Lee has White, down 0 to 3

Move 78. Black had a large framework in the centre, and White had to break it or lose. The wedge at L11 split Black's shape and made several cutting points at once. AlphaGo's reply at 79 was wrong, yet its win estimate stayed near 70% until move 87. Behind on the board, it then played low-percentage tricks, because it maximizes the probability of winning, not the margin. Lee won by resignation after 180 moves.

Part IIThe search, live

Where does the search look?

A two-move game tree stands in for the board. AlphaGo (the root player) picks a move, the opponent replies, and the leaf returns AlphaGo's win probability plus noise, the way the value network would. The opponent picks replies that minimize it. Every simulation walks down by the selection rule above, scores one leaf and backs the value up. AlphaGo plays the root move with the most visits and reports its average value as the win probability.

The Move 37 tree has one root move with a tiny prior that is really the best. In the Move 78 tree, AlphaGo's natural plan has one reply with a tiny prior that refutes it. The red row is the hidden move in both.

Part IIIThe first look comes after about 1/P simulations

Prior against first visit

Run the Move 37 tree with different priors for the hidden move and record the simulation at which it is first visited. The points fall on the line \(N = 1/P\), and the exploration constant does not move them. A prior of \(10^{-4}\) means about 10,000 simulations before the move is looked at once.

The reason: a move nobody has visited is scored at its parent's running value plus \(c\,p\sqrt{N}\). A visited move \(i\) has about the same value plus \(c\,P_i\sqrt{N}/(1+N_i)\). The unvisited move wins the argmax once \(1 + N_i > P_i/p\) for every visited move. Adding these up, \(N \approx \sum_i P_i/p \approx 1/p\). The constant \(c\) multiplies both sides and cancels.

The law needs the visited moves to have similar values and unvisited moves to start at the parent's value. If the visited moves look much worse than the parent, the hidden move is tried sooner; if much better, later. In the Move 78 tree the clock runs inside the opponent's node, so the total is about \(1/p\) divided by the share of simulations that go to plan A.

Part IVGame 4: confidence is not calibration

The win probability that stayed high

Demis Hassabis reported that AlphaGo's estimate was still about 70% at move 79, after the decisive mistake, and that it fell only at move 87. The curve below is an illustrative path through those two reported values; the points in between are not DeepMind data. The Move 78 tree in Part II gives the same picture: with too few simulations it reports about 73% for a plan whose true value is 10%.

Nick's reading through generative Bayesian computation: AlphaGo reported one number, the mean of the win probability. A network that learns the quantiles of the value instead, as an implicit quantile network does (Dabney et al., 2018), could report a 70% mean with a fat left tail, which is a warning. The rationale under Bellman

  1. Go is a Bellman problem with one reward

    A win scores 1 and everything else 0, with no discounting. The optimal value of a position is then the probability of winning under best play, and it satisfies the Bellman equation. The value network is a learned approximation to it.

  2. Search is a sampled Bellman backup

    Each simulation replaces the max and the expectation by one path and one leaf value. Averaged over many paths, \(Q(s,a)\) approaches the backed-up value for the moves the search visits. The prior decides which moves those are.

  3. Move 37: the max overrides the prior

    The prior said \(10^{-4}\), but once the move was visited its \(Q\) was high, and the max took it. Novelty came from maximizing value, not from imitating people.

  4. Move 78: the prior hides the threat

    With \(P \approx 10^{-4}\) the wedge kept \(N(s,a)\) near zero, so AlphaGo's estimate was a max over the replies it had seen, not over all of them. The estimate was confident and wrong for eight moves.

Bellman, reward only at the end (win = 1, \(\gamma = 1\))
\[ V^\star(s) = \max_a \mathbb{E}\bigl[V^\star(s') \mid s, a\bigr] = P(\text{win} \mid s) \]
AlphaGo's leaf value mixes the value net and a fast rollout outcome \(z\)
\[ V(s_L) = (1 - \lambda)\, v_\theta(s_L) + \lambda\, z_L, \quad \lambda = \tfrac12 \]
Selection, steered by the prior
\[ a^\star = \arg\max_a \Bigl[ Q(s,a) + c\, P(s,a) \frac{\sqrt{N(s)}}{1 + N(s,a)} \Bigr] \]
What the search can report
\[ \hat V(s) = \max_{a \in A_{\text{seen}}} Q(s,a) \ne V^\star(s) \]

AlphaGo's networks

Both networks read the board as a stack of 48 feature planes on the 19 × 19 grid (stones, liberties, ko, history) through 13 convolutional layers of 3 × 3 filters. The policy network was first trained on 30 million positions from human games and predicted the expert's move 57% of the time; self-play reinforcement learning then improved it. The value network was trained on 30 million self-play positions, one per game, so it could not memorize whole games. The search used the human-trained policy as its prior because its suggestions were more varied.

What the toy leaves out

The real tree has about 250 moves per turn and 150 turns, and the search expands it as it goes. Our tree has two levels and a handful of moves, and the leaf values are set by hand. Unvisited moves start at the parent's running value, as in Leela Zero and KataGo; the 2016 paper started them at a draw. That choice matters for the 1/P law, so the law is about this rule, not a theorem about AlphaGo.

What happened next

AlphaGo Zero (2017) dropped the human games, learned from self-play alone, and merged the two networks into one with two heads. Professionals adopted the machines' ideas, and fifth-line shoulder hits are no longer taboo. Lee Sedol retired in 2019, saying AI could not be beaten.
