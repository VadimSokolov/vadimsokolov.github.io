PGA Tour Lab: What Is Tiger Woods Worth?

Regress a season of prize money on driving distance, greens in regulation and putting for every PGA Tour player. On the dollar scale Tiger Woods sits nine standard deviations above the line and drags it toward him. Take logs and he becomes an ordinary point: the model predicts more than he earned. Then price a yard, a green and a putt, list the over- and under-performers, and see how the game changed by 2018.

An outlier can be a sign of the wrong scale

The book’s example in four steps. Each button sets the model builder to one step.

  1. On the dollar scale Tiger is nine standard deviations out

    The additive model predicts $3.6 million for Tiger Woods. He won $9.2 million, a standardized residual of 8.8. The residuals are skewed to the right and the model predicts negative earnings for some players, two more signs that a straight line in dollars is the wrong shape.

  2. One player moves the line

    Tiger’s Cook’s distance on the dollar scale is about 2, above the strict cutoff of 1 and nearly a hundred times the usual alarm level of \(4/n\). Leave him out and the coefficient on driving distance falls from $23,100 to $13,100 a yard. A model whose slopes depend on one player is a model of that player.

  3. On the log scale he predicts more than he earned

    Tiger was first in greens in regulation and second in driving distance and in putting. In \(\log(\text{money})\) those advantages add, so in dollars they multiply: the log model predicts $12.0 million, and his residual is −0.5. His Cook’s distance falls to 0.007 and dropping him changes no slope by more than 4%.

  4. Read log coefficients as percent effects

    With \(\hat\beta = 0.166\) on GIR, one more percentage point of greens multiplies earnings by \(e^{0.166} = 1.18\). Ten yards off the tee gives \(e^{0.15} = 1.16\), and 0.01 fewer putts per green gives \(e^{0.214} = 1.24\). The t-ratio of nevents is −0.94, so it can go.

  5. Coefficients are about one season

    Refit to 2018 and a yard is worth about twice as much (0.032 against 0.015), GIR a little less (0.131 against 0.166). Tiger’s 2000 driving distance would have been in the middle of the 2018 field, and his 75.2% GIR is above anyone’s that year, so plugging his 2000 season into the 2018 model is an extrapolation.

The two models
\(\displaystyle \text{money} = \beta_0 + \beta_1\cdot\text{drive} + \beta_2\cdot\text{gir} + \beta_3\cdot\text{putts} + \varepsilon\)
\(\displaystyle \log(\text{money}) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\cdot\text{drive} + \beta_2\cdot\text{gir} + \beta_3\cdot\text{putts} + \varepsilon\)
The log model in dollars: effects multiply
\(\displaystyle \text{money} = e^{\beta_0} \cdot e^{\beta_1\cdot\text{drive}} \cdot e^{\beta_2\cdot\text{gir}} \cdot e^{\beta_3\cdot\text{putts}} \cdot e^{\varepsilon}\)
\(\displaystyle \Delta x \;\Rightarrow\; \text{money} \times e^{\beta\cdot\Delta x}, \quad e^{\beta} - 1 \approx \beta \ \text{ for small } \beta\)
Standardized residual, leverage and Cook’s distance (R: rstandard, hatvalues, cooks.distance)
\(\displaystyle r_i = \frac{e_i}{s\sqrt{1 - h_i}}, \quad h_i = x_i^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} x_i\)
\(\displaystyle D_i = \frac{r_i^2 \cdot h_i}{(1 - h_i) \cdot p}\)
2000, log(money) ~ drivedist + gir + avgputts
\(\displaystyle 36.17 + 0.0146\cdot\text{drive} + 0.166\cdot\text{gir} - 21.37\cdot\text{putts}\)
\(\displaystyle R^2 = 0.669, \quad s = 0.571\)

Takeaway for students. A huge residual is a fact about the model, not only about the player. When the biggest earner is the biggest outlier and the residuals lean one way, try the scale on which skills multiply. On that scale \(e\) raised to a residual reads as “earned this many times his prediction”: Notah Begay III earned 7 times his, the most in 2000.

Steps 1 to 3 of the example.

d00 <- read_csv("data/pga-2000.csv") model00 <- lm(money ~ nevents + drivedist + gir + avgputts, data = d00) model00log <- lm(log(money) ~ nevents + drivedist + gir + avgputts, data = d00) m1 <- lm(log(money) ~ drivedist + gir + avgputts, data = d00) rstandard(m1)[d00$name == "Tiger Woods"]

Median, not mean

\(e\) to the fitted log is the predicted median earnings for a player with those statistics. If the errors are normal, the mean is larger by \(e^{s^2/2}\): with \(s = 0.571\) that is a factor of 1.18. The calculator reports the median.

Season averages hide where strokes are won

GIR and putts per green mix skills: a long approach that misses the green also makes the next putt easier. Broadie (2012) measured each shot against the tour average from the same spot (strokes gained) across eight million shots, and found that shots from beyond 100 yards explain about two-thirds of the spread in scores, against Pelz’s claim that the short game matters most.

Try this in class

  1. Load step 1 and ask students to find Tiger before you point at him. Ask what a predicted value below zero means, and how many players get one.
  2. Tick “Leave Tiger Woods out” on the dollar scale and read the driving-distance coefficient. Do the same on the log scale. Ask which model you would trust to price a yard.
  3. On the log scale, compare the count of players beyond 2 with the 8.9 expected under normality. Then switch back to dollars and count again, above and below the line separately.
  4. In the calculator, load the median golfer, then add 10 yards, then add 1 point of GIR. Predict the result of both together before moving the second slider, and check that the multipliers multiply.
  5. Load “Tiger 2000” with the 2018 model. Ask why the page warns about the prediction, and what the histograms say about how the tour changed.
