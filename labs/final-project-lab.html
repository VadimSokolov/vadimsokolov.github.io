Why the decision comes first

The decision fixes which number matters (Q on the picking day), which error matters (a shortfall and a surplus cost different amounts), and how you will score the model at the end (pounds, not R²).

A forecast is only as useful as the decision it feeds

The source note reconstructs, from public reports, how commercial fruit forecasters work. Their code is proprietary, so every model and number on this page is a reconstruction or a simulation. One grower told reporters the model must say when fruit ripens and how long the picking window stays open, and that it must be bespoke to each farm. Both requirements come back in steps 3 and 7.

The chain on the left is the spine of the whole project. Each later step fills in one box: what you observe (step 2), how the system evolves (step 3), how wrong the forecast is (step 4), what to do about it (step 5), how to learn the rule from simulation (step 6), and how to explain it (step 7).

Two corrections: scale up the sample, divide out the misses

Dividing by the sample fraction fixes the sample size. It does not fix detection. If only 55% of green fruit are seen, the naive count is 45% low, however many plants you sample. That error is bias, and more data of the same kind will not remove it. Sampling more plants shrinks the random error, which falls like 1/√(count).

Real detectors also confuse neighbouring stages (a large white berry labelled pink). The full observation model uses a confusion matrix π: Ys | N ~ Poisson(Σr πsr pr Nr). The detection rates and the confusion matrix are calibrated by hand-counting a few plants.

The heat wave does two things to the harvest

The first days of the wave sit near 28 °C, close to the optimum, so development speeds up. As the wave peaks, daily means pass 31 °C, g(T) falls toward zero, and fruit stall in the white to red stages. Picking collapses on the hottest days. When it cools, the stalled fruit restart together and picking runs above the no-heat run for about two weeks. That flush is the dangerous moment for the grower, because a burst of labour is needed exactly when ripe strawberries cannot wait.

The same weather also shortens the workable day, since picking stops in the hottest hours. Step 5 shows that the second effect changes the booking rule itself.

The simulation follows the source note's appendix: daily mean 18 + 4 sin(2πt/40) + N(0, 1.5²), heat added on days 35–45, 4,000 flowers at day 0 plus new flowers at a Gaussian rate peaking at 600 a day on day 20 (spread 6 days, our choice), 15 g fruit, picked clean daily. Flows use expected values; at these counts binomial noise is small.

The shape of the curve is a diagnostic

Counting error is nearly all in σ₀, and it is small: about 2.3%. Almost all the error comes from weather and development, which pile up week by week. If each week brings an independent shock, variances add, the variance grows like h and the error grows like √h. The FruitCast numbers fit that shape, and the fitted curve reaches 20% at about 4.2 weeks, consistent with a "below 20%" guarantee up to about a month.

The Dutch 10%, 20%, 30% grows in a straight line. Linear growth is what a persistent bias produces: an error that has the same sign every week compounds instead of averaging out. That points to a calibration problem, and calibration problems are what more data from the farm should cure.

Five lessons from the worked example

  1. A normal day books slightly above the mean crop. With H = 8, a picker brings in 96 kg worth £240 against a cost of £100, so α = 0.583 > ½ and z* > 0: 42.3 pickers against a naive 41.7.

  2. Heat reverses the safety margin. With H = 5 a picker brings in only 60 kg, worth £150, α = 0.333 and z* < 0. The grower books below the mean crop (63.5 against 66.7), and more uncertainty means fewer pickers: at r = 0.17 the answer drops to 61.1.

  3. Naive booking costs about £5 on a normal day and £43 to £84 on a hot day. When α is near ½ the profit curve is flat at the top, so small booking errors are cheap. Curvature is Π″(L) = −v(κH)² fQ, and the optimum moves further from the mean as α moves from ½.

  4. Better forecasts are worth real money. Cutting r from 0.17 to 0.10 is worth about £278 per block-day on a normal day and £238 on a hot day. EVPI ≈ v m φ(z*) r grows in proportion to r.

  5. Below break-even hours, book nobody. At H = c/(vκ) = 3.3 h a picker just pays for themself. A disease penalty g per kg of unpicked fruit raises α to 1 − c/((v+g)κH): with g = £1 the hot-day α rises from 0.333 to 0.524 and break-even falls to 2.4 h.

Why learning quantiles is the same as minimising the grower's regret

Write capacity K = κHL and the cost of capacity b = c/(κH) per kg. A short algebra step turns the grower's profit into a constant minus (v + g) times the pinball loss of the booking. Minimising expected pinball loss gives the α-quantile of Q given the data. So fitting a quantile regression is the same job as minimising regret, and the best booking is the predictive α-quantile divided by κH.

One fit gives every quantile at once. A hotter day, a new wage or a disease penalty changes only α, never the model.

Method used here. The paper trains an implicit quantile network (two hidden layers of 128 ReLU units, cosine features for τ) on 400,000 simulated blocks. For speed this lab uses k-nearest-neighbour quantiles: standardise X, find the 200 nearest training blocks with a k-d tree, and take quantiles of their log(Q/Q̂) residuals, added back to this block's plug-in forecast. Both are nonparametric estimates of the same conditional quantiles F⁻¹(τ | X).

The simulator. One week ahead, for picking day 7. True counts in green, small white and large white are lognormal (medians 1.8M, 1.2M, 0.9M, log-sd 0.5). Remaining thermal time Rs ~ Gamma(ks, ψDs/ks) with D = 220, 110, 60 and k = 10, 8, 6, log ψ ~ N(0, 0.05²). Weather forecast Ĝ ~ N(84, 15²) truncated at 45; each realised day adds Ĝ/7 + N(0, 2²). A fruit ripens on day 7 if Rs falls between the day-6 and day-7 totals. Berries weigh 15eε g, ε ~ N(0, 0.1²). The camera reports Ys ~ Poisson(pδsNs), p ~ Beta(40, 10), δ = 0.8, 0.9, 1. The network input is X = (log(1+Y₁), log(1+Y₂), log(1+Y₃), Ĝ). At these counts the binomial and Poisson draws use normal approximations.

Farm-specific calibration

A new farm has few seasons of its own data, so its parameters are pulled toward the pooled value across farms. The weight on the farm's own data is nf/(nf + σ²/τ²), where nf is seasons observed, σ² is season-to-season noise and τ² is how much farms really differ.

1
4.0

Data sharing

The pooled prior needs data from many farms, and yield data is commercially sensitive. One answer is federated sharing: each farm fits locally and shares only parameter summaries, which is all the hierarchical model needs. The first season on a new farm will be the least accurate, and the grower should be told so.

In the step 4 terms, a farm-specific bias shows up as error that grows linearly with the horizon. Pooling plus a few seasons of the farm's own data is what flattens it to √h.

People in the loop

Growers want to stay in charge of the decision. Give them a predictive distribution and let them apply their own costs, rather than a single number.

–

A grower who knows tomorrow is hot, or that the crew is short, can move α without touching the model.

Lead with the action. Give the reason in the decision maker's units (kilograms, pickers, pounds). State the risk as a probability they can act on. Put a price on improvement so they can judge whether better data is worth buying. Keep the model details for the appendix.
