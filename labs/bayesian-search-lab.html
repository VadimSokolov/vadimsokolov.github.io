Logroño de los Caballeros

On the log scale, evidence adds

In 2022 the mining company Aurania Resources announced that it had located Logroño de los Caballeros, a Spanish gold-mining town abandoned in the Ecuadorian jungle for more than 400 years. The team used Bayesian search theory with Larry Stone of Metron, who had earlier helped find the USS Scorpion and Air France 447. Colonial records from the 1580s and 1590s, a 1574 map, stream-sediment gold, magnetic and radiometric surveys, LiDAR and geochemistry each said a little. Together they pointed to the Río Santiago valley, where fieldwork found alluvial gold. This game copies the method on an invented map.

  1. Each clue is a likelihood ratio

    "Half a league from the Río Zamora" says little on its own: it lights up a band on both banks. In log form it is a layer you add to the map. The church bells add a circle around the village. Where the band and the circle cross, both layers are positive and the sum is large.

  2. Weak independent clues make a sharp peak

    A clue that multiplies the odds by 3 adds \(\log 3 \approx 1.1\). Five such clues that agree on one spot add 5.5, a factor of \(e^{5.5} \approx 245\) over cells where they all say no. That only works if the clues are independent. Two chroniclers copying each other count once.

  3. Weight sources by reliability

    Multiplying a layer by a reliability \(r\) between 0 and 1 flattens it toward zero. A 1574 sketch that may be mis-drawn gets a smaller \(r\) than a measured gold grade. Setting \(r = 0\) removes a source entirely.

  4. A failed dig is evidence too

    If the town is in the plot with probability \(p\) and a dig finds it with probability \(q\), a miss leaves \(p(1 - q) / (1 - pq)\) in the plot and divides every other cell by \(1 - pq\). On the log scale, the miss adds \(\log(1 - q)\) inside the plot, and normalising does the rest.

  5. From beliefs to decisions

    The map says where the town probably is. The decision is what to do with a day. Digging pays \(P(\text{plot})\cdot q\) now. A stream sample pays nothing now but can sharpen the map so the next dig is better. Compare both per day spent.

Log posterior over cells c (rk = reliability of source k)
\(\displaystyle \log p(c \mid \text{clues}) = \log p(c) + \sum_k r_k \cdot \log \text{LR}_k(c) - \log Z\)
\(\displaystyle \text{LR}_k(c) = \frac{P(\text{clue } k \mid \text{town at } c)}{P(\text{clue } k \mid \text{baseline})}\)
Stream sample with gold grade g at distance d from the town
\(\displaystyle \log g \sim \text{Normal}\big(\log(5 + 150\cdot e^{-d/2.5}),\ 0.8^2\big) \quad \text{ppb}\)
Dig in a plot with probability p, detection q, nothing found
\(\displaystyle P(\text{miss}) = 1 - p\cdot q\)
\(\displaystyle p_{\text{plot}} \leftarrow p(1 - q) / (1 - pq), \quad p_{\text{other}} \leftarrow p_{\text{other}} / (1 - pq)\)
Advisor: chance of a find on the next dig, per day
\(\displaystyle \text{dig now: } \max P(\text{plot})\cdot q / 2\)
\(\displaystyle \text{scout first: } E[\max P'(\text{plot})\cdot q \mid \text{reading}] / (\text{cost} + 2)\)

Takeaway for students. No single clue found Logroño. Each one shifted the log-odds a little, the shifts added up, and the sum was sharp enough to send a field team to one valley. The same arithmetic, adding log-likelihood ratios and renormalising, runs spam filters, naive Bayes classifiers and search-and-rescue planning.

The real clues

The book lists the corroborating evidence. Juan López de Avendaño wrote in 1588 that Logroño was half a league from the Río Zamora. Two soldiers drowned in 1588 crossing "the river" to fight an uprising. In the mid-1590s seven soldiers drowned trying to reach a garrison downstream. A 1684 Jesuit account tells of an elderly woman who remembered hearing Logroño's church bells from her village at the mouth of the Río Zamora.

What is stylised here

  • The map, rivers, ridges, village and garrison are drawn for the game. They are not the real geography.
  • Every likelihood surface, grade, detection rate and cost is invented.
  • Each game hides the town by drawing from \(e^{\sum w^* \cdot \text{LLR}}\) with hidden weights \(w^*\) near the default reliabilities, then simulates the 1574 map and the stream readings from that town. The clues are informative but noisy, and the reveal shows the hidden weights.
  • The town is a single cell, and the sources are treated as independent.

Reliability is a modelling choice

Raising a likelihood to a power \(r\) is a simple way to discount a source you only half trust. It is a judgment call. If two documents share a source, give them a combined weight of about 1, since counting both at full strength double counts one witness.

Over-trusting (\(r > 1\)) makes the map look sharper than the evidence allows. The advisor then digs confidently in the wrong place.

Try this in class

  1. Press "Stack the clues one by one". After each card switches on, ask students to point to the peak and say how sure they are. Read the top cell's probability from the "Why this cell?" panel as it climbs.
  2. Set the 1574 map's reliability to 0, then to 1.5. How far does the peak move? Should one sketch be able to move it that far? Check at the end of the game whether this map was mis-drawn.
  3. Before a dig, read \(P(\text{plot})\) and \(q\) from the selection box and predict the plot's probability after a miss with \(p(1 - q) / (1 - pq)\). Dig and compare with the status line.
  4. Half the class follows the advisor every turn and half plays by gut feel. Play 10 games each and compare finds and days left on the board.
  5. Find a turn where the advisor prefers a stream sample to digging. Ask why spending a day on information can beat digging now, and when it stops being worth it.
