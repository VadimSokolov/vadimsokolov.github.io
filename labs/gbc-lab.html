GBC Architecture Lab: \(Y = F(X, U)\)

Generative Bayesian computation writes every uncertain quantity as a deterministic function of what you know and of noise you draw yourself: \(Y = F(X, U)\), with \(U\) independent of \(X\). Learn \(F\) from simulated or historical pairs \((X, Y)\), and every fresh draw of \(U\) is a draw of \(Y\) given \(X\). This lab takes the map apart on the galaxy velocities, where \(Y\) is a mixture's parameters and \(X\) a summary of the data, and on a time series, where \(Y\) is tomorrow's value and \(X\) is today's. A third part trains the same kind of map two ways, by the check loss and as a GAN, and asks why one minimization beats a saddle point. A fourth gives GBC and rejection ABC the same simulations and watches ABC widen as the summary grows. A fifth asks how many fund managers can pick stocks, and learns a map for a Dirichlet process posterior.

One equation

The noise outsourcing theorem (Kallenberg, 1997) says such an \(F\) always exists: for any pair \((X, Y)\) there is a measurable \(F\) with \(Y = F(X, U)\) almost surely, \(U \sim U(0,1)\) independent of \(X\). For scalar \(Y\) it is the conditional quantile function, \(F(X, \tau) = Q_{Y\mid X}(\tau)\).

Inference: Part I

\(Y = \theta\), the parameters. \(X = S(y)\), a summary of a data set. Pairs come from the prior and the simulator. Fix \(X\) at the observed data and draw \(U\): each draw is a posterior sample.

Forecasting: Part II

\(Y = y_{t+1}\), the next value. \(X = y_t\), the present. Pairs come from history. Feed a draw back in as the next \(X\) and you have a simulated future path; a thousand paths are a forecast distribution.

Step 2. Train: the architecture of \(F\)

Noise \(U\) goes through a fixed random projection \(R\), is concatenated with the summary \(S\), and three dense ReLU layers map the result to the \(3K\) mixture parameters. Training draws a fresh \(U\) at every step and fits by a loss whose minimizer is the conditional distribution of \(\theta\) given \(S\). Change the widths and watch what each one costs.

θ = F(S, U): layers and sizes

What \(R\) is: one matrix, drawn once

\(R\) has \(d\) rows and \(M\) columns with entries drawn independently from \(N(0, 1/M)\). A linear map of a Gaussian is Gaussian, so \(RU \sim N(0, RR^\top)\) exactly, and \(RR^\top \to I\) as \(M\) grows: after \(R\) the noise is a standard \(d\)-dimensional Gaussian. How wide should \(U\) be? Modes need pieces, not dimensions

Noise outsourcing needs one uniform per parameter: draw \(\theta_1 = F_1(X, U_1)\), then \(\theta_2 = F_2(X, \theta_1, U_2)\), and so on. Fifteen numbers for a five-component mixture, thirty for ten slots. Can one uniform carry several modes? Take the quantile function of the galaxy kernel density, approximate it by a ReLU network with \(H\) units (at most \(H + 1\) linear pieces in one input), and push a single uniform through it. A gap between modes is a steep piece; a mode is a flat one.

Unchecked, the knots sit at equally spaced τ. Checked, they sit at equally spaced velocities, so the steep stretches (the gaps) get their own pieces.

So what does extra width buy? Each input coordinate is another direction in which a ReLU unit can place a hyperplane, so a wider \(U\) cuts the noise space into more affine regions, and separate regions can carry separate modes. But only before the first trainable layer: a frozen \(R\) hands the network a \(d\)-dimensional Gaussian whatever \(M\) was, so \(2^{20}\) is what the theory permits, not what the optimizer sees. On 82 data points, \(U\) at \(2^{10}\) is ample.

Step 3. Evaluate: fix \(X = S(y_{\text{obs}})\) and draw \(U\) again and again

Each draw of \(U\) gives one \(\theta\), so 5,000 draws are 5,000 posterior samples from one forward pass. Two losses were trained on the same \(10^5\) pairs and the same network, and both are checked against a Gibbs sampler under the same prior (exact up to Monte Carlo error).

What the prompt still leaves open

The prior. Dirichlet concentration, the spread of the means and of the scales. The occupied count moves with the Dirichlet concentration more than with anything else.

The labels. Sorting slots by mean before training is what removes label switching; say so, or the network regresses on arbitrary slot numbers.

Which τ the pinball loss uses. With Gaussian \(U\) one natural choice is \(\tau_j = \Phi(U_j)\) for coordinate \(j\). That learns each parameter's marginal and nothing about how they move together (see the pinball tab in Step 3). Ask for an autoregressive chain or a joint loss such as the energy score.

Where to spend the simulations. 10⁵ draws from a broad prior teach the network every kind of data set, and few of them look like yours; the fitted posterior comes out too smooth. Screening a larger batch and training on the draws whose summary is near yours helps without changing the target: here it brought the occupied count in line with Gibbs, though the density stays smoother than the exact posterior.

Part IIForecasting: \(y_{t+1} = F(y_t, U)\), trained live

A series with two regimes

The history is simulated from \(y_{t+1} = 0.6\,y_t + 0.8\tanh(1.5\,y_t) + 0.6\,\varepsilon_t\), \(\varepsilon_t \sim N(0,1)\). It wanders around \(+2\) or \(-2\) and now and then jumps between them. The network does not know this formula. It sees only the pairs \((y_t, y_{t+1})\) and fits \(F(X, \tau)\) by the pinball loss \(\rho_\tau(y - F(X, \tau))\) with a fresh \(\tau \sim U(0,1)\) for every pair. This is the book's quantile network: \(X\) and \(\tau\) (through 16 cosine features) each pass a layer of 32 ReLU units, the two are multiplied element by element, then one more layer of 32 and a linear output.

Then the generative step: start at \(y_T\), draw \(U\), compute \(y_{T+1} = F(y_T, U)\), feed it back, repeat. One-step quantile forecasts are what any quantile regression gives; paths are what a generative map gives, and from a start near zero they split into two regimes, which no single interval can describe.

Part IIIGBC versus GANs: a minimization against a saddle point

Same map, two ways to train it

A generative adversarial network also learns \(Y = F(X, U)\): a generator pushes noise onto the target. It finds \(F\) by playing it against a discriminator, a saddle point problem whose inner maximization is never solved exactly. GBC finds \(F\) by one minimization of the check loss. The network can be identical; the loss, and with it the geometry of optimization, differs. This part follows Polson and Sokolov, Generative Bayesian Computation versus Generative Adversarial Networks: Why a Minimization Beats a Saddle Point (working paper, November 2026).

GAN

\[ \min_G \max_D\ \mathbb{E}\log D(Y) + \mathbb{E}\log\bigl(1 - D(G(X, U))\bigr) \]With the best discriminator this is \(2\,\mathrm{JS}(P, Q_G) - \log 4\), but the generator only ever sees the current, partly trained \(D\).

GBC

\[ \min_F\ \mathbb{E}\,\rho_\tau\bigl(Y - F(X, \tau)\bigr), \quad \tau \sim U(0,1) \]Pointwise convex in the output, minimized by the conditional quantile function, with expected gradient \(F_X(F) - \tau\), zero only at the truth.

The key identity

Integrated over \(\tau\), the check loss is half the CRPS, so the excess risk is half the squared Cramér distance:\[ R(F) - R(F^\star) = \tfrac12\,\mathbb{E}_X\!\int (F_X - G_X)^2\,dz. \]The training loss is itself a distance between the learned and the true distribution.

The Dirac GAN spirals; the check loss descends

The cleanest case (Mescheder et al., 2018): data are a point mass at 0, the generator is a point mass at \(\theta\), the discriminator is \(D(x) = \sigma(\psi x)\). The equilibrium is \((0, 0)\), but the simultaneous gradient field is orthogonal to \((\theta, \psi)\), so each step multiplies the squared distance to equilibrium by \(1 + h^2 f'(\psi\theta)^2 > 1\). For the same target, gradient descent on the check loss moves the quantile toward 0 by a fixed amount per step and stops there.

Right: a normal posterior with the right centre and scale \(c\,s\). Part IVGBC versus ABC: regression instead of rejection

One reference table, two ways to use it

Both methods start from the same \(N\) simulations \((\theta_i, X_i)\), \(\theta_i \sim \pi\), \(X_i = S(y_i)\), \(y_i \sim p(y \mid \theta_i)\). Rejection ABC keeps the few \(\theta_i\) whose \(X_i\) lands near \(X_{\text{obs}}\) and throws the rest away. GBC fits \(\theta = F(X, \tau)\) on all \(N\) pairs and then evaluates it at \(X_{\text{obs}}\). The example is a Gaussian location model with an exact answer, chosen to give ABC its best case: the summary is sufficient. This part follows Polson's slides When GBC Beats ABC (41916 Bayes, AI and Deep Learning, Chicago Booth), and reruns their code.

ABC

Accept \(\theta_i\) when \(\lVert X_i - X_{\text{obs}}\rVert \le \epsilon\). The draws come from\[ p_\epsilon(\theta \mid X_{\text{obs}}) \propto \pi(\theta)\, P\bigl(\lVert X - X_{\text{obs}}\rVert \le \epsilon \mid \theta\bigr), \]wider than the posterior, with bias of order \(\epsilon^2\). Fix the acceptance rate at 1% and \(\epsilon\) must grow with the dimension of \(X\).

GBC

Noise outsourcing gives \(\theta = F(X, \tau)\), \(\tau \sim U(0,1)\), with \(F\) the posterior quantile function, the minimizer of\[ \mathbb{E}\,\rho_\tau\bigl(\theta - F(X, \tau)\bigr). \]A two-layer ReLU network of width 128 is fitted once on all \(10^5\) pairs; a posterior draw is one forward pass at \(X_{\text{obs}}\).

The rate

The acceptance rate of an \(\epsilon\) ball in \(\mathbb{R}^d\) is of order \(\epsilon^d\). Balancing that variance against the \(\epsilon^2\) bias gives ABC a mean squared error of order\[ N^{-4/(d+4)}, \]so each extra summary dimension costs ABC a power of \(N\). GBC's error depends on how smooth \(F\) is in \(X\), not on the volume of a ball.

One data set: the marginal of \(\theta_1\)

The exact posterior is \(\theta_j \mid X \sim N\bigl(\tfrac{20}{21} X_j, \tfrac{1}{21}\bigr)\), independent over \(j\). Run ABC yourself: where the width comes from

This panel builds the reference table in your browser for \(d = 1, \dots, 10\), keeps the nearest 1% for five fresh data sets per dimension, and records \(\epsilon\), the distance of the last accepted draw. If the accepted \(X_i\) filled the ball of radius \(\epsilon\) around \(X_{\text{obs}}\) uniformly, each coordinate would vary by \(\epsilon^2/(d+2)\) and the accepted \(\theta_1\) by \((\tfrac{20}{21})^2\) times that on top of the posterior variance:\[ \frac{\text{sd}_{\text{ABC}}}{\text{sd}} \approx \sqrt{1 + 21\,\bigl(\tfrac{20}{21}\bigr)^2\, \frac{\epsilon^2}{d+2}}. \]In fact the density of \(X\) changes across a ball this wide and the kept draws crowd toward the side nearer the prior mean, so the formula overstates the width at large \(d\); it still explains where the width comes from. The regression adjustment of Beaumont, Zhang and Balding (2002) replaces each accepted \(\theta_i\) by \(\theta_i - \hat\beta^\top (X_i - X_{\text{obs}})\), with \(\hat\beta\) from a least squares fit on the accepted pairs.

Part VWho can pick stocks? A map for a Dirichlet process

Estimate = truth + noise, a thousand times

Of the thousands of actively managed mutual funds, how many managers have real skill? Barras, Scaillet and Wermers (2010) estimate that about three quarters have zero alpha net of fees, about a quarter are unskilled, and a sliver are skilled. The sliver is what an investor wants, and it is what the data speak to least. This part follows Polson's slides Who Can Actually Pick Stocks? (41916 Bayes, AI and Deep Learning, Chicago Booth), after Fisher and Jensen (2022). The slides came without code; the numbers here are from an independent rerun, gbc-funds-lab.py, and from the code in this page.

The hierarchy

\[ \hat\alpha_i \mid \alpha_i \sim N(\alpha_i, s_i^2), \quad \alpha_i \mid G \sim G, \quad G \sim \mathrm{DP}(\theta, G_0). \]\(G\), the distribution of skill across the industry, is the parameter, and it is infinite dimensional. Here \(G_0 = N(0, 3^2)\), \(\theta \sim \text{Gamma}(2, 1)\), \(s_i \sim U(1, 2)\) per cent a year.

The target

One curve, the skill tail of this panel of funds:\[ \pi(c) = \tfrac{1}{n}\#\{i : \alpha_i > c\} \]at \(c = -3, -2, -1, -\tfrac12, 0, \tfrac12, 1, 2, 3\) per cent a year. Nine monotone numbers. A functional of \(G\) and of the allocations, with no closed-form posterior.

The map

\[ Y = F(X, Z), \quad Y = \pi(\cdot), \quad Z \sim N(0, I_6), \]with \(X\) 34 summaries of the panel: quantiles of \(\hat\alpha_i\) and of \(t_i = \hat\alpha_i / s_i\), their mean and spread, the shares with \(|t_i| > 2\). Trained once on \(2 \times 10^5\) panels simulated from the prior by the energy score.

The blur and the winner's curse

The true skill sits on three points; the estimates are those points blurred by standard errors of 1 to 2 per cent a year, the same size as the effects. Counting the funds whose estimate beats a threshold counts the lucky with the good: with a thousand noisy estimates, the top of the ranking fills with zero-alpha funds that had a good draw.

Calibration and cost: why train a map at all

A chain is a per-data-set computation; a map is paid for once. Checking a map does not use convergence diagnostics: draw fresh panels from the prior, where the truth is known, and ask whether the 90% bands contain it 90% of the time. With 200 panels a coverage of 0.90 has a standard error of 0.021.

Generative Bayesian computation in four moves

  1. Write the unknown as a function of noise

    \(Y = F(X, U)\) with \(U\) independent of \(X\) always exists. For scalar \(Y\) it is the conditional quantile function; for a vector, a chain of them. Modes and gaps are steep and flat stretches of \(F\), not extra noise dimensions.

  2. Get pairs, not likelihoods

    For inference the pairs come from the prior and a simulator, \(\theta \sim \pi\), \(y \sim p(y\mid\theta)\), \(X = S(y)\). For forecasting they come from history. Nothing is ever evaluated as a density.

  3. Fit F by a proper loss

    The pinball loss at a random \(\tau\) recovers the conditional quantile function of a scalar. For several outputs at once, use a chain of quantile maps or a joint proper score such as the energy score. A loss that scores each coordinate alone learns the margins and loses the dependence.

  4. Evaluate by forward passes

    Fix \(X\) at what you observed and draw \(U\). Posterior samples, forecast paths and the occupied-components count are all averages over those draws. Training was paid once, so a new data set or a new start value costs one pass, not a new chain.

Why no label switching

In MCMC on a mixture, the posterior is symmetric in the \(K!\) relabellings and chains wander between them. Here the training targets are sorted by mean before the network sees them, so slot 1 is always "the lowest mean". The network never learns a label because no label was ever a target. What it does learn is a posterior over sorted slots, where an empty slot's mean is close to its prior.

Where the pinball loss falls short

Score each of the 30 outputs with its own \(\tau_j = \Phi(U_j)\) and the best the network can do is the marginal quantile of each parameter, drawn independently of the others. The weights then no longer sum to one and a weight need not belong with its mean. The energy score scores the whole vector, so it rewards getting the dependence right. The book's remedy for vectors is the autoregressive chain \(\theta_j = F_j(X, \theta_{<j}, \tau_j)\).

What R really costs

Zero parameters, but at \(M = 2^{20}\) and \(d = 2^{10}\) it is \(2^{30}\) numbers, 4 GiB in single precision, and a 2-gigaflop multiply per draw. Since \(RU\) is exactly Gaussian, the offline run draws \(RU\) directly as \(N(0, I_{1024})\), its limit. A trainable first layer would absorb any full-rank \(RR^\top\) anyway, so only the rank of \(R\) matters.

  4. In the R panel, hold d = 32 and raise M from 2⁴ to 2¹⁶. Predict the off-diagonal spread at each step from \(1/\sqrt M\) before you look.
  5. With equally spaced knots, how many ReLU units does a single uniform need before the group near 9.7 separates from the main body? With knots placed by velocity?
